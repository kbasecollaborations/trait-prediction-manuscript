\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

We compiled four binary growth datasets---ATLeaf (Schäfer et al., 2023), Biolog (see supplemental material), Marine (Gralka et al., 2023), and Populus (see supplemental material)---capturing growth outcomes of phylogenetically and ecologically diverse microbial strains in various carbon sources.
ATLeaf included 224 strains grown on 45 carbon substrates, Biolog encompassed 362 strains and 64 substrates, Marine comprised 175 strains and 100 substrates, and Populus contained 58 strains tested on 191 substrates.
The overall workflow, including data aggregation, genome annotation (RAST, KOFAM, GapMind), and the machine learning pipeline, is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A.
All analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes across all datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B).
We used ATLeaf, Biolog, and Marine datasets for model training and testing, reserving Populus for independent testing.
The phylogenetic distribution of microbes across datasets is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}.

We evaluated model performance using cross-validation, cross-dataset testing, and phylogeny-dependent splits, alongside feature importance analysis to identify mechanistically relevant features.

To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two existing approaches: (1) GapMind, a mechanistic pathway-based tool that predicts growth from metabolic pathway completeness (Price et al., 2020); and (2) a phylogeny-based nearest neighbor classifier (k=3) that predicts phenotypes from closely related strains (see Methods).

GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $\sim$0.65; permissive: $\sim$0.70; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A), indicating that pathway completeness alone does not capture the full complexity of microbial metabolism and regulation.

The phylogeny-based approach, which leverages the well-documented association between metabolic phenotypes and phylogenetic relationships (Langille et al., 2013; A. C. Martiny et al., 2013), performed well within clades (random split: 0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade split: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B).
Out-of-clade performance approached null model levels, demonstrating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient for accurate prediction across diverse lineages.
Notably, Cellobiose and Arginine showed unexpectedly high accuracy ($>$0.90) in certain out-of-clade splits, potentially reflecting highly conserved pathways.

The limited performance of both baseline approaches motivated the development of machine learning models.

\subsection*{Machine learning models show strong intra-dataset results but have generalization issues}

We trained gradient-boosted tree (GBT) classifiers using KOFAM annotations as features ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; see Methods).
KOFAM annotations provide comprehensive coverage of metabolic functions, have been used successfully in similar prediction tasks (Li et al., 2023), and span the entire metabolic repertoire rather than being limited to curated pathways like GapMind.
We selected GBTs over random forests due to their slightly better performance (Table~\ref{tab:best_models_counts} in Supplementary) and evaluated models using balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance.

We aggregated data from all four datasets and assessed model performance using four evaluation scenarios: (1) random cross-validation splits; (2) cross-dataset evaluation (training on three datasets, testing on held-out dataset); (3) in-clade cross-dataset splits (phylogenetically similar samples); and (4) out-of-clade splits (phylogenetically distant samples).

In random cross-validation (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90), significantly outperforming GapMind.
However, cross-dataset performance dropped substantially (0.50--0.75; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalization.
The performance difference between regular cross-dataset and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), suggesting that dataset-specific biases affect model performance as much as phylogenetic relatedness.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with previous observations that random splits overestimate generalization due to fitting phylogeny-specific patterns (J. B. H. Martiny et al., 2015; Li et al., 2023).

The ML performance advantage over GapMind in random splits diminishes under cross-dataset and out-of-clade evaluation, suggesting models learn dataset-specific rather than universal patterns.
Despite the well-characterized nature of carbon catabolic pathways (Gowda et al., 2022), models did not generalize as expected, likely due to phylogenetic diversity and dataset-specific biases.

\subsection*{Why are GapMind and ML models misclassifying?}

GapMind misclassifications can arise from incomplete pathway annotations, alternative metabolic routes not captured in curated rules, or regulatory mechanisms that prevent pathway expression (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A).
We analyzed misclassification patterns per-phenotype and per-dataset to diagnose systematic issues (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}B).
While some phenotypes show better GapMind performance (e.g., Mannitol, Galactose) than others (e.g., Serine, Alanine), there was no clear directional bias in misclassifications (false positives vs.\ false negatives).
Similarly, datasets varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) but without directional bias.
The absence of systematic bias across phenotypes and datasets suggests that experimental conditions do not systematically affect GapMind performance or introduce biases that could confound ML model training.

To understand ML generalization failures, we performed feature importance stability analyses using SHAP (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
Across random splits of the aggregated dataset (top panel), at least one feature was consistently identified as important, indicating models learn common patterns when data distribution is uniform.
However, when comparing stable features between individual datasets and the aggregated dataset (bottom panel; Populus excluded due to small sample size), only Histidine, Maltose, Mannitol, and m-Inositol shared at least one common stable feature across datasets.

We assessed the biological relevance of stable features by examining their KOFAM annotations (Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).
For Histidine, the main catabolic enzyme (K01712, urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets, demonstrating that ML models can learn mechanistic features when they are present and consistently annotated.
However, core histidine pathway enzymes K01745 and K05603, which appeared in ATLeaf's stable features, were not identified in the aggregated dataset---likely due to annotation gaps or alternative pathways in other datasets.
An uncharacterized protein (K09975) appearing as a common feature for ATLeaf and Marine datasets may warrant further investigation.

\subsection*{Can ML learn actual mechanisms? Testing on GapMind-concordant data}

To test whether ML can learn mechanistic features, we focused on samples where GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes (concordant samples), which likely have clearer genotype-phenotype mappings.
We used GapMind features (presence/absence of pathway genes; $\sim$1000 features after correlation filtering) as the feature matrix, ensuring the feature space is directly tied to known metabolic mechanisms.

Models trained on concordant samples achieved near-perfect balanced accuracy ($>$0.95) on held-out concordant test data for random split evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
Cross-dataset splits filtered for concordance also achieved high accuracy ($>$0.90) on many phenotype-dataset combinations, though some phenotypes (e.g., Glucose) showed poor generalization, possibly due to class imbalance.

Cross-dataset stability analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B) showed a notable increase in shared stable features compared to models trained on full datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), indicating concordant training helps models learn consistent mechanistic features.
Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant} confirms that models consistently identify established pathway genes as top features.

When concordant-trained models were applied to discordant samples, they achieved balanced accuracy of 0.18--0.60 (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C), correctly predicting a subset of samples that GapMind misclassified.
This demonstrates that ML captures features beyond GapMind's curated gene sets, complementing mechanistic approaches.

\subsection*{Strategies to improve model performance}

While concordant training provides the best results, it removes $\sim$30\% of samples, which may not be feasible when mechanistic predictors are unavailable.
We explored alternative strategies to improve model performance by addressing data quality issues (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).

We identified systematically problematic samples: microbes showing zero growth on all substrates, growth on all substrates despite incomplete GapMind pathways, and genomes with the highest misclassification rates.
Removing these samples and filtering by measurement confidence (see Methods) improved cross-dataset performance, though not as much as concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B).
Confidence-based filtering removed fewer samples while achieving comparable performance gains.

Concordant-trained models showed the best precision-recall tradeoff (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C), substantially outperforming GapMind predictions alone.
Removing bad genomes and confidence-based filtering provided modest improvements.

We also compared models trained using GapMind features alone versus combined features from GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature combinations improved random split performance but not cross-dataset generalization, consistent with previous findings that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive feature sets for generalization (Li et al., 2023).

\subsection*{Sample size requirements}

Learning curves for concordant-trained models on the Histidine phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:model_sample_size_requirements}) show performance plateaus at approximately 100 samples across all evaluation scenarios (random, cross-dataset, and out-of-clade splits) and test types (full, concordant, and discordant samples).
This establishes that 50--100 high-quality samples per phenotype are sufficient to train robust models for carbon utilization traits.
