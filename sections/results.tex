\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate the factors governing genotype-phenotype prediction accuracy and generalization, we compiled four binary growth datasets---ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog (see supplemental material), Marine \cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus (see supplemental material)---capturing growth outcomes of phylogenetically and ecologically diverse microbial strains in various carbon sources.
ATLeaf included 224 strains grown on 45 carbon substrates, Biolog encompassed 362 strains and 64 substrates, Marine comprised 175 strains and 100 substrates, and Populus contained 58 strains tested on 191 substrates.
The overall workflow, including data aggregation, genome annotation (RAST, KOFAM, GapMind), and the machine learning pipeline, is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A.
All analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes across all datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B).
We used ATLeaf, Biolog, and Marine datasets for model training and testing, reserving Populus for independent testing.
The phylogenetic distribution of microbes across datasets is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}.

We evaluated model performance using cross-validation, cross-dataset testing, and phylogeny-dependent splits, alongside feature importance analysis to identify mechanistically relevant features.

To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two existing approaches: (1) GapMind, a mechanistic pathway-based tool that predicts growth capabilities by assessing the completeness of metabolic pathways required for utilizing specific carbon sources \cite{Price2020-ts}; and (2) a phylogeny-based nearest neighbor classifier (k=3) that predicts phenotypes based on the growth outcomes of the three most closely related strains (see Methods).

GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $\sim$0.65; permissive: $\sim$0.70; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
While GapMind captures core mechanistic aspects of carbon utilization, these results indicate that pathway completeness alone does not account for the full complexity of microbial metabolism and regulation.

The phylogeny-based approach, which leverages the well-documented association between metabolic phenotypes and phylogenetic relationships \cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob}, performed well within clades (random split: 0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade split: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B).
Out-of-clade performance approached null model levels, demonstrating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient for accurate prediction across diverse lineages.
A fundamental limitation of phylogeny-based methods is that they may not capture metabolic capabilities that vary even among closely related strains due to horizontal gene transfer, gene loss, or regulatory differences.
Notably, Cellobiose and Arginine showed unexpectedly high accuracy ($>$0.90) in certain out-of-clade splits, potentially reflecting highly conserved pathways for these substrates that transcend phylogenetic boundaries.

The limited performance of both baseline approaches---GapMind's incomplete mechanistic coverage and phylogeny's failure to generalize across clades---motivated the development of machine learning models that could integrate genomic features more comprehensively.

\subsection*{Machine learning models show strong intra-dataset results but have generalization issues}

Machine learning models achieved high accuracy on intra-dataset splits but showed performance degradation across datasets and phylogenetic contexts, suggesting they learn dataset-specific rather than universal patterns.

We trained gradient-boosted tree (GBT) classifiers using KOFAM annotations as features ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; see Methods).
We selected KOFAM annotations as our primary feature set for several reasons: they provide comprehensive coverage of metabolic functions directly relevant to carbon utilization, they have been used successfully in similar phenotype prediction tasks \cite{Li2023-up}, and unlike GapMind's curated rule sets, they span the entire metabolic repertoire without being constrained to specific pathways.
This broader feature space allows models to potentially capture relationships beyond known pathway structures.
We evaluated both gradient-boosted trees and random forests, selecting GBTs for our primary analyses due to their slightly better performance across splits and phenotypes (Table~\ref{tab:best_models_counts} in Supplementary).
All models were evaluated using balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance across phenotypes.

We aggregated data from all four datasets and assessed model performance using four evaluation scenarios designed to progressively test generalization:
(1) random cross-validation splits across the aggregated dataset, where training and test samples are drawn from the same distribution;
(2) cross-dataset evaluation, training on three datasets and testing on the held-out dataset to assess generalization across experimental contexts;
(3) in-clade cross-dataset splits, restricting cross-dataset evaluation to phylogenetically similar samples to isolate dataset effects from phylogenetic effects; and
(4) out-of-clade splits, where training and test samples are phylogenetically distant.
For random splits, we verified that test samples were drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In random cross-validation (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90), significantly outperforming the GapMind baseline.
However, cross-dataset performance dropped substantially (0.50--0.75; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalization across experimental contexts.
The performance difference between regular cross-dataset and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), indicating that dataset-specific and phylogenetic effects on generalization could not be clearly distinguished in this analysis.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with previous observations that random splits overestimate generalization due to fitting phylogeny-specific patterns \cite{Martiny2015-ie,Li2023-up}.

Because most carbon source utilization pathways are catalyzed by evolutionarily conserved, well-characterized catabolic enzymes \cite{Gowda2022-cf}, we initially hypothesized that models trained on one dataset would generalize to others.
The failure to generalize suggests that models learn dataset-specific or phylogeny-correlated features rather than the underlying mechanistic relationships.
We note, however, that even modest improvements by ML models in cross-dataset scenarios could be valuable for practical applications, particularly when models can be trained on dataset- or phylogeny-specific data where they outperform existing mechanistic approaches.

\subsection*{Why are GapMind and ML models misclassifying?}

Understanding the sources of prediction errors is essential for developing improvement strategies.
GapMind misclassifications reveal annotation gaps and regulatory complexity, while ML generalization failures stem from learning dataset- or phylogeny-correlated features rather than mechanistically relevant ones.

GapMind misclassifications can arise from multiple factors: incomplete pathway annotations, alternative metabolic routes not captured in curated rules, and regulatory mechanisms that prevent pathway expression under certain conditions (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A).
To diagnose whether these issues are systematic, we analyzed misclassification patterns both per-phenotype and per-dataset (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}B).
While some phenotypes show better GapMind performance (e.g., Mannitol, Galactose) than others (e.g., Serine, Alanine), there was no clear directional bias in misclassifications (false positives vs.\ false negatives).
Similarly, datasets varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) but without directional bias.
The absence of systematic bias suggests that both missing annotations/alternative pathways and regulatory/measurement issues contribute to errors, and that experimental conditions across datasets do not systematically affect GapMind performance in ways that could confound ML model training.

To understand ML generalization failures, we performed feature importance stability analyses using SHAP (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
Across random splits of the aggregated dataset (top panel), at least one feature was consistently identified as important, indicating models learn common patterns when data distribution is uniform.
However, when comparing stable features between individual datasets and the aggregated dataset (bottom panel; Populus excluded due to small sample size), only Histidine, Maltose, Mannitol, and m-Inositol shared at least one common stable feature across datasets.

We assessed the biological relevance of stable features by examining their KOFAM annotations (Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).
For Histidine, the main catabolic enzyme (K01712, urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets, demonstrating that ML models can learn mechanistic features when such features are present, consistently annotated, and serve as clear differentiators of the phenotype.
However, core histidine pathway enzymes K01745 (histidine ammonia-lyase) and K05603 (formimidoyl-glutamate deiminase), which appeared in ATLeaf's stable features, were not identified in the aggregated dataset---likely due to annotation gaps or alternative pathways in other datasets.
An uncharacterized protein (K09975) appearing as a common feature for ATLeaf and Marine datasets may warrant further investigation.

These results indicate that ML models learn few mechanistic features when trained on full datasets containing mixed-quality samples.
The limited feature overlap across datasets suggests that discordant samples---where genotype-phenotype relationships are obscured by annotation gaps, regulatory effects, or measurement error---may be forcing models toward spurious correlations.
This raises a fundamental question: can ML learn actual mechanistic features when trained on samples where genotype-phenotype mappings are clearer?

\subsection*{Can ML learn actual mechanisms? Testing on GapMind-concordant data}

When trained on samples with clear genotype-phenotype mappings (GapMind-concordant data), ML achieves near-perfect accuracy and learns mechanistic features.
Importantly, ML also correctly predicts a subset of samples that GapMind misclassifies, demonstrating that it captures relationships beyond curated pathway rules.

To test this, we focused on samples where GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes (concordant samples).
These samples likely have clearer mechanistic underpinnings, as GapMind's pathway-based predictions align with observed growth phenotypes.
We note that this approach may preferentially select samples where the canonical pathway is the actual mechanism, potentially excluding organisms using alternative metabolic routes; we return to this caveat in the Discussion.
We used GapMind features (presence/absence of pathway genes; $\sim$1000 features across all GapMind carbon utilization phenotypes after correlation filtering) as the feature matrix, ensuring the feature space is directly tied to known metabolic mechanisms.

Models trained on concordant samples achieved near-perfect balanced accuracy ($>$0.95) on held-out concordant test data for random split evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
Cross-dataset splits filtered for concordance also achieved high accuracy ($>$0.90) on many phenotype-dataset combinations, indicating that when mechanisms are clear and features are mechanistically relevant, ML can learn and generalize well.
However, even with concordant data, generalization failed for some phenotypes (e.g., Glucose, Sucrose) despite common stable features across datasets, possibly due to imbalanced class distributions.
\todo{Investigate class imbalance effects on Glucose/Sucrose generalization}

Cross-dataset stability analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B) showed a notable increase in shared stable features compared to models trained on full datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), indicating concordant training helps models learn consistent mechanistic features.
Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant} confirms that models consistently identify established pathway genes as top features, demonstrating that ML can learn actual mechanisms when samples have clear genotype-phenotype mappings.

When concordant-trained models were applied to discordant samples (where GapMind predictions contradict experimental outcomes), they achieved balanced accuracy of 0.18--0.60 (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C).
Performance varied substantially across phenotypes, with some showing above-random accuracy while others did not.
For phenotypes where concordant-trained models performed well on discordant samples, this suggests ML may capture features beyond GapMind's curated gene sets, potentially identifying alternative pathway genes or regulatory signatures.
However, the wide performance range indicates that not all discordant samples are equally recoverable, likely reflecting heterogeneous causes of discordance.

\subsection*{Strategies to improve model performance}

Multiple strategies can improve model performance: removing systematically problematic samples, filtering by confidence estimates, and prioritizing precision or recall for specific applications.

While concordant training provides the best results, it removes $\sim$30\% of samples, which may not be feasible when mechanistic predictors like GapMind are unavailable.
We therefore explored alternative strategies to improve model performance by addressing data quality issues without requiring external mechanistic predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).

We identified systematically problematic samples: microbes showing zero growth on all substrates (potentially dead cultures or contaminated samples), growth on all substrates despite incomplete GapMind pathways (potentially mislabeled or unusual strains), and genomes with the highest misclassification rates across phenotypes.
\todo{Develop genome quality metrics independent of GapMind for broader applicability}
Removing these samples and filtering by measurement confidence (see Methods) improved cross-dataset performance, though not as much as concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B).
Importantly, confidence-based filtering removed fewer samples while achieving comparable performance gains, making it a practical alternative when concordant filtering is not possible.

Concordant-trained models showed the best precision-recall tradeoff (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C), substantially outperforming GapMind predictions alone.
Removing bad genomes and confidence-based filtering provided modest improvements.
Depending on the application, prioritizing precision (minimizing false positives) or recall (minimizing false negatives) may be more informative than balanced accuracy.

We also compared models trained using GapMind features alone versus combined features from GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature combinations improved random split performance but not cross-dataset generalization, consistent with previous findings that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive feature sets for generalization \cite{Li2023-up}.
This suggests that for practical applications where generalization is the primary goal, focusing on biologically relevant features may be more beneficial than maximizing the feature space.

\subsection*{Sample size requirements}

A practical question for experimental design is how many samples are needed to train effective models.
Learning curves for concordant-trained models on the Histidine phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:model_sample_size_requirements}) illustrate how model performance varies with training sample size under different evaluation scenarios: random cross-validation splits, cross-dataset splits, and out-of-clade phylogenetic splits.
Each panel further breaks down performance on full test samples, concordant test samples, and discordant test samples.

For Histidine, performance plateaus at approximately 100 samples across all evaluation scenarios and test types, suggesting that 50--100 high-quality samples per phenotype may be sufficient to train robust models for carbon utilization traits with clear mechanistic underpinnings.
While this finding requires validation across additional phenotypes, the relatively modest sample requirement suggests that strategic data collection focusing on quality over quantity may be more efficient than brute-force data aggregation for building generalizable models.
