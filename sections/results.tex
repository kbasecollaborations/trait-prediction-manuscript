\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Core Story Arc}

\textbf{Goal:} Build carbon utilization prediction models to learn novel mechanistic features. When generalization issues arise or models don't use mechanistic features, diagnose why and develop improvement strategies.

\textbf{Narrative flow:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Baseline approaches (GapMind, phylogeny) show limited accuracy, motivating ML approaches (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions})
\item ML models perform well on intra-dataset/random splits but fail to generalize across datasets and phylogenetic contexts (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance})
\item Why are predictions failing? GapMind misclassifications reveal annotation gaps and regulatory issues; ML generalization failures stem from learning non-mechanistic features (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis})
\item Can ML learn actual mechanisms? Yes—when trained on GapMind-concordant data where mechanisms are clear, ML achieves high accuracy and learns mechanistic features. ML also predicts some discordant samples better than GapMind (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis})
\item Strategies to improve models: removing bad data, adjusting probability thresholds for precision, using confidence estimates, curriculum learning (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies})
\item Additional improvements: learning curves show sample requirements, feature representation combinations provide modest gains, dataset aggregation helps (Figure~\ref{fig:model_optimization})
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

\textbf{Key finding:} Predicting microbial carbon utilization should be straightforward, but existing approaches  (mechanism-based or phylogeny-based) show fundamental limitations.

We compiled four binary growth datasets---ATLeaf (Schäfer et al., 2023), Biolog (see supplemental material), Marine (Gralka et al., 2023), and Populus (see supplemental material)---each capturing binary growth outcomes of microbes in diverse carbon sources.
These datasets were selected based on their comparable experimental goals (measurement of carbon-utilization traits) and their coverage of phylogenetically and ecologically diverse microbial strains.
Specifically, ATLeaf included 224 strains grown on 45 carbon substrates, Biolog encompassed 362 strains and 64 substrates, Marine comprised 175 strains and 100 substrates, and Populus contained 58 strains tested on 191 substrates.
The resulting phenotype data provided a comprehensive foundation for training and benchmarking machine learning classifiers to predict carbon utilization phenotypes across diverse microbes and carbon sources while accounting for dataset-specific characteristics.
The overall workflow, including data aggregation, genome annotation using multiple sources (RAST, KOFAM, GapMind), and the machine learning pipeline, is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A (see Methods).
Our analyses focused on understanding factors influencing predictive performance and strategies for model improvement and interpretability, therefore for all the analyses in the our study we only use the 15 shared phenotypes across all the original datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B).
Subsequent analyses primarily employed the ATLeaf, Biolog, and Marine datasets for model training and testing, while the Populus dataset was reserved exclusively for independent testing.
Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution} describes the phylogenetic distribution of the microbes from the four datasets used in this study.

In order to establish baseline performance for carbon utilization prediction, we evaluated two existing approaches: (1) GapMind, a mechanistic pathway-based prediction tool that infers growth capabilities based on the presence of known metabolic pathways in the genome (Price et al., 2020); and (2) a phylogeny-based method that predicts phenotypes based on the growth outcomes of phylogenetically related strains (see Methods).

GapMind predicts growth capabilities by assessing the completeness of metabolic pathways required for utilizing specific carbon sources.
We applied GapMind to the genomes in our datasets and compared its predictions to the experimental growth outcomes.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A, GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes, with performance varying depending on the completeness threshold used (strict: $\sim$0.65 vs.\  permissive: $\sim$0.70).
This indicates that while GapMind captures some mechanistic aspects of carbon utilization, it fails to account for the full complexity of microbial metabolism and regulation.
Contrarily, the phylogeny-based nearest neighbor approach leverages the evolutionary relationships among microbial strains to predict phenotypes.
These methods have been widely used in microbial ecology and genomics, based on the principle that closely related organisms often share similar traits[refs].
By examining the growth outcomes of closely related strains, the model infers the likely capabilities of a given strain.
In this case, we use a nearest neighbor classifier with k=3, where the phenotype of a strain is predicted based on the majority phenotype of its three closest phylogenetic neighbors.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B, phylogeny-based predictions performed well within phylogenetic clades (random split: 0.75--0.85) but struggled when tested on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade split: 0.55--0.65).
The results when testing on out-of-clade samples approached the performance of null models (Identity and Bernoulli), indicating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient for accurate phenotype prediction across diverse microbial lineages.
Surprisingly, for Cellobiose and Arginine, for certain out-of-clade splits, the nearest neighbor approach performed very accurately (balanced accuracy $>$0.90).

\subsection*{Machine learning models demonstratre strong intra-dataset results but have generalization issues}

\textbf{Key finding:} ML models achieve high accuracy on intra-dataset and random splits but show performance degradation across datasets and phylogenetic contexts.

Initially, we used the KOFAM annotations as features to train binary classifiers (gradient-boosted trees) for predicting carbon utilization phenotypes.
We choose the KOFAM annotations as our primary feature set due to their comprehensive coverage of metabolic functions and pathways, which are directly relevant to carbon utilization.
Plus, KOFAM annotations were previously used successfully in similar phenotype prediction tasks (e.g., Li et al., 2021).
Additionally, it is more fine-grained compared to other annotation sources like RAST, allowing the model to capture subtle functional differences that may influence growth phenotypes.
And unlike the GapMind rule set, KOFAM annotations are not explicitly curated for specific pathways and span the entire metabolic repertoire of the organisms, providing a richer feature space for machine learning.
We evaluated model performance using balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance across phenotypes.
We evaluated two types of machine learning algorithms: gradient-boosted trees (GBT) and random forests (RF).
We limited ourselves to these since previous papers (Li et al., 2021; Agrawal et al., 2022) have shown that Random Forests performed well for trait prediction tasks, and GBTs are known to often outperform RFs in various classification tasks due to their ability to model complex relationships through boosting.
We finally selected GBTs for our main analyses due to their slightly better performance across different splits and phenotypes (see Table~\ref{tab:best_models_counts} in Supplementary).

For this analysis, we aggregated the data from all four datasets and used four evaluation scenarios to assess model performance and generalization:
(1) Random cross-validation splits across the entire aggregated dataset;
(2) Cross-dataset evaluation (training on three datasets and testing on the held-out dataset);
(3) In-clade cross-dataset splits (where the cross-dataset evaluation is restricted to phylogenetically similar samples); and
(4) Out-of-clade splits (out-of-clade splits where training and test samples are phylogenetically distant).

In the random cross-validation scenario (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) across phenotypes, significantly outperforming the GapMind baseline.
However, when evaluated in the cross-dataset scenario (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), performance dropped substantially (0.50--0.75), indicating limited generalization across datasets.
To further dissect the role of phylogeny in model performance, we conducted in-clade cross-dataset evaluations, where training and test samples were phylogenetically similar (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C).
Surprisingly, the performance difference between regular cross-dataset and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal (average difference $\sim$0.02), suggesting that dataset-specific biases dominated model performance as much as phylogenetic relatedness.
For the random splits, we can verify that the test samples were drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as the training samples (see Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

Performance on phylogeny-dependent out-of-clade splits are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance} and reflect similar trends as cross-dataset splits, with balanced accuracy ranging from 0.50--0.80.
Which indicates that the models also struggle to generalize across phylogenetically distant samples.
This is consistent with previous observations on dataset diversity (Albright et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023), where random splits often overestimate generalization performance due to fitting phylogeny-specific patterns.

Overall, we observe that the performance advantage of machine learning models had over GapMind in random split scenarios, diminishes under cross-dataset and out-of-clade evaluation, suggesting models learn dataset-specific rather than universal patterns.
However, we also note that since GapMind itself has modest performance on these datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A), even modest improvements by ML models in cross-dataset and out-of-clade scenarios could still be valuable for practical applications.
Especially since if machine learning models can be trained on dataset or phylogeny specific data, they can provide more accurate predictions than existing mechanistic approaches.

In the subsequent sectoins, we investiage why GapMind misclassifies certain samples and why ML models struggle to generalize, and explore strategies to improve model performance.

\subsection*{Why Are GapMind and ML Models Misclassifying?}

\textbf{Key finding:} GapMind misclassifications reveal annotation gaps and regulatory complexity; ML generalization failures stem from learning phylogenetically correlated rather than mechanistically relevant features. This raises the question: can ML learn actual mechanisms when trained appropriately?

Missclassification by GapMind can arise from multiple factors, including incomplete pathway annotations, alternative metabolic routes not captured in the curated rules, and regulatory mechanisms that prevent pathway expression under certain conditions, we summarize these potential causes in Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A.
To diagnose whether these issues are systematic, we analyzed misclassification patterns both per-genome and per-phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}B).
Per-phenotype analysis revealed that though there are some phenotypes for which GapMind performs better (e.g., Mannitol, Galactose) and some for which it performs worse (e.g., Serine, Alanine), there is no clear directional bias in the misclassifications (false positives vs.\ false negatives).
This suggests that both missing annotations/alternative pathways and regulatory/measurement issues could contribute to errors.
The same pattern holds true when analyzing misclassifications per-dataset, where datasets like Biolog show higher overall discordance ($\sim$30\%) compared to Populus ($\sim$26\%), but again without a clear directional bias.

To understand why ML models struggle to generalize across datasets and phylogenetic contexts, we performed feature importance stability analyses (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
First, we assessed feature stability across random splits of the aggregated dataset (top panel).
Here, we found that at least 1 feature was consistently identified as important across different random splits, indicating that models were learning common patterns when the data distribution was uniform across datasets and phylogenetic groups.
Next, we evaluated feature stability across the cross-dataset splits used in Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B.
For this analysis, we compared the stable features identified in models trained on each individual dataset to those from the aggregated dataset minus that dataset Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C (bottom panel).
Note that we did not use the Populus dataset for this analysis since it was reserved for independent testing and did not contain enough samples for training.
In this case, we observed a notable drop in the number of stable features shared between individual datasets and the aggregated dataset, with only Histidine, Maltose, Mannitol and m-Inositol showing at least one common stable feature.

\subsection*{Can ML Learn Actual Mechanisms? Testing on GapMind-Concordant Data}

\textbf{Key finding:} When trained on GapMind-concordant samples (where mechanisms are clearly mappable to genes), ML achieves near-perfect accuracy on concordant test samples and learns mechanistic features. Importantly, ML also predicts a subset of discordant samples accurately, performing better than GapMind alone.

The analyses in the previous sections indicate that ML models struggle to generalize across datasets and phylogenetic contexts, likely because they learn non-mechanistic features that are dataset-specific.
This raises the question: can ML learn actual mechanistic features when trained on samples where genotype-phenotype mappings are clear?
To test this, we focused on samples where GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes (concordant samples).
These samples likely have clearer mechanistic underpinnings, as GapMind's pathway-based predictions align with observed growth phenotypes.
Additionally, we used GapMind features (presence/absence of pathway genes) as the feature matrix for the ML models, ensuring that the feature space is directly tied to known metabolic mechanisms.
We retrained ML models using only these concordant samples and evaluated their performance on held-out concordant test samples Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A (top panel).
Our results show that models trained on concordant samples achieve near-perfect balanced accuracy ($>$0.95) on held-out concordant test data across all phenotypes for the random split evaluation.
Additionally, when trained on cross-dataset splits filtered for concordance, these models also achieve high balanced accuracy ($>$0.90) on many phenotype-dataset combinations.
Indicating that when mechanisms are clear and features are mechanistically relevant, ML can learn and generalize well.
However, even if we use concordant data, generalization fails for some phenotypes (eg. Glucose) even though there are common stable features across datasets.
This could be due to the imbalanced class distribution in the data for these phenotypes.

Cross-dataset stability analysis performed on models trained on GapMind-concordant samples using the dataset splits used in Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B.
Compared to Figure 4C (bottom panel), there is a notable increase in the number of stable features shared between individual datasets and the aggregated dataset, indicating that concordant training helps models learn consistent mechanistic features.
Furthermore, Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant} summarizes the biological relevance of the top stable features identified in concordant-trained models for representative phenotypes.
Models consistently identify established pathway genes as top features, demonstrating that ML can learn actual mechanisms when samples have clear genotype-phenotype mappings.

When concordant-trained models are applied to discordant samples, they achieve balanced accuracy of 0.18--0.60.
This means ML correctly predicts a subset of samples that GapMind gets wrong, suggesting ML captures features beyond GapMind's gene set.
Thereby, ML complements mechanistic tools by learning additional patterns from the data.

From Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}, we know that many samples are predicted incorrectly by GapMind due to annotation gaps or regulatory issues.
In the subsequent section, we explore most likely causes of these errors and strategies to improve model performance.

\subsection*{Summary and Practical Recommendations}

\textbf{Key insights from this work:}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{ML can learn mechanistic features}, but only when trained on samples with clear genotype-phenotype mappings (GapMind-concordant data). Full datasets contain noise that leads to spurious correlation learning. Therefore, it is important to identify and prioritize high-quality samples for training rather than simply maximizing quantity through data aggregation.

\item \textbf{ML outperforms GapMind} by correctly predicting a subset of discordant samples, capturing features beyond GapMind's curated gene sets.

\item \textbf{Multiple improvement strategies exist:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Most impactful: Training on concordant data (primary strategy from Figure 5)
\item Modest gains: Removing systematically bad genomes/datasets, confidence-based filtering  (Figure 6)
\item Secondary optimizations: Feature combinations (Figure 6)
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Sample size requirements are manageable:} 50 concordant samples per phenotype achieve high performance, though out-of-clade generalization requires 60--80 samples.

\item \textbf{Rigorous evaluation is critical:} Cross-dataset and out-of-clade splits provide realistic performance estimates; random within-dataset CV overestimates generalization by 0.15--0.25.

\item \textbf{Deployment considerations:} Probability threshold tuning enables precision-focused predictions for experimental follow-up
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Practical workflow for building generalizable models:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Evaluate using cross-dataset/out-of-clade splits to avoid overly optimistic estimates
\item When mechanistic predictions (e.g., GapMind) are available, train on concordant samples
\item Without mechanistic knowledge, use feature stability diagnostics (high number of cross-dataset common stable features indicates mechanistic learning) and per-genome quality assessments
\item Collect 40--80 high-quality samples per phenotype from phylogenetically diverse strains from the appropriate ecosystem of interest; prioritize quality over quantity
\item Apply improvement strategies (Figures 5,6) for incremental gains
\item Tune deployment thresholds based on application requirements
\end{enumerate}
