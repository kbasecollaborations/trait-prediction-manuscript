\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate the factors governing genotype-phenotype prediction accuracy and generalisation, we compiled four binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{Bochner2001PM,Bochner2009PM}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{Timm2015Populus}) capturing growth outcomes of phylogenetically and ecologically diverse microbial strains in various carbon sources (819 input genomes, 760 retained after filtering; see Methods for data sources, preprocessing, and quality control procedures).
The overall workflow, including data aggregation, genome annotation (RAST, KOFAM, GapMind), and the machine learning pipeline, is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A.
All analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes across all datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B).
All four datasets were used in the aggregated analyses and leave-one-dataset-out evaluations; Populus was excluded only from selected feature-stability comparisons because of its small sample size.
The phylogenetic distribution of microbes across datasets is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}.


To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two existing approaches: (1) GapMind, a mechanistic pathway-based tool that predicts growth capabilities by assessing the completeness of metabolic pathways required for utilising specific carbon sources~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and (2) a phylogeny-based nearest neighbour classifier (k=3) that predicts phenotypes based on the growth outcomes of the three most closely related strains (see Methods).

GapMind achieved a modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $\sim$0.65; permissive: $\sim$0.70; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).

The phylogeny-based approach, which leverages the well-documented association between metabolic phenotypes and phylogenetic relationships \cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob}, performed well within clades (random split: 0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade split: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B).
Out-of-clade performance approached null model levels, demonstrating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient for accurate prediction across diverse lineages~\cite{Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.
Notably, cellobiose and glycerol showed unexpectedly high accuracy ($>$0.80) in certain out-of-clade splits, potentially reflecting highly conserved pathways for these substrates.
The limited performance of both baseline approaches motivated us to test whether machine learning models integrating comprehensive genomic features could better capture the complexity of genotype-phenotype relationships and generalise across experimental contexts.

\subsection*{Machine learning models show strong intra-dataset results but have generalisation issues}

We trained gradient-boosted tree (GBT) classifiers using KOFAM annotations as features ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; see Methods).
All models were evaluated using balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance across phenotypes.

We aggregated data from all four datasets and assessed model performance using four evaluation scenarios designed to progressively test generalisation:
(1) random cross-validation splits across the aggregated dataset, where training and test samples are drawn from the same distribution;
(2) cross-dataset evaluation, training on three datasets and testing on the held-out dataset to assess generalisation across experimental contexts;
(3) in-clade cross-dataset splits, restricting cross-dataset evaluation to phylogenetically similar samples to isolate dataset effects from phylogenetic effects; and
(4) out-of-clade splits, where training and test samples are phylogenetically distant.
For random splits, we verified that test samples were drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In random cross-validation (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and significantly outperformed the GapMind baseline.
However, the cross-dataset performance decreased substantially (0.50--0.75; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts.
The performance difference between regular cross-dataset and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C).
The minimal difference between unrestricted and in-clade cross-dataset performance indicates that poor generalisation is not explained by phylogenetic distance alone, suggesting that dataset-level inconsistencies in genotype--phenotype relationships contribute substantially to model failure.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with previous observations that random splits overestimate generalisation due to fitting phylogeny-specific patterns \cite{Martiny2015-ie,Li2023-up}.

Despite the expectation that well-characterised catabolic pathways would enable cross-dataset transfer \cite{Gowda2022-cf,Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis}, these results indicate that models trained on aggregated data do not consistently learn transferable mechanistic features.

\subsection*{Why are GapMind and ML models misclassifying?}

To understand these failures, we analysed why both mechanistic and ML models misclassified some samples.
We analysed GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B).
GapMind misclassifications can arise from incomplete pathway annotations, alternative metabolic routes, and regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances}.
While some phenotypes showed higher GapMind accuracy (e.g., mannitol, galactose) than others (e.g., serine, alanine), we observed no consistent directional bias in misclassifications (false positives versus false negatives).
Similarly, datasets varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) without directional bias.
This lack of directional bias suggests that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact.

To understand ML generalisation failures, we performed feature importance stability analyses using SHAP (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
Across random splits of the aggregated dataset (top panel), at least one feature was consistently identified as important, showing that the models recovered recurring predictors when training and test samples came from the same distribution.
However, when comparing stable features between individual and aggregated datasets (bottom panel; Populus excluded due to small sample size), only histidine, maltose, mannitol, and m-inositol \cite{Weber2022-ce} shared at least one common stable feature across datasets.


We assessed the biological relevance of stable features by examining their KOFAM annotations (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).
For histidine, the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets. This indicates that the models can recover pathway-linked features when those genes are present, consistently annotated, and clearly associated with the phenotype.
However, core histidine pathway enzymes K01745 (histidine ammonia-lyase) and K05603 (formimidoyl-glutamate deiminase), which appeared in ATLeaf's stable features, were not identified in the aggregated dataset, likely due to annotation gaps or alternative pathways in other datasets.
An uncharacterised protein (K09975) appearing as a common feature for ATLeaf and Marine datasets may warrant further investigation.

These analyses suggest that the aggregated training set mixes samples that are easy to predict mechanistically with samples that are not.
Therefore, we hypothesised that restricting training to samples with internally consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would allow machine learning models to recover transferable mechanistic features and to generalise across datasets and phylogenetic contexts.
To test this hypothesis, we leveraged GapMind concordance as a criterion for stratifying samples by mechanistic consistency and trained models exclusively on samples in which pathway-based predictions matched experimental outcomes.
\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Can ML learn mechanistically relevant features? Testing on GapMind-concordant data}

We defined concordant samples as those in which GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes. These samples likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways that are already captured in annotation databases.
Concordance was used solely to stratify samples by mechanistic consistency; the model architecture, training procedure, and evaluation strategy were otherwise unchanged.
To verify that the gains were not artefacts of aligning the feature space with the concordance criterion, we repeated the cross-dataset evaluation using KOFAM annotations as an independent feature space while retaining GapMind concordance for sample selection only; the improvement persisted when evaluated on concordant held-out samples (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant}).
Concordance-based filtering preferentially selects samples in which the canonical pathway is the dominant mechanism underlying the observed phenotype; organisms relying on alternative pathways or regulatory contexts not captured by genome-derived features may be underrepresented.
Accordingly, improved transfer in this setting should be interpreted as better learning on a more mechanistically homogeneous subset, not as recovery of all biologically valid utilisation strategies.
We used GapMind features (presence/absence of pathway genes; $\sim$1000 features across all GapMind carbon utilisation phenotypes after correlation filtering) as the feature matrix so that the feature space remained tied to known metabolic mechanisms.

Models trained on concordant samples achieved near-perfect balanced accuracy ($>$0.95) on held-out concordant test data for random split evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
Cross-dataset splits filtered for concordance also achieved high accuracy ($>$0.90) on many phenotype-dataset combinations, showing that concordance filtering improved transfer when the retained samples were supported by pathway-linked features.
However, even with concordant data, generalisation failed for some phenotypes (e.g., glucose and serine), despite common stable features across datasets, possibly due to imbalanced class distributions.
Because the learning algorithm and evaluation scheme were unchanged, these gains point to the training data as the main difference.

Cross-dataset stability analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B) showed a notable increase in shared stable features compared to models trained on full, unfiltered datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), in which only four of 15 phenotypes shared any common stable features across datasets. After concordance filtering, cross-dataset feature overlap increased, consistent with less dataset-specific fitting.
Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant} further shows that these shared features often correspond to established pathway genes. This cross-dataset convergence was not observed when the models were trained on aggregated data (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).

When concordantly trained models were applied to discordant samples (where GapMind predictions contradicted experimental outcomes), they achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.18--0.60 (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C).
Performance varied substantially across phenotypes, with the wide range likely reflecting heterogeneous causes of discordance.
For some phenotypes, above-random accuracy suggests that ML may capture features beyond GapMind's curated gene sets; however, organisms whose phenotypes depend on unique pathways or regulatory mechanisms remain beyond the reach of such models.

\subsection*{Strategies to improve model performance}

Concordance filtering improved transfer, but it has practical constraints: it removes approximately 30\% of the samples and requires mechanistic predictors, such as GapMind, which exist only for well-characterised phenotypes.
We therefore tested alternative filters that targeted data quality without requiring external mechanistic predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).

We systematically identified problematic samples in two categories: microbes showing zero growth on all substrates despite at least one GapMind growth prediction (potentially dead cultures or contaminated samples), and microbes showing growth on all substrates despite incomplete GapMind pathways (potentially mislabelled or unusual strains).
A small number of genomes were responsible for a disproportionate share of the misclassifications, and most of these belonged to one of the two discordant categories (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).
Removing these samples and filtering by measurement confidence (see Methods) improved cross-dataset performance, although not as much as concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B).
This confidence-based filter was intentionally heuristic and was used to test whether a simple quality score could recover part of the concordance-filtering gain without requiring a mechanistic predictor.
Confidence-based filtering removed substantially fewer samples than concordance filtering and produced smaller but consistent improvements in cross-dataset performance. This makes it a useful alternative when concordance filtering is not possible.

Among the three filtering strategies, concordant-trained models showed the best precision-recall trade-off (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C).
Removing problematic genomes and confidence-based filtering provided modest improvements.
Depending on the application, prioritising precision (minimising false positives) or recall (minimising false negatives) may be more informative than balanced accuracy.

We also compared models trained using combined features from GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST with models trained using phenotype-filtered GapMind features (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature filtering had little effect under random splits but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy, consistent with previous findings that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive feature sets for generalisation \cite{Li2023-up}.
Cross-dataset performance improved most when the retained samples matched the pathway-based feature space; adding broader feature sets alone did not produce the same gain.

\subsection*{Sample size requirements}

Given that concordant training improved generalisation with fewer but higher-quality samples, we next asked how many such samples were needed.
The learning curves for concordant-trained models on the histidine phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:model_sample_size_requirements}) show how performance changed with training sample size under random cross-validation, cross-dataset, and out-of-clade splits.
Each panel further breaks down performance on full test samples, concordant test samples, and discordant test samples.
A corresponding learning-curve analysis for galactose is provided in Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:galactose_sample_size_requirements}.

For histidine utilisation, performance plateaued at approximately 50--100 concordant training samples across evaluation scenarios, indicating that strong generalisation can be achieved with modest data in this shared carbon-utilisation setting when genotype--phenotype mappings are internally consistent and represented in the feature space \cite{Figueroa2012SampleSize}.
Extending this analysis to all 15 shared phenotypes showed that the histidine result was broadly representative rather than exceptional (Supplementary Figures~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}--\ref{fig:all_phenotype_saturation}).
Most shared phenotypes reached the 90\% saturation threshold by 50--100 samples, only glucose and galacturonic acid required 200 samples, and no phenotype showed a new saturation point beyond 200 samples.
Some phenotypes nevertheless remained limited in absolute performance, indicating that modest sample requirements do not guarantee high balanced accuracy when the underlying signal is weak or ambiguous.
