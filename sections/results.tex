\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate factors governing genotype--phenotype prediction accuracy and generalisation, we harmonised four published binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{Bochner2001PM,Bochner2009PM}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{Timm2015Populus}) covering phylogenetically and ecologically diverse strains on various carbon sources (819 input genomes, 795 retained after filtering; Methods).
Genomes were annotated with RAST, KOFAM, and GapMind, classifiers were trained on the resulting feature matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A), and analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B); Populus was excluded from selected feature-stability comparisons due to small sample size, and datasets differed in taxonomic coverage (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}).


To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two approaches: GapMind, a mechanistic tool predicting growth from pathway completeness~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and a phylogeny-based nearest neighbour classifier (k=3) using the three closest strains (Methods).
GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $0.65 \pm 0.04$; permissive: $0.70 \pm 0.08$; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
The phylogeny-based approach, leveraging the documented link between metabolic phenotypes and phylogeny \cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob}, performed well in random holdout evaluations (0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B), approaching null model levels and indicating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient across diverse lineages~\cite{Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.
% Cellobiose and glycerol showed unexpectedly high out-of-clade accuracy ($>$0.80), potentially reflecting highly conserved pathways.
These limited baselines motivated testing whether machine learning over comprehensive genomic features could better capture genotype--phenotype relationships across experimental contexts.


\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Machine learning models generalise within but not across datasets}

To overcome these baseline limitations, we trained gradient-boosted tree (GBT) classifiers on KOFAM annotations ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; Methods), evaluating all models with balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance.

Data were aggregated across all four datasets and performance assessed under four evaluation scenarios that progressively test generalisation:
(1) repeated random holdout evaluation, with training and test samples drawn from the same phenotype-cluster distribution;
(2) cross-dataset evaluation, training on three datasets and testing on the held-out one;
(3) in-clade cross-dataset splits, restricting evaluation to phylogenetically similar samples to isolate dataset from phylogenetic effects; and
(4) out-of-clade splits, with phylogenetically distant training and test samples.
For random holdout evaluations, test samples were verified to come from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In repeated random holdout evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and outperformed the GapMind baseline (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank across the 15 shared phenotypes, $p < 0.001$).
Cross-dataset performance, however, decreased substantially (0.50--0.75; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts.
The gap between regular and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), indicating that phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure and that dataset-level inconsistencies likely contribute substantially.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with prior observations that random holdout evaluations overestimate generalisation by fitting phylogeny-specific patterns \cite{Martiny2015-ie,Li2023-up}.
Despite the expectation that well-characterised catabolic pathways would enable cross-dataset transfer \cite{Gowda2022-cf,Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis}, models trained on aggregated data did not consistently learn transferable mechanistic features.

\subsection*{Misclassification patterns implicate training-data heterogeneity}

To understand these failures, we examined GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B), which can arise from incomplete annotations, alternative routes, and regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances}.
Although phenotypes varied in GapMind accuracy (e.g., mannitol, galactose higher; serine, alanine lower), no consistent directional bias was observed in misclassifications, and datasets similarly varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) without directional bias, indicating that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact.

To probe ML generalisation failures, we performed SHAP-based feature-importance stability analyses (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
Across random holdout evaluations of the aggregated dataset, at least one stable feature cluster was consistently identified (top panel), indicating recovery of recurring predictors when training and test samples shared distribution.
By contrast, comparing stable feature clusters between the model trained on the three non-held-out datasets and the model trained on the held-out dataset alone (bottom panel), only six of fifteen phenotypes (glucose, histidine, maltose, mannitol, sucrose, and m-inositol \cite{Weber2022-ce}) shared at least one common stable feature cluster.
Features were grouped into clusters by their SHAP-supervised redundancy (Methods) so that KOs representing the same biological signal would be counted as a single shared signal rather than several spurious agreements.

Examining the KOFAM annotations of these stable features (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}) showed that for histidine, the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets, indicating that models can recover pathway-linked features when those genes are present, consistently annotated, and clearly tied to the phenotype.
Core histidine enzymes K01745 (histidine ammonia-lyase) and K05603 (formimidoyl-glutamate deiminase), present among ATLeaf's stable features, were absent from the aggregated dataset, likely due to annotation gaps or alternative pathways.
These analyses suggest the aggregated training set mixes mechanistically tractable samples with intractable ones; we therefore hypothesised that restricting training to samples with internally consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would let models recover transferable mechanistic features, and used GapMind concordance to stratify samples by mechanistic consistency.

\subsection*{Concordant training produces bounded specialist models}

We defined concordant samples as those in which GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes; these likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways already captured in annotation databases.
Concordance served only to stratify samples; feature space, architecture, training procedure, and evaluation were otherwise unchanged from Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}, and using KOFAM annotations as inputs decoupled the concordance criterion from the feature space while preserving a broad, pathway-linked representation.
Directly comparing full-trained and concordant-trained models under the same KOFAM feature space confirmed that the gain followed sample stratification rather than reuse of GapMind-derived input features (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant}).
Because organisms using alternative pathways or context-dependent regulation are underrepresented, improved transfer should be read as better learning on a mechanistically homogeneous subset rather than recovery of all valid utilisation strategies.
However, the machine-learning model is not limited to reproducing GapMind rules: when discordant phenotypes are associated with genome features present in the training data, concordant-trained models may still classify some of those cases correctly.

Models trained on concordant samples achieved near-perfect balanced accuracy ($>$0.95) on held-out concordant test data under random holdout evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A), serving mainly as a sanity check since concordance filtering removes ambiguously-labelled cases.
Concordance-filtered cross-dataset splits, the more informative generalisation measure, also reached high accuracy ($>$0.90) on many phenotype-dataset combinations, consistent with improved transfer when retained samples are supported by pathway-linked features; glucose (mean BA 0.51) and serine (0.73) remained low despite shared stable features.
Because the learning algorithm and evaluation scheme were unchanged, these gains point to the training data as the main difference.

To establish whether the machine-learning pipeline itself can recover pathway-completeness logic when features fully encode the underlying mechanism, we retrained the same concordant models on GapMind step features (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed reference lines) in addition to the KOFAM main analysis.
Under random holdout evaluation, GapMind-feature models reached high balanced accuracy across phenotypes (median 0.98; range 0.92--1.00), confirming that the pipeline can learn the deterministic pathway-completeness rule when the input features themselves encode it.
The per-phenotype cross-dataset gap between KOFAM and GapMind features is therefore interpretable as the mechanistic information lost by replacing GapMind's curated pathway annotations with general KOFAM annotations.
Across the (phenotype, held-out dataset) cells retained by the minority-class filter (Methods), GapMind features exceeded KOFAM features by a small but significant margin (median GapMind cross-dataset BA $=0.90$ vs KOFAM $=0.83$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$).
The largest individual phenotype gap is glucose, where the GapMind ceiling of 0.77 substantially exceeds the KOFAM cross-dataset BA of 0.51, consistent with the GapMind glucose pathway invoking organism-specific transporter and PTS-system identifiers (e.g., \verb|MFS-glucose|, \verb|SSS-glucose|, \verb|ptsG-crr|) that lack direct KOFAM equivalents.
For most phenotypes the gap is below 0.1, indicating that KOFAM is typically a sufficient feature space for cross-dataset transfer; the residual gap on a small number of phenotypes (notably glucose) reflects pathway-specific annotation limitations rather than a failure of the ML pipeline.

Cross-dataset stability analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B) showed a notable increase in shared stable feature clusters compared to full, unfiltered training (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C): twelve of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one cluster under concordant training, up from six under full-data training, and the mean number of shared clusters per held-out comparison roughly doubled (1.3 versus 0.5).
These shared clusters often grouped established pathway genes, for example histidine-utilisation KOs K01468 (imidazolonepropionase) and K01712 (urocanate hydratase) within a single redundancy cluster recovered across datasets (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant}), a convergence largely absent on aggregated data (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).
For three phenotypes (alanine, mannose, serine), high concordant cross-dataset balanced accuracy was not accompanied by any shared target-aware redundancy clusters; the stable features picked by different concordant-trained models for these phenotypes were functionally heterogeneous (e.g., for mannose, ribose-transporter K10441 and D-xylose-transporter K10545 correlated at $|r|=0.67$ within the held-out concordant ATLeaf samples), reflecting transfer through co-occurring carbohydrate-utilisation markers characteristic of broad carbohydrate-utilising organisms rather than through mannose-specific mechanistic features---a less specific mode of generalisation that nonetheless remains within the applicability domain.
Feature-direction analysis for representative phenotypes (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:shap_beeswarm}) further confirmed that the presence of top KOFAM pathway features, such as histidine-utilisation genes and galactose-associated KOs including K01190, consistently shifted predictions toward growth, supporting a mechanistic reading.

Applied to discordant samples (where GapMind predictions contradicted experimental outcomes), the same models reached low cross-dataset balanced accuracy overall (median 0.34; range 0.14--0.68; Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C), with the wide range likely reflecting heterogeneous causes of discordance.
Above-random accuracy on some phenotypes indicates that ML captured transferable features beyond GapMind's curated gene sets, although organisms whose phenotypes depend on unique pathways, regulatory mechanisms, or experimental context remain beyond reach of static genome-derived features.

In deployment, a concordant-trained model is applied to samples not pre-selected for concordance; we therefore re-evaluated the same models on the full held-out cross-dataset test set, decomposing residual error into false-positive-discordant and false-negative-discordant contributions (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:full_held_out_test}).
Median balanced accuracy on the full test set was 0.68 (range 0.47--0.94), substantially above random and intermediate between the concordant-only (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A) and discordant-only (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C) values above.
This deployment-like evaluation showed that concordant training produces a useful but bounded predictor: it can extend beyond the mechanistic baseline for some discordant samples, but it does not resolve all discordance.
The residual error was dominated by false-positive-discordant samples for most phenotypes, indicating the dominant failure mode: organisms for which GapMind asserts a complete pathway but no growth is observed, a pattern more consistent with regulatory repression or experimental artefact than with missing gene content.

\subsection*{Quality-based filters and pathway-restricted features partially recover the concordance gain}

Concordance filtering improved transfer and clarified the main residual failure mode, but it has practical constraints: it removes approximately 30\% of samples and requires mechanistic predictors such as GapMind, available only for well-characterised phenotypes.
We therefore tested alternative filters targeting data quality without external mechanistic predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A), identifying two categories of problematic samples: microbes showing zero growth on all substrates despite at least one GapMind growth prediction, and microbes showing growth on all substrates despite incomplete GapMind pathways; a small number of genomes accounted for a disproportionate share of misclassifications, most belonging to one of these categories (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).
Removing these samples and filtering by measurement confidence (Methods) improved cross-dataset performance, though less than concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B); measurement-confidence filtering removed far fewer samples and produced smaller but consistent gains, a useful alternative when concordance filtering is not possible.
Concordant-trained models showed the best precision--recall trade-off among the three filters (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C), while the GapMind baseline occupied a similar range to the two weaker filters, indicating that these recovered only part of the concordance advantage.

We also compared models trained on combined GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST features with models trained on phenotype-filtered GapMind features (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature filtering had little effect under random holdout evaluation but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy (paired Wilcoxon across phenotype means: random holdout, $p=0.173$; cross-dataset, $p=0.002$), consistent with prior reports that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive sets for generalisation \cite{Li2023-up}; the precision--recall inset showed phenotype-filtered features primarily improved cross-dataset recall without a large precision penalty.
Overall, quality-based sample filtering and pathway-restricted features each recovered only part of the concordance-driven gain, narrowing but not closing the gap relative to mechanistically concordant training.

\subsection*{Most phenotypes saturate at 50--100 coherent training samples}

Having established that training-set coherence, rather than the classifier itself, drove the largest transfer gains, we next asked how many coherent samples were needed.
The learning curves for concordant-trained models on the histidine phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:model_sample_size_requirements}) show how performance changed with training sample size under random holdout, cross-dataset, and out-of-clade evaluations.
Each panel further breaks down performance on full test samples, concordant test samples, and discordant test samples.

Across all 15 shared phenotypes, performance plateaued at approximately 50--100 concordant training samples under random holdout and cross-dataset evaluations, with only glucose and galacturonic acid requiring 200 samples and no phenotype showing a new saturation point beyond 200 (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}).
Strong generalisation is therefore achievable with modest data when genotype--phenotype mappings are internally consistent and represented in the feature space~\cite{Figueroa2012SampleSize}.
Some phenotypes remained limited in absolute performance, indicating that modest sample requirements do not guarantee high balanced accuracy when the underlying signal is weak or ambiguous.
Sample size was therefore rarely the binding constraint for these carbon-utilisation phenotypes; where performance remained low, the limitation lay in the strength of the underlying genotype--phenotype signal rather than in the amount of training data.

\subsection*{Model confidence defines an applicability domain for genome-based prediction}

A concordant-trained model is, by construction, a specialist centred on the known-mechanism domain rather than a universal predictor.
The preceding sections show how to build such a specialist, but deployment requires a second step: deciding when to trust it on new genomes whose concordance cannot be established in advance because the experimental outcome is not available.
We therefore asked whether the model can itself flag which of its predictions are reliable.
Re-evaluating the concordant-trained models on the full held-out cross-dataset test set, we examined two reliability signals that require only the genome sequence and the GapMind annotation, not the experimental phenotype: the model's own prediction confidence ($\max(p,\,1-p)$ from the predicted class probabilities) and the agreement between the GapMind call and the machine learning prediction.
Abstaining on the least-confident predictions within each predicted class produced per-phenotype risk--coverage curves (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A) that differed sharply with the phenotype's cross-dataset generalisation, illustrated here for three phenotypes spanning the shortcut-gap ranking of Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C.
For m-Inositol, the strongest cross-dataset generaliser, balanced accuracy remained near 0.80 across all coverage levels, consistent with an already accurate model on which confidence-based abstention added little.
For Histidine, an intermediate generaliser, balanced accuracy rose from 0.76 at full coverage to 0.90 when only the top 10\% most confident predictions were retained, the canonical risk--coverage tradeoff in which abstention substantially improves the reliability of the committed predictions.
For Glucose, the weakest generaliser, balanced accuracy remained near 0.5 regardless of coverage: when a model's predictions collapse to a single class, confidence-based abstention cannot recover useful predictions.
GapMind--ML agreement provided a complementary signal (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B): the 83\% of genomes for which the curated mechanistic call and the model prediction coincided reached a balanced accuracy of 0.75, whereas the 17\% on which they disagreed performed close to random (0.49).
Both signals are label-free and computable for a previously uncharacterised genome, so they define a practical applicability domain for genome-based phenotype prediction~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Papangelou2025Conformal,Hendrickx2024Reject}: the model does not commit to every genome but identifies which predictions to trust and flags the rest, often organisms outside the known-mechanism domain, as priority candidates for experimental characterisation aimed at distinguishing alternative mechanisms, annotation gaps, regulatory effects, and experimental artefacts.

At the phenotype level, the same diagnostic logic also identified an a priori signal of when concordance filtering is worth the experimental cost of curating concordant samples.
Across the 15 phenotypes, the shortcut gap of the full-data model (mean random-holdout balanced accuracy minus mean cross-dataset balanced accuracy) tracked the subsequent concordance benefit (mean concordant cross-dataset balanced accuracy minus mean full-data cross-dataset balanced accuracy; Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C; Spearman $\rho = 0.27$, $p = 0.32$).
Glucose, with the largest shortcut gap, also showed the largest gain from concordance filtering, whereas several phenotypes with small shortcut gaps (m-inositol, cellobiose) showed little or no benefit.
The small number of phenotypes limits statistical power, but the pattern offers a usable heuristic: when the full-data model exhibits a large gap between within-dataset and cross-dataset accuracy, concordance filtering is more likely to substantially improve transfer; when the gap is small, filtering is less likely to help.
This phenotype-level diagnostic complements the feature-stability signal of Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C: phenotypes whose full-data models share few stable features across datasets and exhibit a large shortcut gap are the same phenotypes whose generalisation benefits most from concordance filtering, and either signal can flag the need for filtering before concordant samples are curated.
Together these signals form a single workflow: the shortcut gap (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C) diagnoses which phenotypes most need additional data, GapMind--ML disagreement (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B) targets the specific genomes most responsible for the residual error, and model confidence (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A) certifies the deployed predictions.
A retrospective active-learning pilot supported the intervention step: adding 25 simulated held-out labels chosen by GapMind--ML disagreement raised cross-dataset balanced accuracy roughly twice as much as random selection on average (mean $\Delta$ balanced accuracy $= 0.12$ versus $0.06$ across 21 runs), with the gain concentrated on glucose, the high-shortcut-gap phenotype, and effectively absent on m-inositol, where the full-data model already generalises (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D).
