\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate factors governing genotype--phenotype prediction accuracy and generalisation, we harmonised four binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{BiologDatasetCitation}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{Timm2015Populus}) covering phylogenetically and ecologically diverse strains (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}) on various carbon sources (819 input genomes, 795 retained after filtering; Methods).
Genomes were annotated with RAST, KOFAM, and GapMind, and classifiers were trained on the resulting feature matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A).
Analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B); Populus was excluded from selected feature-stability comparisons because of small sample size.


To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two approaches: GapMind, a mechanistic tool predicting growth from pathway completeness~\cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and a phylogeny-based nearest neighbour classifier (k=3) that used the phenotypic information of the three closest strains for prediction (Methods).
GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $0.65 \pm 0.04$; permissive: $0.70 \pm 0.08$; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
The phylogeny-based approach, leveraging the documented link between metabolic phenotypes and phylogeny \cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob}, performed well in random holdout evaluations (0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B), approaching null model levels and indicating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient across diverse lineages~\cite{Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.
The limited mechanistic and phylogenetic baseline performance motivated testing whether machine learning over comprehensive genomic features could capture genotype--phenotype relationships across experimental contexts.


\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Machine learning models predict accurately within but not across datasets}

To test whether genome-derived features could overcome the limitations of the mechanistic and phylogenetic baselines, we trained gradient-boosted tree (CatBoost) classifiers on KOFAM annotations ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; Methods), evaluating all models with balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance.

Data were aggregated across all four datasets and assessed under four evaluation scenarios that progressively test generalisation: repeated random holdout (training and test drawn from the same datasets); cross-dataset (training on three datasets and testing on the held-out one); in-clade cross-dataset splits (restricting evaluation to phylogenetically similar samples to isolate dataset from phylogenetic effects); and out-of-clade splits (phylogenetically distant training and test samples).
Random holdout test samples were found to be drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In repeated random holdout evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and outperformed the GapMind baseline (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank across the 15 shared phenotypes, $p < 0.001$).
Cross-dataset performance, however, decreased substantially (0.50--0.75; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts.
The gap between regular and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), indicating that phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure and that dataset-specific signals likely contribute substantially.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with prior observations that random holdout evaluations overestimate generalisation by fitting phylogeny-specific patterns \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation,Li2023-up}.
Despite the expectation that well-characterised catabolic pathways would enable cross-dataset transfer \cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Gralka2023-ft}, models trained on aggregated data did not consistently learn transferable mechanistic features.

\subsection*{Misclassification patterns implicate training-data heterogeneity}

To understand the cross-dataset failures, we first examined GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B), which can arise from incomplete annotations, alternative routes, and regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances,Gorke2008CCR}.
Although phenotypes varied in GapMind accuracy (e.g., mannitol, galactose higher; serine, alanine lower), no consistent directional bias was observed in misclassifications, and datasets similarly varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) without directional bias, indicating that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact.

To probe ML generalisation failures, we performed SHAP-based feature-importance stability analyses (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), grouping KOs by SHAP-supervised redundancy (Methods) so that biologically equivalent features are counted as a single signal.
Across random holdout evaluations (cross-valiation) of the aggregated dataset, at least one stable feature cluster was consistently identified (top panel), indicating recovery of recurring predictors when training data is sampled from the same pool.
By contrast, only six of fifteen phenotypes (glucose, histidine, maltose, mannitol, sucrose, and m-inositol) shared at least one common stable feature cluster between models trained on the three non-held-out datasets and the held-out dataset alone (bottom panel; per-phenotype full-data counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}).

Examining KOFAM annotations of the cross-dataset stable features (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}) showed that for histidine, the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets.
The repeated recovery of K01712 indicates that models can recover pathway-linked features when those genes are present, consistently annotated, and clearly tied to the phenotype.
Other core histidine enzymes were present in dataset-specific stable features but absent from the aggregated dataset, likely because of annotation gaps or alternative pathways.
The misclassification and feature-stability analyses suggest the aggregated training set mixes mechanistically tractable samples with intractable ones.
We therefore hypothesised that restricting training to samples with internally consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would let models recover transferable mechanistic features, and we used GapMind concordance to stratify samples by mechanistic consistency.

\subsection*{Concordant training produces bounded specialist models}

To test the training-data heterogeneity hypothesis, we restricted training to concordant samples --- those in which GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes --- while keeping feature space (KOFAM), architecture, and evaluation unchanged from Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}, so any gain reflects the training data alone (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant}; Supplementary Text~S5).
Concordant samples likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways already captured in annotation databases, and may also overrepresent well-studied taxa whose genomes the annotation pipeline can score most reliably, so the resulting model is a specialist for mechanistically tractable cases rather than a recoverer of all valid utilisation strategies (a taxonomic confound we return to in the Discussion).

On held-out concordant test data, the concordant-trained model reached per-phenotype balanced accuracy of 0.91--0.99 under random holdout (median 0.95) --- a sanity check, since concordance filtering removes ambiguously-labelled cases --- and median 0.83 under cross-dataset evaluation, with 18 of 44 phenotype--dataset cells above 0.90 (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
Glucose (mean BA 0.51) and serine (0.73) remained low despite shared stable features.
Retraining the same models on GapMind step features established a pipeline-ceiling reference (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed lines): the per-phenotype KOFAM-vs-GapMind cross-dataset gap (median BA $0.83$ vs $0.90$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$) quantifies the mechanistic information lost by replacing GapMind's curated annotations with general KOFAM annotations, with the largest single-phenotype gap on glucose (GapMind 0.77 vs KOFAM 0.51) reflecting organism-specific transporter identifiers that lack direct KOFAM equivalents.
For most phenotypes the gap was below 0.1 (Supplementary Text~S11), and additional concordant labels can still raise KOFAM accuracy toward this ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D).

Cross-dataset feature stability also improved under concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; per-phenotype counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}): twelve of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one stable cluster between three-dataset and held-out-alone models, up from six under full-data training (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), and the mean number of shared clusters per held-out comparison roughly doubled (1.3 versus 0.5).
Shared clusters often grouped established pathway genes; for histidine, the urocanate-pathway enzymes K01468 (imidazolonepropionase) and K01712 (urocanate hydratase) co-occurred within a single redundancy cluster recovered across datasets alongside the histidine-utilisation repressor K05836, with the shared cluster count rising from 4 to 8 under concordant training (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant}).
Held-out-only features also became qualitatively more on-pathway: for histidine, the fraction of unique cluster representatives lying in histidine metabolism rose from 29\% under full-data to 75\% under concordant training (KEGG pathway map00340; the narrower KEGG module M00045 statistic is reported in Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:feature_comparison} and \ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant} and Supplementary Text~S11), whereas full-data training also recovered off-pathway annotations such as $\beta$-ketoadipate components.
For three phenotypes (alanine, mannose, serine), high concordant cross-dataset accuracy was instead driven by broader carbohydrate-utilisation markers --- a less specific but still in-domain mode of transfer (Supplementary Text~S8).

To quantify what the concordant-trained KOFAM model adds over GapMind in deployment, we re-evaluated the same models on the full natural-composition held-out test set (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C) and then decomposed the cross-dataset test set by GapMind-concordance status (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).
Under random holdout the ML model exceeded GapMind on 14 of 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$; mean $+0.06$), but under cross-dataset evaluation the mean shift was undetectable across 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.52$, BH-adjusted $q = 0.58$; with $n = 15$ this is the absence of a mean difference rather than evidence of equivalence), because per-phenotype gains were offset by losses where the model lost signal relative to the mechanistic call (notably galacturonic acid; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:full_held_out_test}).
The decomposition explains the dilution: inside the applicability domain the ML model reached median balanced accuracy 0.83 --- the residual KOFAM-vs-GapMind feature-resolution gap rather than a gain over GapMind, which sits at $y = 1$ by construction --- and outside it the ML model still reached 0.34, correctly relabelling 40\% of GapMind false negatives but only 20\% of false positives (per-phenotype rescue rates 44\% vs 19\%, paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$), consistent with annotation-gap discordance leaving a residual genomic signature that ML can read while regulatory or context-dependent discordance does not (Supplementary Text~S11).
The concordant-trained ML advantage over GapMind is therefore real but bounded: visible under random holdout and inside the applicability domain, averaged out across mixed-composition cross-dataset cells.

Data requirements for the specialist were modest --- concordant-model performance plateaued at 50--100 training samples for 13 of 15 phenotypes, with only glucose and galacturonic acid requiring 200 (Supplementary Figures~\ref{fig:sample_size_histidine} and~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}) --- but concordance filtering itself removes approximately 30\% of samples and requires a mechanistic predictor that exists only for well-characterised phenotypes, motivating tests of simpler quality filters that do not require an external mechanistic call.

\subsection*{Quality-based filters and pathway-restricted features partially recover the concordance gain}

As a robustness check, we tested alternative filters targeting data quality without external mechanistic predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).
A small number of genomes accounted for a disproportionate share of misclassifications; most were microbes with zero growth across substrates despite at least one GapMind growth prediction, or microbes with universal growth despite incomplete GapMind pathways.
Filtering by measurement confidence improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy on the full held-out test set, whereas removing systematically discordant ("problematic") samples did not deliver a measurable mean improvement on its own (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B); measurement-confidence filtering removed roughly a quarter of the training samples and produced a small but consistent gain, providing a lower-burden alternative when concordance filtering is not possible.
Concordant training was the strongest of the three filters on average, although its margin over measurement-confidence filtering was modest on these data, indicating that label-noise-style confidence filtering recovers most of the concordance advantage without requiring an external mechanistic predictor.
Concordant-trained models achieved the best precision--recall trade-off (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C) by recovering recall relative to the GapMind baseline (mean $+0.06$ across phenotypes, paired Wilcoxon $p=0.03$, concordant ML higher in 12/15) at minimal precision cost; measurement-confidence-filtered models occupied a similar region but did not significantly improve recall over GapMind, and problematic-sample removal did not recover the concordance precision--recall gain.

We also compared models trained on combined GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST features with models trained on phenotype-filtered GapMind features (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature filtering had little effect under random holdout evaluation but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.004$, BH-adjusted $q=0.01$), consistent with prior reports that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive sets for generalisation \cite{Li2023-up}; phenotype-filtered features primarily improved cross-dataset recall without a large precision penalty.
Overall, quality-based sample filtering and pathway-restricted features each recovered only part of the concordance-driven gain, narrowing but not closing the gap relative to mechanistically concordant training and confirming that the concordance-based specialist remains the highest-performing configuration when a mechanistic predictor is available.

\subsection*{Model confidence defines an applicability domain and prioritises follow-up experiments}

On a new genome, modest training requirements do not certify any single prediction: deploying the specialist on a genome whose concordance cannot be established in advance requires a per-prediction signal of when the model is operating inside its applicability domain, and the specialist's advantage also averages out on arbitrary natural-composition mixtures (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C,D).
We therefore asked whether a model can flag which of its own predictions to trust using only information available without the experimental phenotype.
Prediction confidence ($\max(p,\,1-p)$) ranked correct above incorrect predictions for both models, though only modestly when pooled across phenotypes (ROC AUC $0.65$ concordant, $0.62$ full-data).
Per-phenotype AUC ranged from near-chance for glycerol to $0.73$--$0.78$ for the phenotypes with the highest confidence-ranking performance, and both models were overconfident under cross-dataset shift (ECE $= 0.12$ and $0.18$ for the full-data and concordant models, respectively; Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B; Supplementary Text~S11), so confidence is useful for ranking and abstention but not as a calibrated probability.
Abstaining on the least-confident predictions raised retained-subset balanced accuracy for phenotypes the models already predict well (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A): for histidine, abstention lifted concordant-model balanced accuracy from $0.76$ to $0.84$ at half coverage, defining a practical applicability domain for the deployed model~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject}.

At the phenotype level, however, no label-free signal predicted which phenotypes a model would handle reliably: neither mean prediction confidence nor feature-space novelty correlated with cross-dataset balanced accuracy across the 15 phenotypes (Spearman $\rho$ between $-0.17$ and $0.05$, all $p > 0.3$).
Because per-phenotype reliability cannot be certified in advance, we asked instead which additional measurements would most improve the deployed concordant model --- a natural extension of the sample-size analysis above, which assumed labels were acquired at random.
In a retrospective simulation, 25 held-out genome labels were added to the concordant training set and the model retrained (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C).
Selecting the least-confident genomes improved transfer the most, exceeding random selection (mean $\Delta$ balanced accuracy $0.09$ vs $0.07$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.001$), whereas selecting the most novel or diverse genomes did not beat random.
The advantage concentrated on glucose, the lowest-performing phenotype among the three shown in Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D, where low-confidence selection roughly doubled the gain from random labelling ($0.24$ vs $0.13$), while the already-reliable m-inositol and histidine gained little from either strategy.
Model confidence therefore provides a deployment-oriented ranking signal, enriching for predictions more suitable for selective reporting and for experiments likely to improve the model.
Together with the data-coherence, filter-alternative, and sample-size results above, the per-genome confidence rankings complete a deployable workflow whose use is bounded by an explicit, label-free applicability gate.
