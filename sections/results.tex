\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate carbon-utilisation prediction and generalisation, we harmonised four binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{BiologDatasetCitation}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{PopulusDatasetCitation}) covering diverse strains (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}) on various carbon sources (819 input strain records, 795 with an available genome assembly, and 780 feature-linked strains eligible for analysis; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dataset_overview}).
We annotated genomes with RAST, KOFAM, and GapMind, trained classifiers on the resulting feature matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A), and focused analyses on the 15 shared phenotypes (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B).
Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}C provides a guide to the analyses that follow, with the balanced accuracy of each variant shown alongside it.


To establish baselines, we evaluated GapMind, a mechanistic tool predicting growth from pathway completeness~\cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and a phylogeny-based nearest-neighbour classifier ($k=3$; Methods).
Unlike broad multi-trait predictors such as PICA, Traitar, and MICROPHERRET~\cite{Feldbauer2015PICA,Weimann2016Traitar,Bizzotto2024MICROPHERRET}, GapMind exposes substrate-specific pathway steps, allowing the same mechanistic framework to provide a baseline and define genotype--phenotype concordance.
GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $0.66 \pm 0.04$; permissive: $0.70 \pm 0.08$; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
The phylogeny-based approach performed well in random holdout (0.72--0.87) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade: 0.46--0.63; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B), approaching null-model levels and indicating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient across diverse lineages~\cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob,Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.

\subsection*{Machine learning models predict accurately within but not across datasets}

To test whether genome-derived features could surpass these baselines, we trained gradient-boosted tree (CatBoost) classifiers on KOFAM annotations ($\sim$5500 features after correlation filtering; Methods), evaluating all models with balanced accuracy.

Data were aggregated across all four datasets and assessed under four evaluation scenarios that progressively test generalisation: repeated random holdout, cross-dataset (leave-one-dataset-out), and in-clade and out-of-clade phylogenetic splits (Methods).
Random holdout test samples were drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In repeated random holdout evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and outperformed the GapMind baseline (paired Wilcoxon across the 15 phenotypes, $p < 0.001$).
Cross-dataset performance, however, decreased substantially (0.52--0.84; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts~\cite{Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis}.
The gap between regular and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.03; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), so phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure; the cause more likely lies in other dataset differences, such as growth-assay modality (Methods), ecology and strain history, and curation.
Consistent with this, near-identical strains conflict on their growth label far more often across datasets than within a single dataset, and the gap persists after adjusting for each dataset's positive-call rate (Supplementary Text~S12); this inconsistency limits cross-dataset transfer but does not prevent it, since most near-identical strains still agree.
Out-of-clade counterparts showed similar trends (0.53--0.86; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with prior observations that random holdout overestimates cross-context generalisation \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation,Li2023-up}.

\subsection*{Cross-dataset failures reflect dataset-specific signals in aggregated training}

To understand these failures, we first examined GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B), which can arise from incomplete annotations, alternative routes, or regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances,Gorke2008CCR}.
Phenotypes varied in GapMind accuracy and datasets varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$19\%), but neither showed a consistent directional bias, indicating that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}B).

To probe generalisation failures, we performed SHAP-based feature-importance stability analyses, grouping KOs by SHAP-supervised redundancy (Methods) so biologically equivalent features count as a single signal (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
Across random holdout evaluations of the aggregated dataset, at least one stable feature cluster was consistently identified, indicating recovery of recurring predictors when training data is sampled from the same pool (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C, top).
By contrast, only six of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one common stable feature cluster between models trained on the three non-held-out datasets and the held-out dataset alone (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C, bottom; per-phenotype full-data counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}).
Almost every held-out model still recovered stable clusters, but largely dataset-specific ones, outnumbering shared clusters roughly five to one (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C, patterned bars); the failure is therefore one of shared signal rather than learnable signal.

Among the six phenotypes that did transfer, histidine is illustrative: KOFAM annotations consistently identified the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) across datasets (Supplementary Text~S4)~\cite{Bender2012HutBacteria}.
Together, these analyses indicate that the aggregated training set pools many distinct genotype--phenotype mechanisms, mixing tractable samples with intractable ones (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
We therefore hypothesised that restricting training to samples with more consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would let models recover transferable mechanistic features, using GapMind concordance to isolate this subset.

\subsection*{Concordant training produces bounded specialist models}

To test this hypothesis, we restricted training to concordant samples (GapMind predictions matching experimental outcomes) while keeping the feature space, architecture, and evaluation unchanged from Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}, so any gain reflects the training data alone (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant}; Supplementary Text~S5).
Concordant samples likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways already captured in annotation databases, and may also overrepresent well-studied taxa the annotation pipeline scores most reliably, so the model is a specialist for mechanistically tractable cases rather than a recoverer of all valid utilisation strategies.

On held-out concordant test data (the easier subset, since discordant cases are excluded; full held-out test in Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C), the concordance-trained model reached per-phenotype balanced accuracy of 0.91--0.99 under random holdout (median 0.96) and median 0.82 under cross-dataset evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
We then retrained the same models on the GapMind step features whose rules define concordance, giving a reference for how completely that rule is recovered from the step evidence itself (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed lines).
This reference exceeded the KOFAM models under cross-dataset evaluation (median 0.90 versus 0.82; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.01$), with the gap below 0.1 for most phenotypes but largest for glucose (0.77 versus 0.51), whose substrate-specific transporter steps have no direct KOFAM equivalent (Supplementary Text~S10).

Cross-dataset feature stability also improved under concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; per-phenotype counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}). Twelve of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one stable cluster between three-dataset and held-out-alone models, up from six under full-data training (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), and shared clusters per comparison rose 2.5-fold (1.31 versus 0.52 on the 42 matched comparisons; $p < 0.001$). A size- and class-matched random-subset control reproduced only the full-data level (0.38 $\pm$ 0.03), confirming the gain is specific to concordance, not subsetting (Supplementary Text~S5).
For histidine, concordant training increased recovery of a cluster containing two urocanate-pathway enzymes and the histidine-utilisation repressor while reducing dataset-specific features (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Text~S4; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}), consistent with the established co-regulation of \emph{hut} catabolic genes~\cite{Bender2012HutBacteria}.
For alanine, mannose, and serine, concordant cross-dataset accuracy was instead driven by broader carbohydrate-utilisation markers, a less specific but still in-domain mode of transfer (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Text~S7).
Because concordant samples are those where GapMind's pathway call already matched growth, this recovery is not independent of how the subset was chosen, although the stability of the same features across datasets does not follow from that selection (Supplementary Text~S5).

To quantify what the concordance-trained KOFAM model adds over GapMind, we evaluated the full held-out test set (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C) and decomposed its cross-dataset portion by concordance status (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).
Under random holdout the ML model exceeded GapMind on 14 of 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$; mean $+0.05$), but under cross-dataset evaluation the mean shift was undetectable across 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.80$, BH-adjusted $q = 0.87$; Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C).
Under per-phenotype sample-level tests sensitivity gains were matched by significantly lower specificity for 8 of 15 phenotypes, so the components largely cancel in balanced accuracy (Supplementary Text~S10; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_phenotype_mcnemar}).
Stratifying the cross-dataset test set by concordance status separates two regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).
Where GapMind already agrees with the measurement it is correct by definition, so the model's median balanced accuracy of 0.82 measures the feature-resolution gap discussed above rather than any gain over the rule.
The informative cases are those where GapMind is wrong, and there the model's binary predictions corrected few of them (BA 0.34), limited by how often it predicts growth at all.
We therefore asked whether its predicted growth probability ranks genomes correctly within the set GapMind called positive and the set it called negative, where the rule itself provides no ordering.
It did so in both sets (pooled AUC 0.67), and concordant training helped specifically where GapMind had missed growth despite an incomplete annotated pathway (0.64 versus 0.53, BH-adjusted $q = 0.003$, 13 of 15 phenotypes), not where it had predicted growth that did not occur ($p = 0.76$).
These recovered genomes were predominantly metabolic generalists carrying broad-specificity carbohydrate transporters and sugar-catabolism regulators, consistent with transport being the step GapMind most often failed to place (9 of 15 phenotypes; Supplementary Text~S10).

These gains do not require large training sets: across the 15 shared phenotypes, concordance-trained performance reached 90\% of its all-sample value within 50 to 100 samples under both random-holdout and cross-dataset evaluation (Supplementary Text~S6; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sample_size_phenotypes}), although the phenotypes saturating earliest were also those with the lowest attainable accuracy.
Additionally, increasing volume does not improve generalisability; adding $\sim$1800 BacDive genomes~\cite{Schober2025BacDive} did not significantly improve cross-dataset performance over a much smaller concordant set and gave less reproducible feature selection (Supplementary Text~S11; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:bacdive_curation}).
Concordance filtering, however, requires a mechanistic predictor available only for well-characterised phenotypes and removes approximately 30\% of samples, so we tested mechanism-free alternatives that use only phylogenetic agreement and experimental labels.

\subsection*{Mechanism-free confidence weighting provides a recall-oriented alternative}

Confidence weighting scored each training and validation sample by whether its experimental label agreed with the labels of its nearest phylogenetic neighbours and with GapMind pathway completeness, down-weighting samples where these disagreed. The mechanism-free setting gives GapMind zero weight ($w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0$), and three comparison settings gave it 0.3 to 0.5 (Methods; Supplementary Text~S9).
Held-out test samples were neither weighted nor used in scoring.
A small number of problematic genomes, discordant with GapMind across many phenotypes, accounted for a disproportionate share of misclassifications (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A) and were tested as an additional filter.

Across the $w_{\mathrm{gap}}$ sweep, mean cross-dataset balanced accuracy varied by less than 0.005 while recall rose and precision fell (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B), a shift towards positive calls rather than greater discrimination.
Problematic-genome removal alone gave no measurable improvement.
Concordant training had the highest mean balanced accuracy (0.680 versus 0.644 for mechanism-free weighting), although the difference was not significant (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.23$).
Recall against GapMind is the informative axis here, because its errors are predominantly missed growth. Recovering those genomes indicates catabolic capability that the curated pathway does not represent.
Mechanism-free weighting raised recall slightly over unfiltered ML with no change in balanced accuracy and lower specificity, adding 103 true positives over unfiltered ML and 127 over GapMind on matched positive observations (Supplementary Text~S9).
Concordant training produced significant phenotype-level recall gains over GapMind for more phenotypes (9 of 15 versus 6), although neither mean recall shift reached significance (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C; Supplementary Text~S9).
Recall gains over GapMind were therefore consistent in direction but small when averaged across phenotypes, and the demonstrable advantage is confined to the genomes GapMind calls incorrectly, where the concordance-trained model orders growth outcomes that the GapMind rule cannot (pooled AUC 0.67; Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).

Curation can also be applied to the features rather than to the samples.
Phenotype-filtered GapMind features (without correlation filtering; Supplementary Text~S5) matched combined GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST features under random holdout but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy (paired Wilcoxon $p<0.001$, BH-adjusted $q=0.003$), and the combined-feature models still concentrated on curated GapMind terms, consistent with prior reports favouring biologically relevant over comprehensive feature sets~\cite{Li2023-up} (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Sample filtering, sample weighting, and feature choice all act at training time and shift average performance; none indicates which individual predictions to trust.
We therefore asked whether a model can flag which of its own predictions to trust from information available at inference time, without the experimental phenotype.

\subsection*{Prediction confidence defines an applicability domain and prioritises follow-up experiments}

The model's own predicted probability is available at inference time without the experimental phenotype.
Pooled across phenotypes, the model assigned higher confidence to predictions that proved correct than to those that did not, though only modestly (ROC AUC $0.66$ concordance-trained, $0.63$ full-data), and both models were overconfident under cross-dataset shift (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B; Supplementary Text~S10).
Consistent with prior reports~\cite{NiculescuMizil2005Calibration}, confidence therefore supports ranking and abstention rather than calibrated probability estimates.
Abstaining on the least-confident predictions accordingly helped only where the model had transferable signal to concentrate (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A): for sucrose it raised retained-subset accuracy from $0.75$ at full coverage to $0.88$ at half coverage against a majority-class rate of $0.61$, whereas for m-Inositol, already accurate across the full test set, it added little, and for glycerol it added nothing.
Abstention can therefore define a practical applicability domain~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject} but cannot substitute for a model that has not learned the phenotype.

Confidence did not, however, indicate which phenotypes a model would handle reliably: neither mean prediction confidence nor the fraction of high-confidence predictions correlated with cross-dataset balanced accuracy (Supplementary Text~S8).
Since reliability cannot be certified per phenotype in advance, we asked instead which additional measurements would most improve a model, simulating the addition of 25 held-out genome labels to the concordant training set and retraining (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C).
Selecting the least-confident genomes produced the largest mean gain in cross-dataset balanced accuracy ($0.09$ versus $0.07$ for random; $p = 0.024$, BH-adjusted $q = 0.052$), although the advantage was not consistent across the six phenotype means ($p = 0.16$), and selecting the most novel or diverse genomes did not beat random~\cite{Vasanthakumari2024ActiveLearning}.
The advantage tracked how poorly the model already transferred (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D): largest for glucose, where low-confidence selection roughly doubled the gain from random labelling ($0.24$ versus $0.13$), smaller for mannose, and absent for histidine, whose model already transferred well.
Confidence therefore ranks usefully in two ways, flagging which predictions to withhold and which genomes to measure next, even though it does not reveal which phenotypes will transfer.
