\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate the factors governing genotype-phenotype prediction accuracy and generalisation, we compiled four binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{Bochner2001PM,Bochner2009PM}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{Timm2015Populus}) capturing growth outcomes of phylogenetically and ecologically diverse microbial strains in various carbon sources (819 input genomes, 795 retained after filtering; see Methods for data sources, preprocessing, and quality control procedures).
We aggregated growth phenotypes, annotated genomes with RAST, KOFAM, and GapMind, and trained classifiers on the resulting feature matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A).
All analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes across all datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B).
All four datasets were used in aggregated analyses and leave-one-dataset-out evaluations; Populus was excluded from selected feature-stability comparisons because of its small sample size.
Datasets differed in taxonomic coverage (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}).


To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two existing approaches: (1) GapMind, a mechanistic pathway-based tool that predicts growth capabilities by assessing the completeness of metabolic pathways required for utilising specific carbon sources~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and (2) a phylogeny-based nearest neighbour classifier (k=3) that predicts phenotypes based on the growth outcomes of the three most closely related strains (see Methods).
GapMind achieved a modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $0.65 \pm 0.04$; permissive: $0.70 \pm 0.08$; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
The phylogeny-based approach, which leverages the well-documented association between metabolic phenotypes and phylogenetic relationships \cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob}, performed well within clades (random split: 0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade split: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B).
Out-of-clade performance approached null model levels, demonstrating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient for accurate prediction across diverse lineages~\cite{Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.
% Cellobiose and glycerol showed unexpectedly high out-of-clade accuracy ($>$0.80), potentially reflecting highly conserved pathways.
The limited performance of both baseline approaches motivated us to test whether machine learning models integrating comprehensive genomic features could better capture the complexity of genotype-phenotype relationships and generalise across experimental contexts.


\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Machine learning models generalise within but not across datasets}

To test whether machine learning could overcome the limitations of the baseline approaches, we trained gradient-boosted tree (GBT) classifiers using KOFAM annotations as features ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; see Methods).
All models were evaluated using balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance across phenotypes.

We aggregated data from all four datasets and assessed model performance using four evaluation scenarios designed to progressively test generalisation:
(1) random cross-validation splits across the aggregated dataset, where training and test samples are drawn from the same distribution;
(2) cross-dataset evaluation, training on three datasets and testing on the held-out dataset to assess generalisation across experimental contexts;
(3) in-clade cross-dataset splits, restricting cross-dataset evaluation to phylogenetically similar samples to isolate dataset effects from phylogenetic effects; and
(4) out-of-clade splits, where training and test samples are phylogenetically distant.
For random splits, we verified that test samples were drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In random cross-validation (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and outperformed the GapMind baseline (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test across the 15 shared phenotypes, $p < 0.001$).
However, the cross-dataset performance decreased substantially (0.50--0.75; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts.
The performance difference between regular cross-dataset and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C).
The negligible gap between unrestricted and in-clade cross-dataset performance indicates that phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure; dataset-level inconsistencies in genotype--phenotype relationships likely contribute substantially.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with previous observations that random splits overestimate generalisation due to fitting phylogeny-specific patterns \cite{Martiny2015-ie,Li2023-up}.

Despite the expectation that well-characterised catabolic pathways would enable cross-dataset transfer \cite{Gowda2022-cf,Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis}, these results indicate that models trained on aggregated data do not consistently learn transferable mechanistic features.

\subsection*{Misclassification patterns implicate training-data heterogeneity}

To understand these failures, we analysed why both mechanistic and ML models misclassified some samples.
We analysed GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B).
GapMind misclassifications can arise from incomplete pathway annotations, alternative metabolic routes, and regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances}.
While some phenotypes showed higher GapMind accuracy (e.g., mannitol, galactose) than others (e.g., serine, alanine), we observed no consistent directional bias in misclassifications (false positives versus false negatives).
Similarly, datasets varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) without directional bias, suggesting that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact.

To understand ML generalisation failures, we performed feature importance stability analyses using SHAP (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
Across random splits of the aggregated dataset, at least one feature was consistently identified as important (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C, top panel), indicating recovery of recurring predictors when training and test samples came from the same distribution.
However, when comparing stable features between individual and aggregated datasets (bottom panel; Populus excluded due to small sample size), only histidine, maltose, mannitol, and m-inositol \cite{Weber2022-ce} shared at least one common stable feature across datasets.


We assessed the biological relevance of stable features by examining their KOFAM annotations (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).
For histidine, the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets. This indicates that the models can recover pathway-linked features when those genes are present, consistently annotated, and clearly associated with the phenotype.
However, core histidine pathway enzymes K01745 (histidine ammonia-lyase) and K05603 (formimidoyl-glutamate deiminase), which appeared in ATLeaf's stable features, were not identified in the aggregated dataset, likely due to annotation gaps or alternative pathways in other datasets.

These analyses suggest that the aggregated training set mixes samples that are easy to predict mechanistically with samples that are not.
Therefore, we hypothesised that restricting training to samples with internally consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would allow machine learning models to recover transferable mechanistic features and to generalise across datasets and phylogenetic contexts.
To test this hypothesis, we leveraged GapMind concordance as a criterion for stratifying samples by mechanistic consistency and trained models exclusively on samples in which pathway-based predictions matched experimental outcomes.
\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Training on GapMind-concordant samples recovers mechanistic features and improves transfer}

To implement this stratification, we defined concordant samples as those in which GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes. These samples likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways that are already captured in annotation databases.
Concordance served only to stratify samples; the model feature space, architecture, training procedure, and evaluation strategy were otherwise unchanged from the KOFAM-based models in Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}.
This filter preferentially selects organisms whose canonical pathways are captured in annotation databases; organisms using alternative pathways or context-dependent regulation are underrepresented, so improved transfer should be read as better learning on a mechanistically homogeneous subset rather than recovery of all valid utilisation strategies.
Using KOFAM annotations as model inputs decoupled the concordance criterion from the feature space while preserving a broad, pathway-linked genomic representation.

Models trained on concordant samples achieved near-perfect balanced accuracy ($>$0.95) on held-out concordant test data under random splits (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A); this serves mainly as a sanity check, since concordance filtering removes ambiguously-labelled cases.
Cross-dataset performance is the more informative generalisation measure.
Cross-dataset splits filtered for concordance also reached high accuracy ($>$0.90) on many phenotype-dataset combinations, consistent with improved transfer when retained samples were supported by pathway-linked features.
However, even with concordant data, generalisation failed for some phenotypes (e.g., glucose and serine), despite common stable features across datasets, possibly due to imbalanced class distributions.
Because the learning algorithm and evaluation scheme were unchanged, these gains point to the training data as the main difference.

Cross-dataset stability analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B) showed a notable increase in shared stable features compared to models trained on full, unfiltered datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), in which only four of 15 phenotypes shared any common stable features across datasets. After concordance filtering, cross-dataset feature overlap increased, consistent with less dataset-specific fitting.
These shared features often corresponded to established pathway genes, including histidine-utilisation KOs K01468 and K01712 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant}). This cross-dataset convergence was not observed when the models were trained on aggregated data (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).
Beyond stability, feature-direction analysis for representative phenotypes (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:shap_beeswarm}) confirmed that presence of top KOFAM pathway features, such as histidine-utilisation genes and galactose-associated KOs including K01190, consistently shifted predictions toward growth, supporting a mechanistic reading of the learned models.

When concordantly trained models were applied to discordant samples (where GapMind predictions contradicted experimental outcomes), cross-dataset balanced accuracy was low overall (median 0.34; range 0.00--0.70; Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C).
Performance varied substantially across phenotypes, with the wide range likely reflecting heterogeneous causes of discordance.
For some phenotypes, above-random accuracy suggests that ML may capture features beyond GapMind's curated gene sets; however, organisms whose phenotypes depend on unique pathways or regulatory mechanisms remain beyond the reach of such models.

In practice, applying a concordant-trained model to a new dataset means evaluating it on samples that have not been pre-selected for concordance. We therefore re-evaluated the same concordant-trained models on the full held-out cross-dataset test set, with the residual error decomposed into false-positive-discordant and false-negative-discordant contributions (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:full_held_out_test}).
Median balanced accuracy on the full test set was 0.67 (range 0.47--0.94), substantially above random and intermediate between the concordant-only (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A) and discordant-only (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C) values reported above.
The residual error was dominated by false-positive-discordant samples for most phenotypes, indicating that the dominant failure mode is organisms for which GapMind asserts a complete pathway but no growth is observed, a pattern more consistent with regulatory repression or experimental artefact than with missing gene content.

\subsection*{Quality-based filters and pathway-restricted features partially recover the concordance gain}

Concordance filtering improved transfer, but it has practical constraints: it removes approximately 30\% of the samples and requires mechanistic predictors, such as GapMind, which exist only for well-characterised phenotypes.
We therefore tested alternative filters that targeted data quality without requiring external mechanistic predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).

We systematically identified problematic samples in two categories: microbes showing zero growth on all substrates despite at least one GapMind growth prediction (potentially dead cultures or contaminated samples), and microbes showing growth on all substrates despite incomplete GapMind pathways (potentially mislabelled or unusual strains).
A small number of genomes were responsible for a disproportionate share of the misclassifications, and most of these belonged to one of the two discordant categories (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).
Removing these samples and filtering by measurement confidence (see Methods) improved cross-dataset performance, although not as much as concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B).
Confidence-based filtering removed substantially fewer samples than concordance filtering and produced smaller but consistent improvements in cross-dataset performance. This makes it a useful alternative when concordance filtering is not possible.

Among the three filtering strategies, concordant-trained models showed the best precision-recall trade-off (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C).
The GapMind baseline occupied a similar precision-recall range to the two weaker filtering strategies, indicating that these filters recovered only part of the concordance-filtering advantage.
Removing problematic genomes and confidence-based filtering therefore provided modest improvements rather than fully restoring the concordant-sample performance.
Depending on the application, prioritising precision (minimising false positives) or recall (minimising false negatives) may be more informative than balanced accuracy.

We also compared models trained using combined features from GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST with models trained using phenotype-filtered GapMind features (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature filtering had little effect under random splits but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy (paired Wilcoxon test across phenotype means: random split, $p=0.173$; cross-dataset split, $p=0.004$), consistent with previous findings that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive feature sets for generalisation \cite{Li2023-up}.
The precision-recall inset showed a similar pattern: phenotype-filtered features primarily improved cross-dataset recall without a large precision penalty.
Cross-dataset performance improved most when the retained samples matched the pathway-based feature space; adding broader feature sets alone did not produce the same gain.
Overall, quality-based sample filtering and pathway-restricted features each recovered only part of the concordance-driven gain in cross-dataset transfer, narrowing but not closing the gap relative to training on mechanistically concordant samples.

\subsection*{Most phenotypes saturate at 50--100 coherent training samples}

Given that concordant training improved generalisation with fewer but higher-quality samples, we next asked how many such samples were needed.
The learning curves for concordant-trained models on the histidine phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:model_sample_size_requirements}) show how performance changed with training sample size under random cross-validation, cross-dataset, and out-of-clade splits.
Each panel further breaks down performance on full test samples, concordant test samples, and discordant test samples.

Across all 15 shared phenotypes, performance plateaued at approximately 50--100 concordant training samples under random and cross-dataset splits, with only glucose and galacturonic acid requiring 200 samples and no phenotype showing a new saturation point beyond 200 (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}).
Strong generalisation is therefore achievable with modest data when genotype--phenotype mappings are internally consistent and represented in the feature space~\cite{Figueroa2012SampleSize}.
Some phenotypes remained limited in absolute performance, indicating that modest sample requirements do not guarantee high balanced accuracy when the underlying signal is weak or ambiguous.
Sample size was therefore rarely the binding constraint for these carbon-utilisation phenotypes; where performance remained low, the limitation lay in the strength of the underlying genotype--phenotype signal rather than in the amount of training data.

\subsection*{Model confidence defines an applicability domain for genome-based prediction}

A concordant-trained model is, by construction, a specialist for organisms whose phenotype is governed by a known pathway; in deployment it is applied to new genomes whose concordance cannot be established in advance, because the experimental outcome is not available.
We therefore asked whether the model can itself flag which of its predictions are reliable.
Re-evaluating the concordant-trained models on the full held-out cross-dataset test set, we examined two reliability signals that require only the genome sequence and the GapMind annotation, not the experimental phenotype: the model's own prediction confidence ($\max(p,\,1-p)$ from the predicted class probabilities) and the agreement between the GapMind call and the machine learning prediction.
Ranking held-out genomes by confidence and abstaining on the least-confident fraction produced a risk--coverage curve along which balanced accuracy improved as coverage decreased (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A): pooled balanced accuracy rose from 0.71 when the model committed to every genome to 0.79 at 50\% coverage and 0.84 at 10\% coverage.
GapMind--ML agreement provided a complementary signal (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B): the 83\% of genomes for which the curated mechanistic call and the model prediction coincided reached a balanced accuracy of 0.75, whereas the 17\% on which they disagreed performed close to random (0.49).
Both signals are label-free and computable for a previously uncharacterised genome, so they define a practical applicability domain for genome-based phenotype prediction~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Papangelou2025Conformal,Hendrickx2024Reject}: the model does not commit to every genome, but it identifies which predictions to trust and flags the rest, typically organisms outside the known-mechanism domain, for experimental characterisation.
