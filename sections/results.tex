\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate factors governing genotype--phenotype prediction accuracy and generalisation, we harmonised four binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{BiologDatasetCitation}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{Timm2015Populus}) covering phylogenetically and ecologically diverse strains (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}) on various carbon sources (819 input genomes, 795 retained after filtering; Methods).
Genomes were annotated with RAST, KOFAM, and GapMind, and classifiers were trained on the resulting feature matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A).
Analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B); Populus was excluded from selected feature-stability comparisons because of small sample size.


To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two approaches: GapMind, a mechanistic tool predicting growth from pathway completeness~\cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and a phylogeny-based nearest neighbour classifier (k=3) that used the phenotypic information of the three closest strains for prediction (Methods).
GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $0.65 \pm 0.04$; permissive: $0.70 \pm 0.08$; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
The phylogeny-based approach, leveraging the documented link between metabolic phenotypes and phylogeny \cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob}, performed well in random holdout evaluations (0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B), approaching null model levels and indicating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient across diverse lineages~\cite{Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.
The limited mechanistic and phylogenetic baseline performance motivated testing whether machine learning over comprehensive genomic features could capture genotype--phenotype relationships across experimental contexts.


\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Machine learning models predict accurately within but not across datasets}

To test whether genome-derived features could overcome the limitations of the mechanistic and phylogenetic baselines, we trained gradient-boosted tree (CatBoost) classifiers on KOFAM annotations ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; Methods), evaluating all models with balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance.

Data were aggregated across all four datasets and assessed under four evaluation scenarios that progressively test generalisation: repeated random holdout (training and test drawn from the same datasets); cross-dataset (training on three datasets and testing on the held-out one); in-clade cross-dataset splits (restricting evaluation to phylogenetically similar samples to isolate dataset from phylogenetic effects); and out-of-clade splits (phylogenetically distant training and test samples).
Random holdout test samples were found to be drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In repeated random holdout evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and outperformed the GapMind baseline (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank across the 15 shared phenotypes, $p < 0.001$).
Cross-dataset performance, however, decreased substantially (0.40--0.84; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts~\cite{Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis}.
The gap between regular and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), indicating that phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure and that dataset-specific signals likely contribute substantially.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with prior observations that random holdout overestimates cross-context generalisation \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation,Li2023-up}.
Despite the expectation that well-characterised catabolic pathways would enable cross-dataset transfer \cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Gralka2023-ft}, models trained on aggregated data did not consistently learn transferable mechanistic features.

\subsection*{Cross-dataset failures reflect dataset-specific signal in aggregated training}

To understand the cross-dataset failures, we first examined GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B), which can arise from incomplete annotations, alternative routes, and regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances,Gorke2008CCR}.
Although phenotypes varied in GapMind accuracy (e.g., mannitol, galactose higher; serine, alanine lower), no consistent directional bias was observed in misclassifications, and datasets similarly varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) without directional bias, indicating that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact.

To probe ML generalisation failures, we performed SHAP-based feature-importance stability analyses (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), grouping KOs by SHAP-supervised redundancy (Methods) so that biologically equivalent features are counted as a single signal.
Across random holdout evaluations (cross-validation) of the aggregated dataset, at least one stable feature cluster was consistently identified (top panel), indicating recovery of recurring predictors when training data is sampled from the same pool.
By contrast, only six of fifteen phenotypes (glucose, histidine, maltose, mannitol, sucrose, and m-inositol) shared at least one common stable feature cluster between models trained on the three non-held-out datasets and the held-out dataset alone (bottom panel; per-phenotype full-data counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}).

Examining KOFAM annotations of the cross-dataset stable features (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}) showed that for histidine, the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets~\cite{Bender2012HutBacteria}.
The repeated recovery of K01712 indicates that models can recover pathway-linked features when those genes are present, consistently annotated, and clearly tied to the phenotype.
Other core histidine enzymes were present in dataset-specific stable features but absent from the aggregated dataset, likely because of annotation gaps or alternative pathways.
Heterogeneous training pools that mix well-characterised with mechanistically ambiguous samples are a known source of poor cross-dataset generalisation in genomic ML~\cite{Whalen2022Pitfalls}.
The misclassification and feature-stability analyses suggest the aggregated training set mixes mechanistically tractable samples with intractable ones.
We therefore hypothesised that restricting training to samples with internally consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would let models recover transferable mechanistic features, and we used GapMind concordance to stratify samples by mechanistic consistency.
\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Concordant training produces bounded specialist models}

To test the training-data heterogeneity hypothesis, we restricted training to concordant samples (where GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes) while keeping feature space (KOFAM), architecture, and evaluation unchanged from Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}, so any gain reflects the training data alone (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant}; Supplementary Text~S5).
Concordant samples likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways already captured in annotation databases, and may also overrepresent well-studied taxa whose genomes the annotation pipeline can score most reliably, so the resulting model is a specialist for mechanistically tractable cases rather than a recoverer of all valid utilisation strategies (a taxonomic confound we return to in the Discussion).

On held-out concordant test data, the concordant-trained model reached per-phenotype balanced accuracy of 0.91--0.99 under random holdout (median 0.95) and median 0.83 under cross-dataset evaluation, with 18 of 44 phenotype--dataset cells above 0.90 (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
Glucose (mean BA 0.51) and serine (0.73) remained low despite shared stable features.
Retraining the same models directly on GapMind confidence features established a pipeline-ceiling reference (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed lines): the per-phenotype KOFAM-vs-GapMind cross-dataset gap (median BA $0.83$ vs $0.90$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$) quantifies the mechanistic information lost by replacing GapMind's curated annotations with general KOFAM annotations, with the largest single-phenotype gap on glucose (GapMind 0.77 vs KOFAM 0.51). These are generally due to organism-specific enzyme or transporter annotations that are not as accurate in the KOFAM feature set.
For most phenotypes the gap was below 0.1 (Supplementary Text~S11).

Cross-dataset feature stability also improved under concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; per-phenotype counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}): twelve of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one stable cluster between three-dataset and held-out-alone models, up from six under full-data training (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), and the mean per-comparison count roughly doubled (1.3 versus 0.5).
Shared clusters often grouped established pathway genes; for histidine, the urocanate-pathway enzymes K01468 (imidazolonepropionase) and K01712 (urocanate hydratase) co-occurred within a single redundancy cluster recovered across datasets alongside the histidine-utilisation repressor K05836, with the shared cluster count rising from 4 to 8 under concordant training (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant}).
This co-clustering is consistent with the long-established co-regulation of \emph{hut} catabolic genes and their repressor as an operonic regulon in diverse bacteria~\cite{Bender2012HutBacteria}.
Held-out-alone-only features shifted towards the pathway: for histidine the unique cluster count fell from 6 to 4 while pathway concentration on map00340 rose from 17\% to 50\% under concordant training (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; the stricter KEGG module M00045 statistic is reported in Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:feature_comparison} and~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant} and Supplementary Text~S11), consistent with concordant training trimming dataset-idiosyncratic features while retaining pathway-relevant ones; full-data training also recovered off-pathway annotations such as $\beta$-ketoadipate components.
This map-based metric undercounts mechanistic recovery when KEGG separates regulators or transporters from the catabolic map: for glycerol, all three off-pathway shared clusters are glp-regulon components (glpR, glpF, glpP) despite 0\% formal pathway residency.
For three phenotypes (alanine, mannose, serine), concordant cross-dataset accuracy was instead driven by broader carbohydrate-utilisation markers.
For alanine in particular, GapMind's pathway definition encodes only transport components, so concordance for this phenotype reflects uptake rather than catabolic coverage.
This pattern represents a less specific but still in-domain mode of transfer (Supplementary Text~S8).

To quantify what the concordant-trained KOFAM model adds over GapMind, we re-evaluated the same models on the full held-out test set (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C) and then decomposed the cross-dataset test set by GapMind-concordance status (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).
Under random holdout the ML model exceeded GapMind on 14 of 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$; mean $+0.06$), but under cross-dataset evaluation the mean shift was undetectable across 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.52$, BH-adjusted $q = 0.58$; with $n = 15$), because per-phenotype gains were offset by losses where the model lost signal relative to the mechanistic call (notably galacturonic acid; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:full_held_out_test}).
The decomposition explains the dilution: inside the applicability domain the ML model reached median balanced accuracy 0.83 (largely due to the residual KOFAM-vs-GapMind feature-resolution gap), rather than a gain over GapMind, which sits at $y = 1$ by construction. But outside this, the ML model still reached 0.34, correctly labelling 40\% of GapMind false negatives but only 20\% of false positives (per-phenotype rescue rates 44\% vs 19\%, paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$). This might be due to ML models being able to recognise patterns that can help overcome annotation-gap discordance (Supplementary Text~S11).
The concordant-trained ML advantage over GapMind is therefore real but bounded: visible under random holdout and as the partial rescue of GapMind false negatives in the discordant subset.

Data requirements for the specialist were modest: concordant-model performance plateaued at 50--100 training samples for 13 of 15 phenotypes, with only glucose and galacturonic acid requiring 200 (Supplementary Figures~\ref{fig:sample_size_histidine} and~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}).
Concordance filtering, however, removes approximately 30\% of samples and requires a mechanistic predictor available only for well-characterised phenotypes, motivating tests of mechanism-free alternatives that use only phylogenetic agreement and experimental labels.

\subsection*{Mechanism-free confidence filtering recovers most of the concordance gain}

To test whether the cross-dataset performance gain requires GapMind information at the filtering step, we evaluated a soft-label confidence filter combining phylogenetic $k$-NN agreement, GapMind pathway completeness, and the experimental label, sweeping the GapMind contribution across $w_{\mathrm{gap}} \in \{0, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5\}$; $w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0$ defines a mechanism-free configuration (Methods; Supplementary Text~S10).
A small number of genomes accounted for a disproportionate share of misclassifications: microbes with no growth despite at least one GapMind growth prediction, and microbes with universal growth despite incomplete GapMind pathways (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).
These problematic genomes were used for additional filtering.
Across the sweep, mean cross-dataset balanced accuracy on the full held-out test set was essentially flat (all four settings within $0.015$), but mean recall rose monotonically from $0.76$ at $w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0$ to $0.83$ at $w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0.5$ at near-constant precision (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B); removing systematically problematic samples did not yield a measurable mean improvement on its own.
Concordant training reached the highest mean balanced accuracy ($0.68$, $71\%$ retention, $n\approx 383$) and the mechanism-free filter reached $0.65$ at a similar data cost ($74\%$ retention, $n\approx 396$); the mean difference was driven by three phenotypes (mannitol $+0.18$, galactose $+0.16$, sucrose $+0.14$) and was not statistically significant overall (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.15$), with both filters improving on the no-filter baseline ($0.64$, $n\approx 534$) without surpassing the GapMind rule-based baseline of $0.69$.
Because GapMind's pathway-completeness rule is conservative and biased towards false negatives, recall most directly reflects the value added by the ML filters over the curated baseline; per-phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C), concordance-trained ML exceeded GapMind recall on 12 of 15 phenotypes (mean $\Delta$ recall $+0.056$, paired Wilcoxon one-sided $p = 0.015$), while the mechanism-free filter did not show a comparable advantage ($p = 0.36$), and both filters lost recall on glycerol and galacturonic acid.
The mechanism-free filter therefore offer practical alternatives when GapMind is not applicable, while concordance training remains preferable when a mechanistic predictor is available because of its significant recall advantage over GapMind.

We also compared models trained on combined GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST features against models trained on phenotype-filtered GapMind features (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature filtering had no effect under random holdout but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.004$, BH-adjusted $q=0.01$), consistent with prior reports that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive sets for generalisation~\cite{Li2023-up}, primarily by raising cross-dataset recall.
Deploying any of these specialists on a new genome, however, still requires a per-prediction signal of when the model is operating within its applicability domain.

\subsection*{Model confidence defines an applicability domain and prioritises follow-up experiments}

Because the specialist's advantage averages out on the unstratified held-out test set (concordant+discordant; Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C,D), the per-prediction reliability signal must be derivable without the experimental phenotype. We therefore explored whether a model can flag which of its own predictions to trust using only information available at inference time.
Prediction confidence ($\max(p,\,1-p)$) ranked correct above incorrect predictions for both models, though only modestly when pooled across phenotypes (ROC AUC $0.65$ concordant, $0.62$ full-data).
Per-phenotype AUC ranged from $0.53$ for glycerol to $0.73$--$0.78$ for the best-ranked phenotypes, and both models were overconfident under cross-dataset shift (ECE $= 0.12$ and $0.18$ for the full-data and concordant models, respectively; Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B; Supplementary Text~S11). This is consistent with prior reports that boosted classifiers tend to be miscalibrated~\cite{NiculescuMizil2005Calibration}, so confidence is useful for ranking and abstention but not as a calibrated probability.
Abstaining on the least-confident predictions raised retained-subset balanced accuracy for phenotypes the models already predict well (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A): for histidine, abstention lifted concordant-model balanced accuracy from $0.76$ to $0.84$ at half coverage, defining a practical applicability domain~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject}.

At the phenotype level, no label-free signal predicted which phenotypes a model would handle reliably: neither mean prediction confidence nor feature-space novelty correlated with cross-dataset balanced accuracy across the 15 phenotypes (Spearman $\rho$ between $-0.17$ and $0.05$, all $p > 0.3$).
Because per-phenotype reliability cannot be certified in advance, we asked instead which additional measurements would most improve the concordant model.
In a retrospective simulation, 25 held-out genome labels were added to the concordant training set and the model retrained (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C).
Selecting the least-confident genomes improved transfer the most, exceeding random selection (mean $\Delta$ balanced accuracy $0.09$ vs $0.07$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.001$), whereas selecting the most novel or diverse genomes did not beat random~\cite{Vasanthakumari2024ActiveLearning}.
Because the labelled candidates and the evaluation samples are drawn from the same held-out dataset, the random-selection baseline of $0.07$ already captures the gain from the model's first exposure to that dataset, and low-confidence selection improves performance further by preferring the most informative candidates (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C--D).
The advantage concentrated on glucose, the lowest-performing phenotype among the three shown in Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D, where low-confidence selection roughly doubled the gain from random labelling ($0.24$ vs $0.13$), while the already-reliable m-inositol and histidine gained little from either strategy.
Model confidence therefore provides a deployment-oriented ranking signal, enriching for predictions suitable for selective reporting and for experiments likely to improve the model.
