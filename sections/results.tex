\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate genotype--phenotype prediction and generalisation, we harmonised four binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{BiologDatasetCitation}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus, unpublished and released with this work) covering diverse strains (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}) on various carbon sources (819 input strain records, 795 retained phenotype records, and 780 feature-linked strains eligible for analysis; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dataset_overview}).
Genomes were annotated with RAST, KOFAM, and GapMind, and classifiers were trained on the resulting feature matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A).
Analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B); Populus was excluded from selected feature-stability comparisons because of small sample size.
We evaluated the analysis variants and balanced-accuracy ranges summarised in the workflow overview (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}C).


To establish baselines, we evaluated GapMind, a mechanistic tool predicting growth from pathway completeness~\cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and a phylogeny-based nearest-neighbour classifier ($k=3$; Methods).
GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $0.66 \pm 0.04$; permissive: $0.70 \pm 0.08$; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
The phylogeny-based approach performed well in random holdout (0.72--0.87) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade: 0.46--0.63; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B), approaching null-model levels and indicating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient across diverse lineages~\cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob,Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.

\subsection*{Machine learning models predict accurately within but not across datasets}

To test whether genome-derived features could surpass these baselines, we trained gradient-boosted tree (CatBoost) classifiers on KOFAM annotations ($\sim$5500 features after correlation filtering; Methods), evaluating all models with balanced accuracy.

Data were aggregated across all four datasets and assessed under four evaluation scenarios that progressively test generalisation: repeated random holdout, cross-dataset (leave-one-dataset-out), and in-clade and out-of-clade phylogenetic splits (Methods).
Random holdout test samples were drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}); duplicate feature vectors reached only a small fraction of random-holdout predictions and left leave-one-dataset-out evaluation free of exact-vector train--test overlap (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:leakage_audit}).

In repeated random holdout evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and outperformed the GapMind baseline (paired Wilcoxon across the 15 phenotypes, $p < 0.001$).
Cross-dataset performance, however, decreased substantially (0.52--0.84; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts~\cite{Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis}.
The gap between regular and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), so phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure; the cause more likely lies in other dataset differences, such as growth-assay modality (Methods), ecology and strain history, and curation.
Consistent with this, near-identical strains conflict on their growth label far more often across datasets than within a single dataset, and the gap persists after adjusting for each dataset's positive-call rate (Supplementary Text~S12; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:label_conflict}); this inconsistency limits cross-dataset transfer but does not prevent it, since most near-identical strains still agree.
Out-of-clade counterparts showed similar trends (0.53--0.86; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with prior observations that random holdout overestimates cross-context generalisation \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation,Li2023-up}.

\subsection*{Cross-dataset failures reflect dataset-specific signal in aggregated training}

To understand these failures, we examined GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B), which can arise from incomplete annotations, alternative routes, or regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances,Gorke2008CCR}.
Phenotypes varied in GapMind accuracy and datasets varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$19\%), but neither showed a consistent directional bias, indicating that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}B).

To probe generalisation failures, we performed SHAP-based feature-importance stability analyses, grouping KOs by SHAP-supervised redundancy (Methods) so biologically equivalent features count as a single signal (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C).
Across random holdout evaluations of the aggregated dataset, at least one stable feature cluster was consistently identified, indicating recovery of recurring predictors when training data is sampled from the same pool (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C, top).
By contrast, only six of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one common stable feature cluster between models trained on the three non-held-out datasets and the held-out dataset alone (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C, bottom; per-phenotype full-data counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}).

For histidine, KOFAM annotations consistently identified the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) across datasets, illustrating that a mechanism-blind classifier can recover a genuine catabolic pathway gene when the experimental label agrees with the annotated mechanism (Supplementary Text~S4)~\cite{Bender2012HutBacteria}.
Heterogeneous training pools that mix well-characterised with mechanistically ambiguous samples are a known source of poor cross-dataset generalisation in genomic ML~\cite{Whalen2022Pitfalls}.
Together, these analyses indicate that the aggregated training set pools many distinct genotype--phenotype mechanisms, mixing tractable samples with intractable ones (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}B,C); we therefore hypothesised that restricting training to samples with internally consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would let models recover transferable mechanistic features, using GapMind concordance to isolate this subset.

\subsection*{Concordant training produces bounded specialist models}

To test this hypothesis, we restricted training to concordant samples (GapMind predictions matching experimental outcomes) while keeping the feature space, architecture, and evaluation unchanged from Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}, so any gain reflects the training data alone (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant}; Supplementary Text~S5).
Concordant samples likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways already captured in annotation databases, and may also overrepresent well-studied taxa the annotation pipeline scores most reliably, so the model is a specialist for mechanistically tractable cases rather than a recoverer of all valid utilisation strategies (a taxonomic confound we return to in the Discussion).

On held-out concordant test data (the easier subset, since discordant cases are excluded; full held-out test in Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C), the concordance-trained model reached per-phenotype balanced accuracy of 0.91--0.99 under random holdout (median 0.96) and median 0.82 under cross-dataset evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
Glucose (mean balanced accuracy 0.51) and serine (0.64) remained low (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A).
Retraining the same models directly on GapMind confidence features established a pipeline-ceiling reference (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed lines): the per-phenotype KOFAM-vs-GapMind cross-dataset gap (median balanced accuracy $0.82$ vs $0.90$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.01$) quantifies the mechanistic information lost when GapMind's curated annotations are replaced by general KOFAM annotations, which encode organism-specific enzyme and transporter identifiers less accurately; the gap was largest for glucose (GapMind 0.77 vs KOFAM 0.51) and below 0.1 for most phenotypes (Supplementary Text~S10).

Cross-dataset feature stability also improved under concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; per-phenotype counts in Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}): twelve of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one stable cluster between three-dataset and held-out-alone models, up from six under full-data training (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), and the mean per-comparison count roughly doubled (1.3 versus 0.5); a size- and class-matched random-subset control reproduced only the full-data level (0.38 $\pm$ 0.03 shared clusters), confirming the gain is specific to concordance, not subsetting (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:subset_control}).
Shared clusters often grouped established pathway genes; for histidine, the urocanate-pathway enzymes K01468 (imidazolonepropionase) and K01712 (urocanate hydratase) co-occurred within a single redundancy cluster recovered across datasets alongside the histidine-utilisation repressor K05836, with the shared cluster count rising from 4 to 8 under concordant training, consistent with the long-established co-regulation of \emph{hut} catabolic genes and their repressor as an operonic regulon~\cite{Bender2012HutBacteria} (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}, concordant-training comparison).
Held-out-alone-only features shifted towards the pathway: for histidine the unique cluster count fell from 6 to 3 while pathway concentration on map00340 rose from 17\% to 33\% under concordant training (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; the stricter KEGG module M00045 statistic is in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}), consistent with concordant training trimming dataset-idiosyncratic features while retaining pathway-relevant ones.
This concentration on pathway genes indicates that concordance-trained models recover mechanistic features, though because concordance selects samples where pathway completeness matches the phenotype, the recovery partly reflects the selection rather than an independent validation of the pathway (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}; Supplementary Text~S5).
This map-based metric undercounts recovery when KEGG separates regulators or transporters from the catabolic map: for glycerol, all off-pathway shared clusters are glp-regulon components despite 0\% formal residency (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}).

For alanine, mannose, and serine, concordant cross-dataset accuracy was instead driven by broader carbohydrate-utilisation markers, a less specific but still in-domain mode of transfer (Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Text~S7).

To quantify what the concordance-trained KOFAM model adds over GapMind, we evaluated the full held-out test set (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C) and decomposed its cross-dataset portion by concordance status (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).
Under random holdout the ML model exceeded GapMind on 14 of 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$; mean $+0.05$), but under cross-dataset evaluation the mean shift was undetectable across 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.85$, BH-adjusted $q = 0.87$), because per-phenotype gains were offset by losses where the model lost signal relative to the mechanistic call (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C).
Per-phenotype sample-level tests identify the trade-off: sensitivity gains were matched by significantly lower specificity for 8 of 15 phenotypes, so the components largely cancel in balanced accuracy (Supplementary Text~S10; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_phenotype_mcnemar}).
The decomposition explains the dilution: inside the applicability domain the ML model reached median balanced accuracy 0.82 (largely due to the residual KOFAM-vs-GapMind feature-resolution gap), rather than a gain over GapMind, which sits at $x = 1$ by construction (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).
Outside this domain, the ML model still reached 0.36, recovering cryptic catabolic capacity (growth despite an incomplete annotated pathway) about twice as often as it corrected present-but-silent pathways (per-phenotype 44\% versus 19\%, paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$, BH-adjusted $q = 0.036$, higher in 12 of 15 phenotypes; Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:rescue_rates}).
The concordance-trained ML advantage over GapMind is therefore real but bounded: false-negative rescue disproportionately involved metabolic generalists distinguished from GapMind-negative non-growers by broad-specificity carbohydrate transporters and sugar-catabolism regulators rather than substrate-specific catabolic enzymes; transport was the step GapMind most often could not place (9 of 15 phenotypes; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:rescue_rates}).

For histidine, concordance-trained performance saturated well below the available sample count (Supplementary Text~S6; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sample_size_histidine}); broader phenotype-specific requirements remain to be established.
As a complementary data-volume test, adding BacDive (over 8000 genomes~\cite{Schober2025BacDive}) did not significantly exceed concordance filtering of a much smaller curated set and gave less reproducible feature selection (Supplementary Text~S11; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:bacdive_curation}).
Concordance filtering, however, removes approximately 30\% of samples and requires a mechanistic predictor available only for well-characterised phenotypes, motivating tests of mechanism-free alternatives that use only phylogenetic agreement and experimental labels (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B; Supplementary Text~S9).

\subsection*{Mechanism-free confidence filtering captures part of the concordance gain}

To test whether the cross-dataset gain requires GapMind at the filtering step, we evaluated a soft-label confidence filter combining phylogenetic $k$-NN agreement, GapMind pathway completeness, and the experimental label, sweeping the GapMind contribution from $w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0$ (mechanism-free) to $0.5$ (Methods; Supplementary Text~S9).
A small number of problematic genomes (Methods) accounted for a disproportionate share of misclassifications and were used for additional filtering (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A).
Across the sweep, mean cross-dataset balanced accuracy on the full held-out test set was essentially flat (all four settings within $0.02$), but mean recall rose monotonically from $0.76$ at $w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0$ to $0.84$ at $w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0.5$ at near-constant precision (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B); problematic-sample removal alone gave no measurable improvement.
Concordant training reached the highest mean balanced accuracy ($0.68$) and the mechanism-free filter reached $0.647$ at a similar data cost (Supplementary Text~S9); the mean difference was not statistically significant (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.28$), and both filters improved on the no-filter baseline ($0.646$) without surpassing the GapMind rule-based baseline of $0.69$ (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B).
Because GapMind's pathway-completeness rule is conservative and biased towards false negatives, recall most directly reflects the value added by the ML filters over the curated baseline; per-phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C), concordance-trained ML exceeded GapMind recall on 11 of 15 phenotypes, though the mean shift across phenotypes was not significant (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.095$), while the mechanism-free filter showed no such trend ($p = 0.56$).
That mean averages a bimodal pattern: per phenotype, concordance-trained ML recovered significantly more true positives than GapMind for 9 of 15, led by mannose ($+0.17$), and significantly fewer for 4, most severely galacturonic acid ($-0.23$) and glycerol ($-0.19$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_phenotype_mcnemar}).

As a complementary test, phenotype-filtered GapMind features (without correlation filtering; Supplementary Text~S5) matched combined GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST features under random holdout but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.001$, BH-adjusted $q=0.003$); the combined-feature models still concentrated on curated GapMind terms, consistent with prior reports favouring biologically relevant over comprehensive feature sets~\cite{Li2023-up} (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).

\subsection*{Prediction confidence defines an applicability domain and prioritises follow-up experiments}

Because the specialist's advantage averages out on the unstratified held-out test set (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C,D), a per-prediction reliability signal must be derivable without the experimental phenotype; we therefore asked whether a model can flag which of its own predictions to trust from information available at inference time.
Prediction confidence modestly ranked correct above incorrect predictions when pooled across phenotypes (ROC AUC $0.66$ concordance-trained, $0.63$ full-data); risk--coverage curves show how this translated into retained-subset performance for three representative phenotypes (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A).
Both models were overconfident under cross-dataset shift (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B; Supplementary Text~S10); consistent with prior reports~\cite{NiculescuMizil2005Calibration}, prediction confidence therefore supports ranking and abstention, not calibrated probability estimates.
Abstaining on the least-confident predictions raised retained-subset balanced accuracy for phenotypes the models already predict well (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A): for histidine, abstention lifted the concordance-trained model's balanced accuracy from $0.76$ to $0.84$ at half coverage, defining a practical applicability domain~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject}.

At the phenotype level, the confidence-derived summaries did not predict which phenotypes a model would handle reliably: neither mean prediction confidence nor the fraction of high-confidence predictions correlated with cross-dataset balanced accuracy (Supplementary Text~S8).
Because per-phenotype reliability cannot be certified in advance, we asked instead which additional measurements would most improve the concordance-trained model.
In a retrospective simulation, 25 held-out genome labels were added to the concordant training set and the model retrained (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C; Supplementary Text~S8).
Selecting the least-confident genomes improved transfer the most, exceeding random selection (mean $\Delta$ balanced accuracy $0.09$ vs $0.07$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.002$), whereas selecting the most novel or diverse genomes did not beat random~\cite{Vasanthakumari2024ActiveLearning}.
The advantage concentrated on glucose, the lowest performer of the three shown, where low-confidence selection roughly doubled the gain from random labelling, while the already-reliable m-inositol and histidine gained little from either strategy (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D).
Prediction confidence therefore provides a deployment-oriented ranking signal for selective reporting and prioritising experiments.
