\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset Compilation and Characterization}

We assembled four distinct binary growth datasets to capture microbial nutrient utilization across diverse phylogenetic lineages and ecological contexts.
The ATLeaf dataset comprised 224 bacterial strains tested across 45 carbon substrates using minimal media on agar plates, with growth assessed through colony counting.
The Literature dataset encompassed 362 bacterial genomes with growth data across 64 carbon sources compiled from published studies spanning multiple decades and employing diverse experimental methodologies.
The Marine dataset contained high-throughput growth profiling of 186 marine heterotrophic bacteria assessed across 135 carbon substrates through optical density measurements.
The PMI dataset included metabolic characterization of 55 bacterial isolates across 191 carbon sources using MOPS minimal media supplemented with individual test compounds.
After filtering for complete annotations and phenotypic data, our final integrated dataset contained growth profiles for 810 unique microbial genomes across 242 carbon sources, providing a comprehensive foundation for comparative analysis of metabolic prediction approaches.

The phylogenetic distribution of strains spanned major bacterial classes including Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, Actinomycetia, Bacilli, and Bacteroidia, with representation from diverse ecological niches ranging from marine environments to terrestrial soils and host-associated microbiomes.
The taxonomic composition varied substantially across datasets, with the ATLeaf collection emphasizing well-studied model organisms from Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonadaceae families, the Marine dataset focusing on obligate marine bacteria including members of the SAR11 clade and Roseobacter group, and the Literature compilation encompassing broader phylogenetic breadth including representatives from Firmicutes and Actinobacteria.
This phylogenetic heterogeneity across datasets provided an opportunity to assess how models trained on one taxonomic distribution perform when applied to different phylogenetic contexts.

For our primary analyses, we focused on 16 carbon sources that were measured consistently across multiple datasets, enabling direct cross-dataset validation.
These compounds included amino acids such as alanine, arginine, histidine, and serine, which serve as both nitrogen and carbon sources and require complex catabolic pathways involving multiple enzymatic steps and regulatory mechanisms.
The sugar substrates included fructose, galactose, glucose, maltose, mannose, and sucrose, representing both monosaccharides and disaccharides with varying degrees of pathway complexity and evolutionary conservation.
Additional compounds included glycerol and mannitol as polyol carbon sources, myo-inositol as a cyclic polyol with specialized degradation pathways, galacturonic acid as a sugar acid derived from pectin degradation, and cellobiose as a beta-linked glucose disaccharide from cellulose breakdown.
The phenotypic distribution varied considerably across these substrates, with glucose and fructose showing predominantly positive growth outcomes reflecting their roles as preferred carbon sources in most bacteria, while specialized compounds such as myo-inositol and galacturonic acid exhibited more balanced distributions between growth and no-growth phenotypes consistent with their restricted utilization by bacteria possessing specific catabolic gene clusters.

We processed all bacterial genomes through a standardized annotation pipeline utilizing multiple complementary resources to capture diverse aspects of metabolic potential. We generated feature representations from KOFAM for enzyme-level annotations, providing detailed characterization of individual catalytic functions through assignment of KEGG Orthology identifiers based on hidden Markov model profiles with stringent score thresholds.
RAST subsystem annotations provided coarse-grained functional categories organized hierarchically into metabolic subsystems, cellular processes, and information processing modules. KOFAM modules offered pathway completeness scores quantifying the proportion of genes present for multi-step metabolic pathways, enabling assessment of whether organisms possessed complete functional routes rather than isolated enzymatic activities. UniRef clusters at multiple identity thresholds provided protein family assignments capturing functional homologs across diverse sequence space, with UniRef90 emphasizing recent evolutionary relationships and UniRef50 capturing deeper homology across distantly related organisms.
This multi-source annotation strategy was designed to mitigate the limitations of any single annotation approach, including database incompleteness, homology detection sensitivity thresholds, and taxonomic biases in reference sequence collections.


\section*{Limitations of Mechanism-Based and Phylogeny-Based Prediction Approaches}

To establish baseline predictive performance and demonstrate the need for machine learning approaches capable of learning complex genotype-phenotype relationships, we first evaluated two knowledge-driven methods: mechanism-based pathway analysis and phylogenetic inference. We employed GapMind, a tool that predicts catabolic potential through comparative genomics and pathway completeness analysis by integrating expert-curated pathway rules with sensitive homology detection. GapMind evaluates the presence and completeness of genes required for over 450 distinct catabolic pathways spanning approximately 400 carbon sources, assigning confidence scores based on the quality of identified pathway components including exact matches to characterized enzymes, close homologs with conserved catalytic residues, and more distant homologs requiring experimental validation. The tool implements pathway-specific rules accounting for known alternative routes, optional pathway steps, and organism-specific variations documented in the biochemical literature.
We tested GapMind predictions using two confidence thresholds to assess the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. The strict criterion required identification of complete pathways where all essential enzymatic steps were present with high-confidence homology to characterized proteins, ensuring that organisms possessed the minimal enzymatic complement necessary for carbon source catabolism based on current biochemical knowledge. The more permissive criterion included both complete pathways and likely complete pathways where one or two non-essential components showed lower-confidence matches or where alternative enzymatic routes might compensate for apparently missing genes. This permissive approach aimed to account for the inevitable incompleteness of genome annotations and the possibility that functional orthologs exist with sequence divergence exceeding standard detection thresholds.
Using the strict complete pathway criterion, GapMind achieved an average balanced accuracy of 0.650 across the 16 common carbon sources, with performance varying substantially across individual phenotypes. Glucose and fructose utilization showed relatively high accuracy near 0.80, consistent with the well-characterized and highly conserved nature of glycolytic pathways and hexose phosphorylation systems. In contrast, complex amino acids such as arginine and histidine showed lower accuracy near 0.55, reflecting greater pathway diversity and incomplete characterization of alternative degradation routes in the literature. When we relaxed the threshold to include likely complete pathways, average balanced accuracy improved modestly to 0.695 across all phenotypes. However, this improvement was not uniform, with certain carbon sources including proline and serine showing decreased accuracy under the more permissive criterion due to increased false positive predictions where genes present in the genome did not confer functional catabolic capability due to regulatory repression, missing cofactors, or context-dependent pathway requirements.
These results establish an important quantitative benchmark demonstrating that even with expert-curated pathway knowledge integrating decades of biochemical research, multiple published pathway databases including KEGG and MetaCyc, and careful comparative genomics employing sensitive homology detection methods, mechanism-based approaches achieve only modest predictive accuracy. The substantial gap between predicted and observed phenotypes indicates that either genome annotations remain systematically incomplete with many functional genes failing to match reference sequences at detectable similarity thresholds, pathway knowledge fails to capture the full complexity of regulatory mechanisms and contextual factors controlling pathway expression and activity, or experimental phenotypic measurements contain significant noise from technical variability, inconsistent growth conditions, or subjective interpretation of growth outcomes. The balanced accuracy of approximately 0.70 leaves substantial room for improvement, motivating the development of data-driven machine learning approaches that might identify predictive genomic signatures beyond those captured in curated pathway databases.
We also evaluated phylogeny-based prediction as an alternative baseline approach leveraging the principle that closely related organisms tend to share metabolic capabilities due to vertical inheritance of core metabolic genes. Using a nearest neighbor classifier implemented on a phylogenetic tree constructed from GTDB taxonomy employing concatenated ribosomal protein sequences, we assigned each test genome the phenotype of its phylogenetically closest training genome based on taxonomic distance. This approach achieved remarkably high accuracy within datasets under random data splitting strategies, with balanced accuracy exceeding 0.85 for most phenotypes in the ATLeaf dataset and approaching 0.90 for highly conserved traits such as glucose and sucrose utilization. These results confirm that phylogenetic relationships provide strong predictive signals when test organisms are phylogenetically similar to training organisms within homogeneous experimental collections employing consistent measurement protocols.
However, when we tested this phylogenetic approach across different datasets or on phylogenetically distant strains through out-of-clade partitioning that ensured test organisms belonged to different taxonomic orders than any training organisms, performance declined substantially to approximately 0.60 balanced accuracy, barely exceeding the performance of mechanism-based approaches and approaching random prediction for some phenotypes. The magnitude of performance degradation proved particularly severe for the phylogenetically diverse Literature dataset where taxonomic sampling was sparse with large phylogenetic distances between many organism pairs, resulting in nearest neighbor assignments based on relatively distant evolutionary relationships. This pattern confirms that while phylogenetic proximity serves as a useful proxy for metabolic similarity within narrow evolutionary and ecological contexts, phylogenetic approaches offer limited utility for predicting phenotypes in novel taxa separated by substantial evolutionary distance from characterized organisms or across diverse experimental contexts where technical variation obscures the phylogenetic signal. The failure of phylogenetic approaches to generalize beyond their training distributions motivates the development of feature-based machine learning methods that can potentially learn mechanistic relationships from genomic content independent of evolutionary proximity.


\section*{Machine Learning Pipeline and Feature Integration}

To leverage comprehensive genomic annotations while addressing the fundamental limitations of both mechanism-based approaches constrained by incomplete knowledge and phylogeny-based approaches unable to generalize beyond training distributions, we developed a standardized machine learning pipeline centered on gradient-boosted decision trees. This algorithm choice was motivated by several considerations including robust performance across diverse feature types without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning, ability to capture non-linear relationships and complex interactions between genomic features, computational efficiency enabling training across hundreds of phenotypes, and built-in feature importance metrics facilitating biological interpretation of learned patterns. Gradient-boosted trees have demonstrated strong performance across diverse biological prediction tasks and provide a rigorous baseline for assessing whether machine learning can extract predictive signal beyond that captured by mechanism-based or phylogenetic approaches.
Our pipeline began with construction of annotation-specific feature representations derived from multiple genomic annotation sources. We converted each annotation type to binary presence-absence matrices indicating whether each genome possessed each annotated feature, with the exception of KOFAM modules which retained pathway completeness scores as continuous features ranging from zero for pathways with no identified components to one for pathways with all required genes present. These continuous completeness scores captured the distinction between organisms possessing complete functional pathways versus those with only partial pathway fragments that might not confer phenotypic capability. The resulting feature matrices proved high-dimensional with KOFAM enzyme annotations generating approximately 7,000 features corresponding to distinct KO identifiers, RAST subsystems producing roughly 500 features representing functional categories, UniRef90 clusters yielding over 2 million features corresponding to protein families at 90\% sequence identity, and UniRef50 providing approximately 1 million features at the more permissive 50\% identity threshold.
To reduce dimensionality and remove features providing minimal information or introducing computational burden without predictive benefit, we applied systematic filtering strategies. Variance filtering removed features with minimal variation across genomes, specifically eliminating features present in fewer than 5\% or more than 95\% of genomes since such features provide limited ability to discriminate between growth and no-growth phenotypes. Correlation-based filtering eliminated highly collinear features by calculating pairwise Pearson correlations across all feature pairs and removing one feature from each pair showing correlation exceeding 0.90, preferentially retaining features with higher marginal association with phenotypes based on univariate statistical tests. These filtering steps reduced computational requirements while removing redundant information that could lead to overfitting without sacrificing predictive performance.
For our combined feature representation integrating information from all annotation sources, we implemented a systematic feature selection process designed to identify the most informative features from each annotation type while maintaining computational tractability. We trained preliminary Random Forest models separately for each annotation source and extracted feature importance scores quantifying each feature's contribution to prediction accuracy measured through mean decrease in Gini impurity across decision tree splits. We selected the top 5,000 features from each annotation source based on these importance rankings, then aggregated these high-importance features across sources to create a comprehensive combined representation. This integration strategy reduced the feature space from over 3 million raw features across all annotation sources to approximately 24,000 filtered features in the combined representation, with contributions varying by annotation source reflecting differences in both the original feature space sizes and the information content provided by each annotation approach.
UniRef30 clusters at 30\% sequence identity provided the largest number of features in the combined representation with approximately 12,000 features, reflecting the fine-grained resolution of distantly related protein families captured at this permissive identity threshold. KOFAM enzyme annotations contributed approximately 6,000 features representing well-characterized catalytic functions. RAST subsystems and KOFAM modules each contributed approximately 2,000 features capturing pathway-level organization. UniProt TrEMBL contributed the fewest features at approximately 2,000, reflecting its focus on characterized proteins with experimental evidence. The resulting combined feature matrix captured complementary information from different annotation perspectives, with enzyme-level annotations providing detailed catalytic function information, subsystem annotations capturing broader organizational principles, module completeness scores quantifying pathway intactness, and protein family clusters identifying functional homologs that might lack formal pathway assignments in curated databases.

\section*{Strong Within-Dataset Performance Masks Generalization Failures}

Initial model training on individual datasets using stratified 5-fold cross-validation demonstrated strong predictive performance for the majority of phenotypes, establishing that genomic features contain substantial information about metabolic capabilities. Models trained on the relatively homogeneous ATLeaf dataset consistently achieved balanced accuracy exceeding 0.85 across most phenotypes, with several phenotypes including glucose, mannose, and sucrose reaching accuracies above 0.90. The high performance in this dataset reflects both the standardized experimental protocols employed for phenotype measurement and the phylogenetic focus on well-studied model organisms with high-quality genome assemblies and comprehensive functional annotations. In contrast, the more phylogenetically diverse Marine and Literature datasets yielded balanced accuracies ranging from 0.75 to 0.90 depending on the specific phenotype, with the broader phylogenetic sampling and greater experimental heterogeneity introducing additional variation that slightly reduced achievable accuracy within datasets.
Across all three training datasets, metabolic features derived from KOFAM enzyme annotations showed marginally superior average performance compared to other single annotation sources, with mean balanced accuracy approximately 0.02 to 0.03 higher than RAST subsystems or UniRef clusters. This modest advantage likely reflects the direct mechanistic relevance of enzyme-level annotations for metabolic phenotypes, as specific catalytic functions represent proximate determinants of catabolic capability. However, no single annotation source demonstrated systematic superiority across all phenotypes, with performance rankings varying substantially across different carbon sources. For example, RAST subsystems performed comparably to KOFAM for complex multi-gene systems such as amino acid biosynthesis pathways, while UniRef clusters occasionally outperformed both for phenotypes involving enzymes with high sequence diversity across taxa where family-level clustering captured functional relationships that individual enzyme annotations missed.
Most models substantially outperformed null model baselines constructed through label permutation that destroyed the relationship between genomic features and phenotypes while maintaining class balance and feature distributions. The typical performance gap between trained models and permutation baselines exceeded 0.30 balanced accuracy, providing strong evidence that genomic features contained meaningful predictive signal genuinely associated with phenotypic outcomes rather than models simply memorizing spurious correlations or exploiting class imbalance. These within-dataset results suggested that machine learning successfully captured relationships between genomic content and metabolic capabilities, motivating evaluation of whether these learned relationships would generalize beyond training datasets.
However, cross-dataset validation revealed severe limitations in model generalizability that were completely masked by within-dataset evaluation metrics. When we trained classifiers on one dataset and evaluated them on a different dataset for the same phenotype, predictive accuracy declined dramatically relative to within-dataset benchmarks, often approaching random performance near 0.50 balanced accuracy. Models trained on ATLeaf and tested on the Literature dataset for the same phenotypes showed mean balanced accuracy reductions of approximately 0.15 compared to within-ATLeaf evaluation, with certain phenotypes experiencing even more severe degradation. Glucose utilization accuracy dropped from 0.89 within ATLeaf to 0.52 when tested on Literature, representing a decline to near-random performance. Glycerol showed similar patterns with within-dataset accuracy of 0.86 declining to 0.54 for cross-dataset prediction. Fructose, galactose, and several amino acid phenotypes exhibited comparable catastrophic failures of generalization.
The Marine dataset exhibited similar patterns when used as either training or test data, with cross-dataset predictions consistently underperforming intra-dataset validation scores by 0.12 to 0.20 balanced accuracy. Training on Marine and testing on ATLeaf yielded mean accuracy reductions of 0.13, while the reverse direction showed reductions of 0.16, indicating that generalization failures were bidirectional rather than specific to particular source or target datasets. The asymmetry in performance decline between training-test dataset pairs likely reflects differences in phylogenetic composition, sample size, and experimental noise levels across datasets, with generalization proving more difficult when test datasets contained phylogenetically novel organisms or more challenging phenotypic measurements.
Notably, only select phenotypes maintained relatively robust cross-dataset generalizability despite the overall pattern of failure. Myo-inositol utilization showed remarkably consistent performance across training and test dataset combinations, with accuracy reductions limited to 0.05 to 0.08 when moving from within-dataset to cross-dataset evaluation. Mannose utilization similarly maintained cross-dataset accuracy within 0.08 of within-dataset performance. These exceptional phenotypes share characteristics that likely contribute to their unusual generalizability, including highly conserved catabolic pathways with limited alternative routes documented in the biochemical literature, relatively simple pathway architectures requiring fewer enzymatic steps compared to complex amino acid degradation routes, and strong sequence conservation of key pathway enzymes enabling reliable homology-based annotation across diverse taxa.
This dramatic performance gap between within-dataset evaluation and cross-dataset validation scenarios provides compelling evidence that models exploit dataset-specific patterns that fail to transfer across experimental collections. These non-transferable patterns may include systematic differences in experimental protocols such as incubation duration, medium composition, and inoculum preparation affecting the detectability of growth phenotypes. Technical variation in growth measurement approaches, including visual inspection on solid media versus turbidity measurements in liquid cultures versus automated metabolic profiling, introduces dataset-specific response patterns that models learn to exploit. Phylogenetic composition differences across datasets enable models to use phylogenetic markers predictive within homogeneous collections but uninformative across diverse taxonomic samplings. Finally, annotation quality variation across genome collections, with some datasets emphasizing well-characterized model organisms possessing comprehensive annotations while others include poorly studied organisms with incomplete functional characterization, creates dataset-specific relationships between annotated features and phenotypes. The failure of cross-dataset generalization demonstrates conclusively that standard within-dataset cross-validation provides inflated performance estimates that do not reflect real-world model utility when applied to new data sources from different experimental contexts or phylogenetic compositions.

\section*{Phylogenetic Distance and Dataset Biases Independently Impair Generalization}

To systematically disentangle the relative contributions of phylogenetic relationships versus dataset-specific technical biases to the severe generalization failures documented in cross-dataset validation, we implemented three distinct train-test partitioning strategies within individual datasets. Random splits served as the baseline, approximating standard k-fold cross-validation by randomly assigning genomes to training and test sets while maintaining stratification to preserve phenotypic class balance. This approach allows test genomes to be phylogenetically similar to training genomes, enabling models to exploit phylogenetic proximity for prediction. Out-of-clade splits tested performance on phylogenetically distant organisms by partitioning data such that all test set genomes belonged to different taxonomic orders than all training set genomes, ensuring substantial phylogenetic distance and preventing models from leveraging close evolutionary relationships. In-clade splits evaluated performance on phylogenetically similar organisms by ensuring test set genomes shared the same taxonomic order as training set genomes while maintaining strict separation of specific strains, allowing assessment of generalization within phylogenetic neighborhoods.
Analysis of the ATLeaf dataset using these three partitioning strategies revealed substantial performance differences driven by phylogenetic distance between training and test samples. Random splits achieved mean balanced accuracy of approximately 0.89 across the 16 phenotypes, consistent with the strong within-dataset performance documented earlier. In-clade splits, restricting test genomes to the same taxonomic orders present in training data, achieved somewhat lower performance at approximately 0.82 mean balanced accuracy, representing a decline of 0.07 relative to random splits. This modest performance reduction suggests that even within taxonomic orders, models partially rely on strain-level phylogenetic proximity for prediction, with performance declining when forced to predict novel strains within familiar clades. Most dramatically, out-of-clade splits testing on taxonomic orders completely absent from training data yielded mean balanced accuracy of approximately 0.74, representing a substantial reduction of 0.15 relative to random splits and 0.08 relative to in-clade splits.
This ordered pattern of declining performance from random to in-clade to out-of-clade splits provides direct quantitative evidence that models progressively lose accuracy as phylogenetic distance between training and test samples increases. The consistent ordering across nearly all phenotypes indicates a general phenomenon rather than phenotype-specific effects, demonstrating that phylogenetic proximity serves as a pervasive predictive signal that models exploit when available but cannot transfer when test organisms fall outside the phylogenetic range of training data. The magnitude of performance degradation proves comparable to that observed in cross-dataset validation, suggesting that phylogenetic distance represents one of the major factors limiting model generalizability.
Performance degradation proved even more pronounced in the phylogenetically diverse Literature dataset, where broader taxonomic sampling created larger phylogenetic distances between organisms and increased the challenge of out-of-clade prediction. Random splits in the Literature dataset achieved mean balanced accuracy of approximately 0.76 across phenotypes, already lower than the ATLeaf dataset due to greater phylogenetic diversity and experimental heterogeneity. In-clade splits declined to approximately 0.68, while out-of-clade splits fell to a mean of 0.58, barely exceeding the 0.50 expected from random guessing. The larger absolute performance decline in Literature compared to ATLeaf likely reflects the more diverse phylogenetic composition, with taxonomic orders in Literature being more distantly related to each other than those in the ATLeaf collection focused primarily on Proteobacteria. When training and test orders are phylogenetically distant, models trained on genomic features from one clade possess limited ability to predict phenotypes in evolutionarily divergent clades due to sequence divergence of homologous genes, presence of non-orthologous gene displacements where functionally equivalent enzymes lack detectable sequence similarity, and lineage-specific innovations in metabolic pathway organization.
The Marine dataset showed similar trends, with random split performance of approximately 0.79 declining to 0.70 for in-clade splits and 0.62 for out-of-clade splits. The consistent patterns across all three datasets establish that phylogenetic distance represents a universal factor limiting generalization, with magnitude of effect depending on the phylogenetic breadth of the specific dataset and the evolutionary distances between constituent clades.
Importantly, specific phenotypes demonstrated differential sensitivity to phylogenetic distance, revealing variation in the degree of evolutionary conservation underlying different metabolic capabilities. For myo-inositol and sucrose utilization in the ATLeaf dataset, out-of-clade performance remained comparable to in-clade performance, with differences limited to 0.03 to 0.05 balanced accuracy. This exceptional robustness to phylogenetic distance suggests that the metabolic mechanisms for utilizing these carbon sources exhibit high conservation across the represented phylogenetic spectrum, with core pathway genes maintaining sufficient sequence similarity for reliable annotation across taxonomic orders and minimal alternative pathway diversity that could create clade-specific genotype-phenotype relationships. Myo-inositol degradation through the well-characterized pathway involving myo-inositol dehydrogenase, glucuronate isomerase, and downstream catabolism of glucuronate proceeds through nearly identical enzymatic steps across diverse bacterial lineages, likely reflecting ancient evolutionary origins predating major bacterial diversification events and strong purifying selection maintaining pathway architecture due to the ecological importance of inositol utilization.
In sharp contrast, phenotypes showing large performance drops exceeding 0.20 balanced accuracy between in-clade and out-of-clade splits included glucose, fructose, glycerol, and several amino acids. These substantial sensitivity differences likely reflect either phylogenetically variable pathway architectures where different bacterial lineages have evolved distinct enzymatic routes for catabolizing the same substrate, or the presence of clade-specific regulatory mechanisms not captured by simple gene presence-absence features. Glucose transport and phosphorylation systems exemplify this diversity, with Gram-negative bacteria typically employing phosphoenolpyruvate-dependent phosphotransferase systems while Gram-positive organisms often use ATP-dependent glucokinases, and different bacterial classes utilizing distinct glucose permease families that share minimal sequence homology despite serving equivalent physiological functions. This mechanistic diversity at the molecular level creates phylogenetically structured variation in which specific genes predict glucose utilization, preventing models trained on one phylogenetic group from accurately predicting glucose utilization in distant clades possessing functionally equivalent but genetically distinct systems.
To isolate the contribution of dataset-specific technical biases independent of phylogenetic effects, we conducted cross-dataset analysis using phylogenetically matched subsets that controlled for evolutionary distance between training and test organisms. We restricted test sets to organisms belonging to taxonomic orders that were also represented in the training dataset, ensuring that phylogenetic distances between test genomes and their nearest training genome neighbors remained comparable to those observed in within-dataset random splits. This matching strategy eliminated phylogenetic distance as a confounding variable, allowing assessment of whether performance differences between within-dataset and cross-dataset evaluation reflected solely technical and experimental variation rather than evolutionary divergence.
Even under these carefully controlled conditions with phylogenetic distance held constant, substantial performance gaps persisted between intra-dataset and cross-dataset predictions. Mean accuracy reductions of 0.10 to 0.15 balanced accuracy remained when comparing within-dataset random splits to cross-dataset predictions on phylogenetically matched subsets. This finding provides definitive evidence that experimental protocols, growth conditions, measurement variability, and other dataset-specific technical factors exert effects on model generalizability comparable in magnitude to those arising from phylogenetic distance. The independent contributions of phylogenetic and technical factors indicate that both must be addressed to achieve robust generalization, with neither factor alone accounting for the full extent of generalization failures observed when models trained on one dataset are applied to completely independent datasets.
Dataset-specific technical variation likely encompasses multiple distinct sources of systematic bias. Different experimental platforms including agar plate growth assays, liquid culture turbidity measurements, and automated metabolic profiling systems such as Biolog arrays measure growth through fundamentally different physical phenomena, with varying sensitivity thresholds for detecting weak or slow growth phenotypes. Incubation conditions including duration, temperature, aeration, and medium formulation profoundly influence which metabolic capabilities manifest as detectable growth, with some pathways requiring extended incubation to accumulate sufficient biomass or specific cofactors present only in certain medium compositions. Subjective factors in phenotype assignment, particularly for borderline growth outcomes near detection limits, introduce human observer variation that can create systematic differences in how ambiguous cases are classified across different studies. Finally, batch effects in genome sequencing and annotation pipelines introduce technical variation in the genomic features themselves, with different assembly algorithms, gene prediction tools, and annotation databases producing systematically different feature profiles for the same biological organisms that models can exploit as spurious predictive signals specific to particular data collection efforts.


\section*{Models Learn Phylogenetically Correlated Rather Than Mechanistically Relevant Features}

To assess whether machine learning models genuinely identify genes with causal mechanistic connections to phenotypes or merely learn to predict based on phylogenetic marker genes that correlate with phenotypes due to evolutionary history without playing functional roles in metabolism, we conducted comprehensive feature importance analysis comparing models trained on complete datasets versus biologically informed feature subsets. For complete models utilizing the full feature space, we examined the top 20 features ranked by SHAP importance values across all phenotypes and datasets, extracting both the mean absolute SHAP value quantifying average contribution to predictions and the consistency of importance rankings across cross-validation folds. We manually curated the biological relevance of these highly ranked features through systematic literature review and metabolic database queries, classifying each feature into one of three categories: directly mechanistic features representing enzymes that catalyze reactions in the catabolic pathway for the carbon source, indirectly mechanistic features including regulatory proteins controlling pathway expression, transporters importing the carbon source, or enzymes synthesizing required cofactors, and non-mechanistic features with no clear functional connection to the phenotype based on current biological knowledge.
Analysis revealed that models trained on full feature sets frequently prioritized genes lacking clear mechanistic connections to the phenotypes they were predicting. For glucose utilization, a phenotype with exceptionally well-characterized biochemistry spanning glycolysis and glucose import systems documented in thousands of publications, top-ranked features across datasets included genes involved in flagellar assembly and chemotaxis, cell wall peptidoglycan biosynthesis, general stress response systems, and various housekeeping functions in addition to the expected glycolytic enzymes and glucose transporters. While some non-metabolic genes might plausibly play indirect regulatory roles or reflect growth-phase dependent expression patterns that correlate with glucose utilization capability, the majority showed no documented connection to glucose metabolism in the literature. For histidine catabolism, models identified various aminotransferases involved in general amino acid metabolism, ammonia assimilation genes, and nitrogen regulatory proteins alongside the bona fide histidine utilization pathway components including histidine ammonia-lyase, urocanase, imidazolonepropionase, and formimidoylglutamaminase.
Quantitative assessment of feature relevance across all phenotypes revealed that the proportion of clearly mechanistic features among the top 20 ranged from approximately 30\% for complex phenotypes with poorly characterized alternative pathways to approximately 60\% for simple, well-studied phenotypes with single dominant catabolic routes. This means that between 40\% and 70\% of the most important features identified by models lacked obvious mechanistic justification, representing instead phylogenetic markers, functionally unrelated genes that happen to co-occur with metabolic genes due to shared evolutionary history, or genes involved in general cellular processes that show frequency differences across bacterial lineages with different metabolic capabilities. The contamination of feature importance rankings by non-mechanistic genes indicates that despite achieving high within-dataset accuracy, models are not cleanly learning causal metabolic determinants but rather exploiting complex patterns of genomic feature co-occurrence shaped by phylogenetic relationships.
Furthermore, feature stability analysis demonstrated remarkably poor agreement across datasets, providing additional evidence that models learn dataset-specific associations rather than universal mechanistic principles. When we compared the top 20 features identified by models trained separately on ATLeaf and Marine datasets for identical phenotypes, calculating Jaccard similarity coefficients to quantify feature set overlap, the median similarity was only 0.12 across phenotypes. This extremely low overlap indicates that models trained on different datasets identify almost entirely different sets of genomic features as most important for predicting the same metabolic capability. Some phenotypes showed even more severe instability, with Jaccard similarities approaching zero indicating completely disjoint feature sets across datasets. Only the most highly conserved phenotypes such as myo-inositol utilization, which already demonstrated robust cross-dataset generalization, showed moderate feature stability with Jaccard similarities reaching 0.25 to 0.30.
This lack of cross-dataset consistency in identified features conclusively demonstrates that models exploit dataset-specific patterns rather than learning generalizable mechanistic relationships. If models were genuinely identifying causal metabolic genes, we would expect high overlap in top-ranked features across independent datasets, as the same enzymatic activities should be required for catabolizing specific carbon sources regardless of which bacterial strains are studied or which experimental protocols are employed for phenotype measurement. The observed instability reveals that each dataset presents a different constellation of phylogenetic relationships, technical variation patterns, and annotation biases that models learn to exploit through different combinations of features that happen to be predictive within each specific context but fail to transfer across contexts.
Within individual datasets, models achieved moderate feature stability across cross-validation folds, providing a superficially reassuring signal that models were learning reproducible patterns. For most phenotypes, at least 8 of the top 20 features appeared consistently across all five cross-validation folds, and the correlation between feature importance rankings across fold pairs typically exceeded 0.70. This within-dataset stability indicates that given a fixed phylogenetic distribution and experimental protocol, models reliably converge to similar solutions identifying the same genomic features as predictive. However, this within-dataset reproducibility combined with cross-dataset instability confirms that models reliably identify features that are predictive within a specific experimental and phylogenetic context but not genuinely causal features that would maintain predictive utility across diverse contexts. The models essentially function as sophisticated nearest-neighbor classifiers operating in high-dimensional genome feature space, learning that organisms possessing specific combinations of genes tend to exhibit specific phenotypes within training distributions while failing to capture the actual causal mechanisms that would enable prediction beyond those distributions.

\section*{Biologically Informed Feature Selection Improves Interpretability but Not Generalization}

To test whether explicitly constraining models to mechanistically relevant genes identified through biological knowledge could overcome the generalization failures observed with full feature sets, we developed both automated and manual strategies for constructing phenotype-specific biologically informed feature sets. Our automated approach leveraged the KEGG pathway database by querying with carbon source-specific keywords to retrieve associated KO identifiers, complete pathway maps showing the series of enzymatic conversions required for catabolism, and functionally related enzymes that might participate in peripheral reactions feeding into or drawing from core catabolic pathways. For each of the 15 phenotypes with well-characterized pathways documented in KEGG, this automated retrieval strategy yielded feature sets ranging from approximately 10 genes for simple pathways such as glycerol catabolism involving glycerol kinase, glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, and triose phosphate entry into glycolysis, to over 300 genes for complex amino acids with multiple alternative degradation routes and extensive peripheral metabolism. These biologically selected feature sets represented a dramatic dimensionality reduction from the full KOFAM feature space of approximately 7,000 features, focusing models specifically on genes with documented roles in the metabolism of interest.
Our manual curation approach involved intensive literature review to identify essential genes for catabolism of four representative carbon sources selected to span a range of pathway complexities and biochemical mechanisms. For histidine, we compiled the canonical five-step degradation pathway involving sequential action of histidine ammonia-lyase converting histidine to urocanate, urocanate hydratase producing imidazolonepropionate, imidazolonepropionase generating formiminoglutamate, and formimidoylglutamaminase yielding glutamate that enters central metabolism, plus associated transporters and regulatory proteins. For galactose, we identified the Leloir pathway enzymes including galactokinase, galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase, and UDP-galactose 4-epimerase that convert galactose to glucose-6-phosphate, along with galactose permeases. For glycerol, we specified glycerol facilitator proteins, glycerol kinase, and glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. For cellobiose, we included beta-glucosidases that hydrolyze the beta-1,4-glycosidic bond and cellobiose-specific phosphotransferase system components. These manually curated feature sets contained only 10 to 30 genes per phenotype, representing the minimal enzymatic complement required based on biochemical knowledge.
Models trained on these drastically reduced biologically informed feature sets maintained remarkably competitive intra-dataset performance compared to full-feature models, demonstrating that a small number of mechanistically relevant features capture most of the genuine predictive signal associated with metabolic capabilities. For the four manually curated phenotypes, balanced accuracy declined by only 0.02 to 0.05 when restricting models to 10 to 30 biologically selected genes compared to models with access to 7,000 KOFAM features. This minimal performance loss indicates that the vast majority of features in full models contribute little true predictive value, with their apparent importance reflecting exploitation of phylogenetic correlations rather than causal relationships. The ability of small, focused feature sets to nearly match full-feature performance validates the biological intuition that metabolic phenotypes depend primarily on specific pathway genes rather than requiring information from across the entire genome.
Cross-dataset performance showed modest improvements for specific dataset-phenotype combinations when training on biologically selected features compared to full feature sets. Of the 15 phenotypes tested with automated KEGG-based feature selection, 6 showed statistically significant improvements in cross-dataset balanced accuracy based on paired t-tests across the five cross-validation folds with Bonferroni-corrected p-values below 0.05. The magnitude of these improvements ranged from 0.03 to 0.08 balanced accuracy, representing meaningful but not transformative gains. Phenotypes benefiting most from biological feature selection tended to be those with relatively simple, well-characterized pathways and limited alternative routes, where focusing models on known pathway genes effectively eliminated confounding phylogenetic markers. In contrast, phenotypes with complex metabolism involving multiple alternative degradation pathways or extensive peripheral reactions showed minimal or no benefit from biological feature selection, likely because the automated KEGG queries either retrieved too many peripherally related genes that reintroduced phylogenetic confounding or missed important alternative pathway genes not captured in KEGG annotations.
However, despite these improvements for select phenotypes, cross-dataset performance remained substantially below intra-dataset benchmarks even with optimal biological feature selection. The persistent performance gaps of 0.10 to 0.18 balanced accuracy between within-dataset and cross-dataset evaluation indicate that constraining feature spaces to known pathway genes addresses only part of the generalization problem. Biological knowledge can guide models toward mechanistically plausible features and eliminate some phylogenetic confounders, but it cannot compensate for underlying data quality issues including incomplete genome annotations where relevant pathway genes fail to be identified, experimental variability where identical genotypes produce different phenotypic outcomes under different measurement protocols, or genuine biological complexity where pathway expression and activity depend on regulatory states and environmental contexts not encoded in static genomic features.
Feature importance analysis and interpretability assessment revealed substantial differences between models trained on full versus biologically selected feature sets, even when quantitative performance metrics proved similar. Models trained on biologically informed features consistently prioritized established catabolic genes in their importance rankings, with the top five features almost invariably corresponding to core enzymatic steps in the canonical degradation pathways. For histidine utilization, all five cross-validation folds identified imidazolonepropionase, urocanate hydratase, and formimidoylglutamate deiminase among the top five features, with histidine ammonia-lyase and histidine transporters completing the top rankings. For cellobiose, beta-glucosidase and cellobiose-specific phosphotransferase system components consistently dominated importance rankings. For glycerol, the three core pathway genes comprising glycerol facilitator, glycerol kinase, and glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase consistently appeared as the top-ranked features with substantially higher SHAP importance values than any other features.
Cross-dataset feature stability improved dramatically when restricting models to biologically informed features compared to full feature sets, providing quantitative evidence that biological knowledge guides models toward more generalizable mechanistic features. Jaccard similarity coefficients for the top 20 features identified by models trained on ATLeaf versus Marine datasets increased from a median of 0.12 for full-feature models to 0.45 for biologically informed models across the phenotypes examined. For some phenotypes such as glycerol and galactose with especially simple and well-characterized pathways, Jaccard similarities reached 0.60 to 0.65, indicating substantial overlap in which specific genes models identified as most important despite being trained on completely independent datasets. This improved stability suggests that when feature spaces are constrained to mechanistically plausible genes, models are more likely to identify the same causal determinants rather than dataset-specific phylogenetic markers.
Despite these substantial gains in interpretability, feature stability, and modest improvements in generalization for select phenotypes, biologically informed feature selection failed to resolve the fundamental generalizability problem affecting the majority of phenotypes. The persistent inability to achieve robust cross-dataset generalization despite constraining models to known pathway genes suggests that factors beyond feature selection play critical limiting roles. These factors likely include systematic differences in which pathway genes are successfully annotated across different genome collections, with some datasets emphasizing model organisms possessing comprehensive manually curated annotations while others rely on automated pipelines that miss difficult-to-detect homologs. Experimental measurement variability introduces noise in phenotype labels that obscures even genuine genotype-phenotype relationships when models are tested on data from different experimental platforms. Finally, genuine biological complexity including context-dependent gene regulation, requirement for pathway co-expression that static genomic features cannot capture, and dependence on growth conditions that vary across datasets means that even perfect knowledge of relevant genes may be insufficient for prediction without additional information about regulatory states and environmental contexts. These results indicate that while biological knowledge provides valuable constraints that improve model interpretability and partially address phylogenetic confounding, achieving robust generalization requires not just better feature selection but fundamentally higher-quality training data where genotype-phenotype relationships are clear and reliable.


\section*{Aggregating Datasets and Integrating Multiple Annotation Sources Provide Incremental Benefits}

To mitigate phylogenetic and dataset-specific biases through increased training diversity, we explored two complementary strategies for enhancing model inputs and training distributions. First, we aggregated datasets by combining ATLeaf and Marine collections to increase both the total number of training samples and the phylogenetic breadth of the training distribution. This aggregation strategy yielded training sets containing 300 to 400 genomes for phenotypes measured in both datasets, substantially exceeding the 150 to 200 genomes available in either dataset individually. The combined training distribution spanned a broader phylogenetic range encompassing both terrestrial Proteobacteria and marine specialists, potentially enabling models to learn metabolic patterns that generalize across diverse ecological and evolutionary contexts rather than patterns specific to narrow phylogenetic or environmental niches.
Models trained on these aggregated datasets showed significantly improved cross-dataset prediction performance compared to single-dataset models for 6 of 15 phenotypes tested, with improvements ranging from 0.05 to 0.12 balanced accuracy when evaluated on held-out datasets not included in training. These phenotypes included galactose, glycerol, mannose, alanine, serine, and galacturonic acid, which collectively represented both simple and moderately complex pathways. The mechanisms underlying these improvements likely involve increased phylogenetic diversity in training enabling models to encounter and learn from a broader range of gene-phenotype associations, larger sample sizes providing more statistical power to identify genuine signals while averaging over phylogenetically specific noise, and exposure to multiple experimental protocols during training potentially helping models learn features robust to technical variation rather than protocol-specific artifacts.
For the remaining 9 phenotypes, aggregated models performed comparably to single-dataset models, with performance differences smaller than 0.03 balanced accuracy falling within the range of cross-validation variance. Importantly, we observed no systematic performance penalties from aggregation across any phenotypes, indicating that combining diverse data sources provides benefits or neutrality without introducing detrimental artifacts that would cause performance degradation. This finding validates dataset aggregation as a generally safe strategy that at worst maintains performance equivalent to single-dataset training while offering potential improvements for a substantial fraction of phenotypes. The phenotypes showing minimal benefit from aggregation tended to be those already exhibiting poor cross-dataset generalization with single-dataset training, suggesting that simply increasing sample size and phylogenetic diversity cannot overcome generalization barriers for phenotypes with fundamental data quality issues or genuinely context-dependent biology.
The phenotypes benefiting most from aggregation exhibited specific characteristics that help explain their particular responsiveness to this strategy. These phenotypes tended to show moderate phylogenetic conservation rather than either extreme conservation or extreme variability, falling in an intermediate regime where increased phylogenetic sampling provides valuable information about how genotype-phenotype relationships vary across clades without encountering overwhelming diversity that prevents models from learning transferable patterns. Phenotypes with moderate pathway complexity, involving multiple enzymatic steps but lacking extensive alternative routes, appeared more amenable to learning from diverse training data compared to either trivially simple phenotypes where single genes suffice for prediction or highly complex phenotypes with numerous alternative pathways creating fundamentally different genotype-phenotype landscapes across bacterial lineages.
Our second strategy involved comprehensive evaluation of whether integrating features from multiple annotation sources could enhance predictive performance by capturing complementary aspects of metabolic pathway architecture not represented in any single annotation approach. We trained models separately using each individual annotation source and using our combined feature representation integrating top features from all sources, then compared performance across 104 phenotypes spanning all four datasets to identify which representation yielded the highest balanced accuracy for each specific phenotype. This systematic comparison revealed that the combined feature representation produced the most accurate models for approximately 85 phenotypes, representing 82\% of the total evaluated. Of the remaining 19 phenotypes, KOFAM features alone performed best for 11 phenotypes, RAST subsystems for 5 phenotypes, and UniRef clusters for 3 phenotypes.
The strong overall performance of combined feature representations indicates that integrating information from multiple annotation perspectives provides synergistic benefits for the substantial majority of phenotypes, with the combined representation either matching or exceeding the best single-source performance. The integration provided greatest benefit for phenotypes where single-source models demonstrated limitations or showed high variance across cross-validation folds, suggesting that combining annotations helps stabilize predictions and compensates for weaknesses in individual annotation approaches. The mechanism underlying these synergistic effects likely involves complementary coverage of different aspects of metabolic systems, with enzyme-level annotations capturing specific catalytic functions, subsystem annotations providing information about broader pathway organization and co-regulated gene clusters, module completeness scores quantifying whether organisms possess full functional routes, and protein family clusters identifying functionally related proteins based on sequence similarity that might not receive formal functional annotations.
Individual annotation sources showed no consistent superiority across all phenotypes, with optimal annotation choice proving highly dependent on the specific metabolic pathway and organism distribution. KOFAM-based enzyme features excelled for well-characterized central metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and pentose phosphate pathways where individual enzymatic steps have been extensively studied and homologs can be reliably identified across diverse taxa through sequence similarity to characterized proteins. RAST subsystem features performed better for complex multi-gene systems such as amino acid biosynthesis pathways and vitamin synthesis routes that involve coordinated expression of numerous genes organized into functional modules, with the subsystem-level organization providing valuable information beyond individual gene presence. UniRef cluster features captured functionally similar proteins for pathways with high sequence diversity across different bacterial lineages, identifying distant homologs that might not match strict enzyme annotation thresholds but nonetheless perform equivalent catalytic functions, particularly valuable for proteins that have undergone substantial sequence divergence or convergent evolution to similar functions.
The success of multi-source integration across the majority of phenotypes supports the strategy of combining diverse annotation perspectives rather than attempting to identify a single optimal annotation approach. Different annotation sources emphasize different aspects of genomic organization and function, with no single source capturing all relevant information for the full diversity of metabolic pathways and phylogenetic contexts represented in our datasets. However, despite the clear benefits of multi-source integration and dataset aggregation demonstrated through systematic evaluation, even these optimized approaches left substantial room for improvement. Cross-dataset performance with combined features and aggregated training data remained 0.08 to 0.15 balanced accuracy below within-dataset benchmarks for most phenotypes, indicating that feature engineering and training set expansion strategies alone cannot fully resolve the underlying generalizability challenges. These persistent limitations motivated our investigation of whether data quality rather than quantity or representation fundamentally limits model performance.


\section*{High-Quality Data Enables Robust Model Generalization}

To test whether data quality fundamentally limits generalization, we restricted analysis to samples where GapMind predictions correctly matched experimental outcomes. Models trained exclusively on these concordant samples achieved near-perfect accuracy exceeding 0.95 across four representative phenotypes (glycerol, cellobiose, galactose, histidine). Remarkably, this performance was maintained across all evaluation scenarios: random within-dataset splits, cross-dataset predictions, out-of-clade phylogenetic splits, and in-clade splits. Both KOFAM-based and combined feature representations yielded nearly identical results with differences smaller than 0.02.
The contrast with complete-data models proved striking. For glycerol, complete-data models achieved 0.87 for within-dataset splits but only 0.62 for cross-dataset and 0.59 for out-of-clade evaluation, while GapMind-filtered models maintained 0.97 to 0.98 across all scenarios. Feature importance analysis revealed consistent identification of mechanistically relevant genes. For histidine, top features included imidazolonepropionase, urocanate hydratase, and formimidoylglutamate deiminase—canonical pathway components. Cross-dataset feature stability increased dramatically, with Jaccard similarity improving from 0.10-0.15 for complete-data training to 0.62-0.78 for GapMind-filtered training. These results demonstrate that machine learning algorithms fully identify mechanistically relevant features and achieve robust generalization when trained on high-quality data.

\section*{Systematic Characterization of Prediction Discordances}

The performance difference between filtered and unfiltered models motivated systematic investigation of GapMind-experiment discordances. We calculated per-genome misclassification rates across all phenotypes where GapMind made predictions. The distribution proved highly skewed with median 0.18 but substantial right-tail deviation. The top 10\% of genomes exhibited rates exceeding 0.45, while the bottom 25\% showed rates below 0.10. The 20 genomes with highest misclassification rates accounted for 31\% of all discordances despite representing only 2.4\% of the dataset.
Analysis of problematic genomes revealed consistent patterns. Five genomes showed no growth on any tested carbon source including universal substrates, suggesting viability issues or extreme growth requirements. Eight belonged to poorly characterized taxonomic groups with limited database representation and lower annotation completeness. Six exhibited unusual annotation patterns with aberrant gene counts, potentially indicating assembly quality issues or genomic contamination.
Per-phenotype analysis revealed concordance rates ranging from 0.52 for complex amino acids to 0.89 for simple sugars, with median 0.71. We classified misclassifications directionally: false positives (GapMind predicted growth, experiments showed none) comprised 58\%, while false negatives comprised 42\%. Directional bias varied substantially. Aromatic amino acids showed 68\% false negatives, suggesting alternative degradation pathways not captured in GapMind's rules. Nucleosides and complex carbohydrates showed 64\% false positives, suggesting regulatory repression or missing cofactors despite gene presence.
Dataset-specific error profiles emerged. The Literature dataset showed highest discordance at 0.34, consistent with heterogeneous compilation. ATLeaf exhibited lowest discordance at 0.22, reflecting standardized conditions and model organisms. The Marine dataset showed intermediate discordance of 0.28 with elevated false negatives for specific compounds, potentially reflecting novel metabolic capabilities in marine bacteria.

\section*{Optimizing Data Quality Through Threshold Selection}

We analyzed trade-offs between data quality and quantity across GapMind confidence thresholds. The strictest threshold retaining only complete pathways excluded 40\% to 60\% of samples but yielded the best cross-dataset and out-of-clade generalization with improvements of 0.08 to 0.15. The intermediate threshold including mostly complete pathways retained 65\% to 80\% of samples and provided improvements of 0.05 to 0.11. The permissive threshold including likely complete pathways retained 75\% to 90\% but showed reduced improvements of only 0.03 to 0.07.
Learning curve analysis established minimum sample size requirements. For phenotypes with concordant training sets exceeding 200 samples, performance plateaued at approximately 50\% of available data. For smaller sets between 50 and 100 samples, performance improved through 100\% without plateaus. Critical minimum thresholds emerged around 40 samples, below which filtering became counterproductive due to increased variance. Dataset aggregation enabled effective training for rare phenotypes by pooling high-quality samples across sources.
We tested hybrid two-stage approaches: initial training on concordant samples followed by fine-tuning on complete data. Hybrid models achieved intermediate performance between pure filtered and unfiltered approaches, outperforming complete-data models by 0.04 to 0.08 for cross-dataset evaluation but underperforming pure filtered models by 0.03 to 0.06. Feature importance showed intermediate mechanistic signal (55\% to 70\% clearly mechanistic features) compared to complete-data models (35\% to 60\%) and pure filtered models (75\% to 90\%).
Phylogenetic distance analysis revealed that test genomes in underrepresented taxonomic groups such as Deltaproteobacteria and Verrucomicrobia showed particularly large distances to concordant training samples (0.18 to 0.24 phylogenetic units versus 0.08 to 0.12 for well-represented Gammaproteobacteria), resulting in lower prediction accuracy even for filtered models.
