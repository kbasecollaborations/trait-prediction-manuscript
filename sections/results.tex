\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Dataset overview and baseline predictions}

To investigate factors governing genotype--phenotype prediction accuracy and generalisation, we harmonised four published binary growth datasets (ATLeaf \cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{BiologDatasetCitation}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{Timm2015Populus}) covering phylogenetically and ecologically diverse strains on various carbon sources (819 input genomes, 795 retained after filtering; Methods).
Genomes were annotated with RAST, KOFAM, and GapMind, classifiers were trained on the resulting feature matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}A), and analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_characteristics}B); Populus was excluded from selected feature-stability comparisons due to small sample size, and datasets differed in taxonomic coverage (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distribution}).


To establish baseline performance, we evaluated two approaches: GapMind, a mechanistic tool predicting growth from pathway completeness~\cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving}, and a phylogeny-based nearest neighbour classifier (k=3) using the three closest strains (Methods).
GapMind achieved modest balanced accuracy across the 15 shared phenotypes (strict threshold: $0.65 \pm 0.04$; permissive: $0.70 \pm 0.08$; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}A).
The phylogeny-based approach, leveraging the documented link between metabolic phenotypes and phylogeny \cite{Langille2013-fh,Martiny2013-ob}, performed well in random holdout evaluations (0.75--0.85) but poorly on phylogenetically distant samples (out-of-clade: 0.55--0.65; Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_predictions}B), approaching null model levels and indicating that phylogenetic relatedness alone is insufficient across diverse lineages~\cite{Michener2014Phylogeny,Goberna2016Predicting,CaroQuintero2015HGT}.
% Cellobiose and glycerol showed unexpectedly high out-of-clade accuracy ($>$0.80), potentially reflecting highly conserved pathways.
These limited baselines motivated testing whether machine learning over comprehensive genomic features could better capture genotype--phenotype relationships across experimental contexts.


\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Machine learning models generalise within but not across datasets}

To overcome these baseline limitations, we trained gradient-boosted tree (GBT) classifiers on KOFAM annotations ($\sim$4000--5000 features per genome after correlation filtering; Methods), evaluating all models with balanced accuracy to account for class imbalance.

Data were aggregated across all four datasets and assessed under four evaluation scenarios that progressively test generalisation: repeated random holdout (training and test drawn from the same phenotype-cluster distribution); cross-dataset (training on three datasets and testing on the held-out one); in-clade cross-dataset splits (restricting evaluation to phylogenetically similar samples to isolate dataset from phylogenetic effects); and out-of-clade splits (phylogenetically distant training and test samples).
Random holdout test samples were drawn from the same phylogenetic clades as training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

In repeated random holdout evaluations (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A), the ML models achieved high balanced accuracy (0.75--0.90) and outperformed the GapMind baseline (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank across the 15 shared phenotypes, $p < 0.001$).
Cross-dataset performance, however, decreased substantially (0.50--0.75; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B), indicating limited generalisation across experimental contexts.
The gap between regular and in-clade cross-dataset splits was minimal ($\sim$0.02; Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), indicating that phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure and that dataset-level inconsistencies likely contribute substantially.
Out-of-clade splits showed similar trends (0.50--0.80; Figure~\ref{fig:in_clade_out_clade_performance}), consistent with prior observations that random holdout evaluations overestimate generalisation by fitting phylogeny-specific patterns \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation,Li2023-up}.
Despite the expectation that well-characterised catabolic pathways would enable cross-dataset transfer \cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Gralka2023-ft}, models trained on aggregated data did not consistently learn transferable mechanistic features.


\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Misclassification patterns implicate training-data heterogeneity}

To understand these failures, we examined GapMind misclassification patterns across phenotypes and datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A,B), which can arise from incomplete annotations, alternative routes, and regulatory mechanisms~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2024Improving,Griesemer2018Combining,Pan2018Advances,Gorke2008CCR}.
Although phenotypes varied in GapMind accuracy (e.g., mannitol, galactose higher; serine, alanine lower), no consistent directional bias was observed in misclassifications, and datasets similarly varied in overall discordance (Biolog: $\sim$30\%; Populus: $\sim$26\%) without directional bias, indicating that discordance does not arise from a single dataset-specific artefact.

To probe ML generalisation failures, we performed SHAP-based feature-importance stability analyses (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C), grouping KOs by SHAP-supervised redundancy (Methods) so that biologically equivalent features are counted as a single signal.
Across random holdout evaluations of the aggregated dataset, at least one stable feature cluster was consistently identified (top panel), indicating recovery of recurring predictors when training and test samples shared distribution.
By contrast, only six of fifteen phenotypes (glucose, histidine, maltose, mannitol, sucrose, and m-inositol) shared at least one common stable feature cluster between models trained on the three non-held-out datasets and the held-out dataset alone (bottom panel).

Examining KOFAM annotations of these stable features (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}) showed that for histidine, the main catabolic enzyme K01712 (urocanate hydratase) was consistently identified across all datasets, indicating that models can recover pathway-linked features when those genes are present, consistently annotated, and clearly tied to the phenotype; other core histidine enzymes were present in dataset-specific stable features but absent from the aggregated dataset, likely due to annotation gaps or alternative pathways.
These analyses suggest the aggregated training set mixes mechanistically tractable samples with intractable ones.
We therefore hypothesised that restricting training to samples with internally consistent genotype--phenotype relationships would let models recover transferable mechanistic features, and we used GapMind concordance to stratify samples by mechanistic consistency.
\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Concordant training produces bounded specialist models}

To test this hypothesis, we defined concordant samples as those in which GapMind predictions matched experimental outcomes; these likely overrepresent organisms using conserved pathways already captured in annotation databases, and may also overrepresent well-studied taxa whose genomes the annotation pipeline can score most reliably (a taxonomic confound we return to in the Discussion).
Feature space (KOFAM), architecture, and evaluation were unchanged from Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}, decoupling the concordance criterion from the input features (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant}; Supplementary Text~S5).
Improved transfer therefore reflects better learning on a mechanistically homogeneous subset rather than recovery of all valid utilisation strategies.

Models trained on concordant samples achieved near-perfect balanced accuracy ($>$0.95) on held-out concordant test data under random holdout evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A), serving mainly as a sanity check since concordance filtering removes ambiguously-labelled cases.
Concordance-filtered cross-dataset splits, the more informative generalisation measure, also reached high accuracy ($>$0.90) on many phenotype-dataset combinations, consistent with improved transfer when retained samples are supported by pathway-linked features; glucose (mean BA 0.51) and serine (0.73) remained low despite shared stable features.
Because the learning algorithm and evaluation scheme were unchanged, these gains point to the training data as the main difference.

To establish a pipeline ceiling when features fully encode the mechanism, we retrained the same concordant models on GapMind step features (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed lines).
These models reached near-deterministic accuracy under random holdout (median 0.98; range 0.92--1.00), so the per-phenotype KOFAM gap (median cross-dataset BA $0.90$ vs $0.83$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$) quantifies the mechanistic information lost by replacing GapMind's curated annotations with general KOFAM annotations.
The largest gap was glucose (GapMind 0.77 vs KOFAM 0.51), reflecting GapMind's organism-specific transporter identifiers that lack direct KOFAM equivalents; for most phenotypes the gap was below 0.1 (Supplementary Text~S11), indicating KOFAM is typically a sufficient feature space for cross-dataset transfer, and glucose's gap is therefore a feature-resolution ceiling for the KOFAM-only model rather than a fixed property of the phenotype, addressable by additional concordant training labels (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D).

Cross-dataset stability analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B) showed a notable increase in shared stable feature clusters compared to full, unfiltered training (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C): twelve of fifteen phenotypes shared at least one cluster under concordant training, up from six under full-data training, and the mean number of shared clusters per held-out comparison roughly doubled (1.3 versus 0.5).
These shared clusters often grouped established pathway genes, for example histidine-utilisation KOs K01468 (imidazolonepropionase) and K01712 (urocanate hydratase) within a single redundancy cluster recovered across datasets (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant}).
Held-out-dataset-unique features differed qualitatively across regimes: under concordant training they were predominantly target-pathway paralogues or alternative steps, whereas under full-data training they included off-pathway annotations such as $\beta$-ketoadipate components for histidine (29\% vs 75\% of unique cluster representatives map to the canonical KEGG module M00045 for histidine; Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:feature_comparison} and~\ref{tab:feature_comparison_concordant}; Supplementary Text~S11).
For three phenotypes (alanine, mannose, serine), high concordant cross-dataset balanced accuracy was driven by broader carbohydrate-utilisation markers rather than target-pathway clusters --- a less specific mode of transfer that remains within the applicability domain (Supplementary Text~S8).

To quantify what the concordant-trained KOFAM model adds over the mechanistic GapMind baseline in deployment, we re-evaluated the same models on the full natural-composition held-out test set and compared their balanced accuracy phenotype by phenotype against the GapMind permissive baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C).
Under random holdout, the ML model exceeded GapMind on 14 of 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$; mean improvement $+0.06$), consistent with the model capturing genome-encoded information beyond GapMind's curated pathway-completeness rules when training and test draw from the same distribution.

Decomposing cross-dataset performance by GapMind-concordance status (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D) showed where this advantage holds up under stricter generalisation.
Inside the applicability domain (concordant test subset, purple), the ML model reached median balanced accuracy 0.83 --- the residual KOFAM-vs-GapMind feature-resolution gap quantified above (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed reference) --- rather than a gain over GapMind, which sits at $y = 1$ on this subset by construction.
Outside the applicability domain (discordant test subset, orange), where GapMind is wrong by construction, the ML model still reached median balanced accuracy 0.34, correctly relabelling 40\% of GapMind false negatives but only 20\% of false positives (per-phenotype rescue rates 44\% vs 19\%, paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.02$), consistent with annotation-gap discordance leaving a residual genomic signature ML can read while regulatory or context-dependent discordance does not (Supplementary Text~S11).

Averaging the two regimes diluted the advantage: cross-dataset mean balanced accuracy on the full test set showed no detectable shift from GapMind across 15 phenotypes (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.52$, BH-adjusted $q = 0.58$; with $n=15$ this is the absence of a mean difference rather than evidence of equivalence; Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C, squares), because per-phenotype gains were offset by losses on samples where the model lost signal relative to the mechanistic call (notably galacturonic acid; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:full_held_out_test}).
The practical advantage of concordant-trained ML over GapMind is therefore real but bounded, and is visible only when the test set is partitioned by applicability-domain membership.
Concordance filtering itself, however, has practical costs --- it removes approximately 30\% of samples and requires a mechanistic predictor that exists only for well-characterised phenotypes --- motivating tests of simpler quality filters that do not require an external mechanistic call.

\subsection*{Quality-based filters and pathway-restricted features partially recover the concordance gain}

As a robustness check, we tested alternative filters targeting data quality without external mechanistic predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}A): a small number of genomes accounted for a disproportionate share of misclassifications, most being microbes with zero growth across substrates despite at least one GapMind growth prediction or universal growth despite incomplete GapMind pathways.
Removing these samples and filtering by measurement confidence (Methods) improved cross-dataset performance, though less than concordant training (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B); measurement-confidence filtering removed far fewer samples but produced smaller, consistent gains, a useful alternative when concordance filtering is not possible.
Concordant-trained models showed the best precision--recall trade-off among the three filters (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C), while the GapMind baseline occupied a similar range to the two weaker filters, indicating that these recovered only part of the concordance advantage.

We also compared models trained on combined GapMind, KOFAM, and RAST features with models trained on phenotype-filtered GapMind features (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D).
Feature filtering had little effect under random holdout evaluation but improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.004$, BH-adjusted $q=0.01$), consistent with prior reports that biologically relevant features outperform comprehensive sets for generalisation \cite{Li2023-up}; phenotype-filtered features primarily improved cross-dataset recall without a large precision penalty (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D inset).
Overall, quality-based sample filtering and pathway-restricted features each recovered only part of the concordance-driven gain, narrowing but not closing the gap relative to mechanistically concordant training and confirming that the concordance-based specialist remains the strongest configuration when a mechanistic predictor is available.


\FloatBarrier{}

\subsection*{Most phenotypes saturate at 50--100 coherent training samples}

Because no simpler filter fully substituted for concordance, deploying the specialist requires obtaining enough concordant training samples for each new phenotype, and we therefore asked how many such samples are actually needed.
The learning curves for concordant-trained models on the histidine phenotype (Figure~\ref{fig:model_sample_size_requirements}) show how performance changed with training sample size under random holdout, cross-dataset, and out-of-clade evaluations.
Each panel further breaks down performance on full test samples, concordant test samples, and discordant test samples.

Across all 15 shared phenotypes, performance plateaued at approximately 50--100 concordant training samples under random holdout and cross-dataset evaluations, with only glucose and galacturonic acid requiring 200 samples and no phenotype showing a new saturation point beyond 200 (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}).
Strong generalisation is therefore achievable with modest data when genotype--phenotype mappings are internally consistent and represented in the feature space, but modest sample requirements do not guarantee high balanced accuracy: where performance remained low, the limitation lay in the strength of the underlying genotype--phenotype signal rather than in training-set size.

\subsection*{Model confidence defines an applicability domain and prioritises follow-up experiments}

Modest per-phenotype sample requirements do not, however, certify any single new-genome prediction: deploying the specialist on a genome whose concordance cannot be established in advance requires a per-prediction signal of when the model is operating inside its applicability domain, and the specialist's advantage also averages out on arbitrary natural-composition mixtures (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C,D).
We therefore asked whether a model can flag which of its own predictions to trust using only information available without the experimental phenotype.
Prediction confidence ($\max(p,\,1-p)$) ranked correct above incorrect predictions for both models, though only modestly when pooled across phenotypes (ROC AUC $0.65$ concordant, $0.62$ full-data); per-phenotype AUC ranged from near-chance for glycerol to $0.73$--$0.78$ for the strongest generalisers, and both models were overconfident under cross-dataset shift (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B; Supplementary Text~S11), so confidence is useful for ranking and abstention but not as a calibrated probability.
Abstaining on the least-confident predictions raised retained-subset balanced accuracy for phenotypes the models already predict well (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A): for histidine, abstention lifted concordant-model balanced accuracy from $0.76$ to $0.84$ at half coverage, defining a practical applicability domain for the deployed model~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject}.

At the phenotype level, however, no label-free signal predicted which phenotypes a model would handle reliably: neither mean prediction confidence nor feature-space novelty correlated with cross-dataset balanced accuracy across the 15 phenotypes (Spearman $\rho$ between $-0.17$ and $0.05$, all $p > 0.3$).
Because per-phenotype reliability cannot be certified in advance, we asked instead which additional measurements would most improve the deployed concordant model --- a natural extension of the sample-size analysis above, which assumed labels were acquired at random.
In a retrospective simulation, 25 held-out genome labels were added to the concordant training set and the model retrained (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C).
Selecting the least-confident genomes improved transfer the most, exceeding random selection (mean $\Delta$ balanced accuracy $0.09$ vs $0.07$; paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.001$), whereas selecting the most novel or diverse genomes did not beat random.
The advantage concentrated on the weak generaliser glucose, where low-confidence selection roughly doubled the gain from random labelling ($0.24$ vs $0.13$), while the already-reliable m-inositol and histidine gained little from either strategy (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D).
Model confidence therefore provides a deployment-oriented ranking signal, enriching for predictions more suitable for selective reporting and for experiments likely to improve the model.
Together with the data-coherence, filter-alternative, and sample-size results above, these per-genome signals complete a deployable workflow whose use is bounded by an explicit, label-free applicability gate.
