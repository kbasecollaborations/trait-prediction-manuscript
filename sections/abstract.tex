\begin{abstract}
{
\noindent
Predicting microbial carbon utilization from genomic data remains challenging despite well-characterized metabolic pathways.
Machine learning (ML) models aimed at genotype-phenotype prediction often struggle to generalize beyond their training datasets.
Here, we investigated this generalization problem using microbial carbon utilization as a model system, compiling binary growth phenotypes for 819 bacterial genomes across 242 carbon sources from four independent experimental datasets.
We show that generalization failures arise primarily from data coherence rather than algorithmic limitations: when training data contain internally inconsistent genotype-phenotype relationships, models achieve high within-dataset accuracy but fail to transfer across datasets and phylogenetic contexts, instead learning dataset- or phylogeny-correlated shortcuts.
To address this, we stratified samples based on concordance between a mechanistic predictor of carbon utilization (GapMind pathway completeness) and experimental growth outcomes.
Models trained exclusively on concordant samples---where genotype-phenotype mappings are internally consistent and encoded within the genomic feature space---achieved robust cross-dataset generalization and consistently recovered known pathway genes as top predictive features.
These models also correctly predicted a subset of samples misclassified by the mechanistic predictor, indicating that ML captures information beyond curated pathway rules.
As few as 50--100 high-quality training samples sufficed for robust generalization.
We further evaluated data-driven filtering strategies that partially improve generalization when mechanistic predictors are unavailable.
Together, these results reframe generalization in biological ML as a data coherence problem: when genotype-phenotype relationships are internally consistent, models learn transferable mechanistic features; when they are not, models default to spurious correlations.
}
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Machine learning, Microbial phenotypes, Genotype-phenotype mapping, Carbon utilization}
