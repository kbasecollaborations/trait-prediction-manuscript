\begin{abstract}
{
\noindent
Predicting microbial carbon utilisation from genomic data remains challenging despite well-characterised metabolic pathways, and machine learning (ML) models for genotype-phenotype prediction often struggle to generalise beyond their training data.
Here, we investigated this problem using microbial carbon utilisation as a model system by compiling binary growth phenotypes for 819 bacterial genomes across 242 carbon sources from four independent experimental datasets.
We found that generalisation failures arose primarily from data coherence rather than algorithmic limitations: when training data contained internally inconsistent genotype-phenotype relationships, models achieved high within-dataset performance but transferred poorly across datasets and phylogenetic contexts, instead learning dataset- or phylogeny-correlated shortcuts.
When test conditions resembled training conditions, training on the full dataset maximised performance, making this strategy appropriate for same-environment applications.
To improve transferability, we stratified samples by concordance between a mechanistic predictor of carbon utilisation, GapMind pathway completeness, and experimental growth outcomes.
Models trained on concordant samples, where genotype-phenotype relationships were internally consistent and represented in the genomic feature space, achieved robust cross-dataset generalisation and consistently recovered known pathway genes as top predictive features.
These models also correctly classified a subset of samples misclassified by the mechanistic predictor, indicating that ML captures information beyond curated pathway rules.
For histidine utilisation, robust generalisation required only 50--100 high-quality training samples.
Together, these results reframe ML generalisation as a data coherence problem: when mappings are internally consistent and biologically encoded in the available features, models learn transferable mechanistic signals; when they are not, they default to spurious correlations that fail to generalise.
}
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Machine learning, Microbial phenotypes, Genotype-phenotype mapping, Carbon utilisation}
