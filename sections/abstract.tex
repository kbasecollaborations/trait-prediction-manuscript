\begin{abstract}
{
\noindent
Predicting microbial carbon utilization from genomic data remains challenging despite well-characterized metabolic pathways.
Yet, machine learning (ML) models aimed at genotype-phenotype prediction often struggle to generalize beyond their training datasets.
Here, we investigate this generalization problem using microbial carbon utilization as a model system, compiling binary growth phenotypes for 819 bacterial genomes across 242 carbon sources from four independent experimental datasets.
We show that generalization failures arise primarily from data coherence rather than algorithmic limitations: when training data contain internally inconsistent genotype-phenotype relationships, models achieve high within-dataset accuracy but fail to transfer across datasets and phylogenetic contexts, instead learning dataset- or phylogeny-correlated shortcuts.
When test conditions resemble training conditions, full-dataset training maximizes performance, making this approach appropriate for same-environment applications.
To address generalization failure, we stratified samples based on concordance between a mechanistic predictor of carbon utilization (GapMind pathway completeness) and experimental growth outcomes.
Models trained exclusively on concordant samples---where genotype-phenotype mappings are internally consistent and encoded within the genomic feature space---achieved robust cross-dataset generalization and consistently recovered known pathway genes as top predictive features.
These models also correctly predicted a subset of samples misclassified by the mechanistic predictor, indicating that ML captures information beyond curated pathway rules.
Learning curves for histidine utilization demonstrate that robust generalization can be achieved with relatively modest numbers of high-quality training samples (approximately 50--100), highlighting the efficiency gains possible when mechanistic coherence is enforced.
We further evaluate data-driven filtering strategies that partially improve generalization when mechanistic predictors are unavailable.
Together, these results reframe generalization in biological ML as a data coherence problem rather than a data volume or algorithmic one: when genotype-phenotype relationships are internally consistent and represented within the available feature space, ML models learn transferable mechanistic features; when they are not, models default to spurious correlations that fail to generalize.
}
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Machine learning, Microbial phenotypes, Genotype-phenotype mapping, Carbon utilization}
