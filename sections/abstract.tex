\begin{abstract}
  {
    \noindent
Predicting microbial nutrient utilization from genomic data remains challenging despite advances in comparative genomics and machine learning.
Mechanistic approaches based on curated pathway knowledge provide interpretable predictions but are limited due to incomplete annotations, regulatory complexity and unknown or alternate pathways.
Phylogeny is commonly used to assign traits however this is limited to evolutionary proximity and results in poor generalization across distant phylogenetic contexts.
In this study, we compiled 819 microbial genomes with measured (binary) growth outcomes across 242 carbon sources from four independent experimental collections, integrated genomic annotations from multiple sources, and developed phenotype-specific classifiers using gradient-boosted decision trees.

Machine learning models achieved strong within-dataset performance but exhibited substantially lower cross-dataset accuracy.
We systematically disentangled the independent contributions of dataset-specific biases and phylogenetic distance through controlled partitioning strategies.
Feature importance analysis revealed that models trained on complete datasets frequently identified phylogenetically correlated rather than mechanistically relevant genes.
Integrating multiple annotation sources improved performance for 82\% of phenotypes, and biologically informed feature selection increased interpretability, but neither approach resolved generalizability failures.

When we restricted training to samples where GapMind mechanism-based predictions correctly matched experimental outcomes, models achieved near-perfect accuracy and exhibited robust generalizability across datasets, phylogenetic clades, and annotation sources.
Systematic characterization of prediction discordances revealed that poor generalizability stems primarily from incomplete genome annotations, experimental measurement variability, and genuine biological pathway differences.
We quantified these error sources through per-genome and per-phenotype misclassification analysis, established optimal confidence thresholds balancing data quality against quantity, and determined minimum training set size requirements.
These findings demonstrate that under controlled conditions with high-quality data, machine learning successfully identifies mechanistically relevant genomic features, establishing that generalizability failures reflect data quality limitations rather than algorithmic inadequacy.
  }
\end{abstract}
