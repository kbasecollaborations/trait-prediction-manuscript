\begin{abstract}
{
\noindent
Which carbon sources a bacterium uses shapes its ecological niche, yet this trait resists prediction from genome sequence despite well-characterised catabolic pathways.
Genome-based models often score well within one study but lose accuracy on others, and whether the algorithm or the data is at fault was unclear.
We combined grow-or-no-grow records for 819 strains across 242 carbon sources from four studies, focusing on the 15 shared sources; accuracy fell across studies even among close relatives, so phylogeny alone did not explain it.
Restricting training to strains whose measured growth matched their annotated catabolic pathways, with the model otherwise unchanged, raised the carbon sources with predictors recurring across studies from six to twelve of fifteen, often pathway genes, whereas size-matched random subsets did not.
A much larger genome collection improved the unfiltered model but did not significantly exceed the smaller agreement-filtered set, whose predictors were more reproducible.
Because agreement filtering needs a mechanistic tool, a phylogeny-and-label surrogate recovered part of the benefit, not the recall gain.
Neither filter beat a curated pathway predictor overall; the agreement filter instead recovered growth incomplete annotations miss more often than it overturned pathways lacking growth (44\% versus 19\%), mainly metabolic generalists rather than substrate-specific routes.
Reliable prediction therefore depends less on strain number than on coherence between growth and annotated pathways: train where the two agree, and prioritise measuring the genomes a model is least sure of, improving transfer over random sampling.
}
\end{abstract}
