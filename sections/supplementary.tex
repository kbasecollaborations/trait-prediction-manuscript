\subsection*{Supplementary Text S1: Dataset Descriptions}

We obtained and harmonised four experimental carbon source utilisation datasets spanning phylogenetically and ecologically diverse bacterial communities.
Because of annotation pipeline limitations and incomplete phenotypic data, the number of feature-linked strains used in analyses was lower than the original dataset sizes (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_overview}).
All analyses focused on the 15 shared phenotypes present across all four datasets.

\paragraph*{ATLeaf.}
The ATLeaf dataset~\cite{Schafer2023-nk} comprised 224 strains isolated from \textit{Arabidopsis thaliana} leaf surfaces, tested on 45 carbon substrates using minimal media on agar plates.
After genome annotation and quality filtering, 206 genomes were retained.

\paragraph*{Biolog.}
The Biolog dataset encompassed 362 strains across 64 substrates, compiled from multiple published studies~\cite{BiologDatasetCitation}.
% TODO(CITATION): Ask Chris for the correct citation for this compiled Biolog phenotype matrix.
Growth phenotypes were measured using Biolog Phenotype MicroArray (PM) technology, which assesses carbon source utilisation via colourimetric tetrazolium-based readout in 96-well plates.
All 362 genomes passed quality control and were retained for downstream analysis.

\paragraph*{Marine.}
The Marine dataset~\cite{Gralka2023-ft} contained 175 heterotrophic marine bacteria assessed on 100 carbon substrates using carbon source utilisation assays.
The processed phenotype table retained 172 strain records.
Of these, 157 had matching processed genomic features and entered feature-based analyses; 15 were excluded during identifier alignment before model fitting.

\paragraph*{Populus.}
The Populus data are a previously unpublished Biolog carbon-source assay of ORNL Plant-Microbe Interfaces bacterial isolates from \textit{Populus deltoides} rhizosphere and endosphere, measured before this work and released here for the first time; the assay recorded growth as optical density in minimal medium for 58 isolates across 191 carbon sources, and the source spreadsheet (\texttt{PMI strain BiologSummary.xlsx}) is provided in the Zenodo deposit.
% Populus provenance: previously unpublished ORNL Plant-Microbe Interfaces Biolog assay; the source spreadsheet PMI strain BiologSummary.xlsx is included in the Zenodo record (covered by the deposit DOI).
After filtering, 55 genomes were retained.
Because of its small sample size and narrow phylogenetic breadth, Populus contributed less statistical power and was excluded from selected feature-stability comparisons.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S2: Genome Quality Control}

\paragraph*{Assembly filtering.}
As a first filter, we excluded highly fragmented genome assemblies by removing genomes with more than 500 contigs.

\paragraph*{CheckM2 quality assessment.}
Remaining genomes were evaluated for assembly quality using CheckM2 \cite{Chklovski2023CheckM2}, which provides machine-learning-based estimates of genome completeness and contamination.
We retained genomes with completeness $>$90\% and contamination $<$5\%, consistent with community standards for high-quality genome assemblies.

\paragraph*{Outlier detection.}
We performed statistical outlier detection on the KEGG feature matrix using Isolation Forest \cite{Liu2008IsolationForest} with 100 estimators, contamination rate of 0.05, and random state 42.

\paragraph*{Pangenome-based completeness assessment.}
As an independent quality metric, we estimated genome completeness based on the presence of species-specific core genes identified from pangenome analyses.
Core gene detection used MMseqs2 \cite{Steinegger2017MMseqs2} with a minimum sequence identity of 90\%, coverage of 80\%, and E-value threshold of $10^{-3}$.
Completeness was calculated as the fraction of species core genes detected in each genome (Figure~\ref{fig:pangenome_completeness}).
The pangenome-completeness audit was run on the 829 genome records available to that workflow and was used as an independent corroborating quality metric; downstream feature-based analyses used up to 780 strains with matched phenotype and genomic-feature identifiers.

\paragraph*{Overall attrition.}
Across all datasets, the quality control pipeline retained phenotype records for 795 of 819 input strains (97.1\%).
Per-dataset retention rates were: ATLeaf 206/224 (92.0\%), Biolog 362/362 (100.0\%), Marine 172/175 (98.3\%), and Populus 55/58 (94.8\%).
Of the 795 retained phenotype records, 780 matched processed genomic-feature identifiers and were eligible for feature-based analyses.
ATLeaf showed the highest absolute attrition, from assembly fragmentation and CheckM2 filtering; the upstream-curated Biolog set passed without further attrition.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S3: Model Selection and Evaluation Details}

\paragraph*{GapMind pathway annotation.}
Carbon-utilisation pathway completeness was computed with GapMind for carbon sources~\cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Price2020GapMind} from the PaperBLAST suite (\url{https://github.com/morgannprice/PaperBLAST}, commit 1033662, accessed May 2025).
For each pathway step, candidate genes were identified by sequence search against GapMind's curated reference databases with USEARCH v11.0.667~\cite{Edgar2010} (minimum 30\% identity; E-value threshold of $0.02 \times$ the number of genomes searched) and by profile search with HMMer v3.3.1~\cite{Eddy2011}.
Candidates were validated by reverse search against the reference database, and pathway completeness was scored from the presence and quality of matches to required steps, accounting for alternative routes and isofunctional enzymes.
% TODO(VALIDATE): confirm the HMM profile count (drafted as 145) and the worked E-value examples (E approx 12.46 for ATLeaf, E approx 4.12 for Marine) against the actual GapMind run logs before submission.
Because it scaled with dataset size, the E-value threshold differed across datasets (E~$\approx$~12.46 for ATLeaf and E~$\approx$~4.12 for Marine).
The integer step scores used here (0--2) and the strict and permissive thresholds applied for the mechanistic baseline are described in the Methods.

We explored a range of classifier families on KOFAM features, including gradient-boosted trees (CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost), regularised-linear and projection methods (Lasso, Elastic-Net, group Lasso, sparse PLS-DA), Random Forest, and a phylogeny-aware model, across all 15 phenotypes and four evaluation regimes.
The families clustered within a 0.05 balanced-accuracy band in the great majority of phenotype--regime cells (88\% ties), with the gradient-boosted trees strongest and Random Forest the only consistent under-performer (Table~\ref{tab:best_models_counts}).
We adopted CatBoost, one of the best-performing models; further per-phenotype hyperparameter tuning did not improve its accuracy, so the curated configuration was retained (1000 iterations, learning rate 0.03, tree depth 4, L2 regularisation 15, random subspace method with 10\% of features per split, Bayesian bootstrap, and early stopping on logloss validation with patience 50).
These comparisons used a single protocol without early stopping, so absolute values differ by one to three balanced-accuracy points from the early-stopping CatBoost reported elsewhere.
All models were evaluated using balanced accuracy as the primary reporting metric to account for class imbalance, which varied substantially across phenotypes and datasets.

The minority-class filter (Methods) prevents balanced-accuracy collapse on test cells with extreme class imbalance.
The minority count was computed against the appropriate test-set definition for each figure: the concordant subset for Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A and the concordant-test points of Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D, the discordant subset for the discordant-test points of Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D, and the full labelled subset for Figures~\ref{fig:ml_performance}B, \ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C, \ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B--D, and~\ref{fig:full_held_out_test}.
Implementation is consolidated in \texttt{scripts/minority\_filter.py}.
In every machine-learning-versus-GapMind comparison the same minority filter is applied identically to both series, so each contrast is evaluated on a matched set of phenotype--dataset cells.

\paragraph*{Replicate genomes and random-holdout overlap.}
A small number of genomes in the pooled data share an identical KOFAM feature vector with another genome: 19 of 822 genomes fall into nine identical-vector groups, each consisting of within-dataset replicate or re-assembled isolates (for example, the marine pair \texttt{4A09} and \texttt{4A09\_2}).
Because random-holdout partitions are drawn from the pooled data, such a pair can be split across training and test, so a small fraction of random-holdout test predictions have a same-label feature near-duplicate in the training pool (approximately 1.4\% with an identical feature vector and approximately 8\% at Jaccard $\geq 0.98$; \texttt{scripts/leakage\_check\_feature\_dup.py}).
This contributes to the optimism of the random-holdout estimate.
Every identical-vector group lies within a single dataset, so leave-one-dataset-out cross-dataset evaluation keeps both copies on the same side of the split and is unaffected.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S5: Concordant Training Under the KOFAM Feature Space}

In the main analysis, GapMind concordance was used only to select training samples, whereas KOFAM annotations provided the model feature space.
This design decouples the concordance criterion from the model input features and avoids a tautological comparison in which GapMind-derived features are used to recover GapMind-defined concordance.
To isolate the effect of concordant training, we compared KOFAM-based models trained on all samples with KOFAM-based models trained only on GapMind-concordant samples.

Figure~\ref{fig:kofam_concordant} shows that concordant training improves cross-dataset balanced accuracy on concordant test samples under the same KOFAM feature space used in the main analysis.
The parity plot (panel~A) indicates that the majority of phenotype--split combinations achieve higher balanced accuracy with concordant training when evaluation is restricted to concordant held-out samples.
Per-phenotype box plots (panel~B) confirm that the concordant-training improvement is consistent across most phenotypes, supporting the interpretation that data coherence, rather than feature--criterion alignment, drives the generalisation gains reported in the main text.

A related caveat applies to the feature-recovery analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}).
Concordance is defined by agreement with GapMind, and the pathway-coverage metric scores stable features against KEGG reference maps, so the selection criterion and the recovery yardstick are biologically related.
They are not, however, the same construct: GapMind scores pathway completeness against curated reference protein databases (Supplementary Text~S3) rather than against KEGG modules, the model features are KofamScan KEGG Orthologs, and the recovered quantity is the cross-dataset stability of those features under SHAP analysis, which is not entailed by the selection rule.
GapMind and KOFAM nonetheless annotate gene content of the same genomes, so a residual biological correlation remains.
Consistent with the recovery not being entailed by selection, several concordant phenotypes recover predominantly off-map features (for example, the glycerol glp regulon at 0\% formal pathway residency, and alanine, whose GapMind definition encodes only transport), showing that pathway-map residency is not an automatic consequence of concordance filtering.
We further isolated the effect of selection from sample count with a size- and class-matched random-subset control (\texttt{scripts/figure5/figure5b\_random\_control.py}): repeating the stability analysis on random subsets matched to each concordant subset's size and class balance recovered only $0.38 \pm 0.02$ shared stable clusters across three draws, matching full-data training (0.5) and well below concordant training (1.3), so the feature-stability gain is specific to concordance rather than to subsetting.
Because concordance selects samples where pathway completeness matches the phenotype, the pathway-coverage percentages partly reflect this selection.

For the GapMind-feature pipeline-ceiling reference (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}A, red dashed lines), raw rather than correlation-filtered GapMind features were used because GapMind represents the same underlying gene under multiple per-phenotype prefixes (for example, the same amino-acid transporter appears as \texttt{Alanine-braC}, \texttt{Serine-braC}, etc.), so the standard 0.95 correlation filter consolidates these duplicates and unfairly depletes the per-phenotype feature space for some pathways.
Because concordant labels are by construction consistent with GapMind's pathway-completeness rule, the GapMind-feature setting establishes the balanced accuracy reachable by the pipeline when feature resolution matches the mechanism.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S8: SHAP-Supervised Redundancy Clustering Procedure}

The stable-feature comparisons in Figures~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C and~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B report cluster-level rather than raw-KO intersections so that paralogues, operon-mates, and alternative enzymes representing the same biological signal are counted as one entity.
The grouping uses the SHAP package's \texttt{shap.utils.hclust} routine~\cite{Lundberg2017SHAP,Lundberg2020TreeSHAP}: a univariate gradient-boosted model is fit from each candidate KO to the phenotype, and a pairwise distance of $1-R^{2}$ is computed between the resulting per-sample predictions.
The resulting distance matrix is single-linkage clustered with the default cutoff of 0.5, so two KOs are placed in the same cluster when they share more than 50\% of their univariate explanatory power with respect to the phenotype.
Clustering was performed on the pooled concordant feature matrix across all four datasets so that cluster identities are common to the full-data and concordant-data analyses.
The pooled clustering procedure was applied only after stable features had been identified; it did not influence model fitting, held-out predictions, or balanced-accuracy estimates.
For tractability and to avoid the variance-inflation that the early-stopping schedule introduces into per-feature SHAP attributions across the 20 random seeds, the stability analyses used a fixed 500-iteration CatBoost variant trained without early stopping; the fixed-iteration variant matched the default-classifier performance in internal checks.

For three phenotypes (alanine, mannose, serine), the stable features selected by different concordance-trained models did not reach the target-redundancy threshold yet remained cross-correlated within the held-out dataset (e.g., for mannose, the ribose- and xylose-uptake transporter ATP-binding proteins K10441 and K10545 with $|r|=0.67$ in the held-out concordant ATLeaf samples).
Inspection of the underlying KOFAM annotations showed that the stable features comprise a heterogeneous mixture of carbohydrate-utilisation and accessory genes rather than members of a shared mechanistic pathway.
For alanine, mannose, and serine, the concordance-trained model therefore generalises through co-occurring substrate-utilisation markers characteristic of broad carbohydrate utilisers rather than through convergent mechanistic features.
This pattern represents a less specific mode of transfer that remains within the applicability domain of organisms sharing expanded carbohydrate-utilisation repertoires.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S9: Reliability and Experiment-Prioritisation Details}

CatBoost probabilities were not post hoc calibrated, so prediction confidence ($\max(p,\,1-p)$) was used only as a ranking score for selective prediction rather than as an absolute probability of correctness; calibration was assessed with reliability diagrams and the expected calibration error over ten equal-width probability bins, pooled across phenotypes for each model.
Per-phenotype risk--coverage curves were generated by stratifying abstention by predicted class: within each phenotype and model, predictions were partitioned by the predicted label and at each coverage level the top fraction of most-confident class-1 predictions (highest $p$) and the top fraction of most-confident class-0 predictions (highest $1-p$) were retained in parallel before balanced accuracy was recomputed on the retained subset.
This class-stratified construction avoids the degenerate single-predicted-class subsets that arise on individual phenotypes when ranking by raw $\max(p,\,1-p)$ for class-skewed classifiers, which would collapse balanced accuracy on the retained subset to 0.5 regardless of accuracy on the discarded fraction.

Feature-space novelty of a held-out genome was defined as its mean Jaccard distance to the five nearest training genomes in KOFAM feature space, computed against the appropriate per-split training set.
For the retrospective experiment-prioritisation simulation, each held-out dataset was split into candidate and evaluation pools; 25 candidate genome labels were added to the concordant training set, the model was retrained, and the change in cross-dataset balanced accuracy was measured on the evaluation pool across three random seeds and the four held-out datasets.
Because the candidate and evaluation pools come from the same held-out dataset, the random-selection baseline already captures the gain from the model's first exposure to that dataset, so low-confidence selection is credited only for the additional improvement from preferring informative candidates.
Models in the experiment-prioritisation simulation were trained with a reduced number of boosting iterations for computational efficiency.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S10: Composite Confidence Score for Data Quality Filtering}

The composite confidence score used to identify ambiguous training samples (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B) takes the form
\begin{equation*}
    y_{\mathrm{soft}} = w_{\mathrm{phylo}} \cdot \mathrm{phylo} + w_{\mathrm{gapmind}} \cdot \mathrm{GapMind} + w_{\mathrm{exp}} \cdot \mathrm{exp},
\end{equation*}
combining three components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Phylogenetic (phylo): agreement among $k = 3$ nearest phylogenetic neighbours.
    \item GapMind: pathway completeness score mapped to a six-level scale (0.0--1.0) based on the GapMind completeness category.
    \item Experimental (exp): binary experimental phenotype, used only for training-set quality assessment and never available to the model at inference.
\end{itemize}
Samples with $y_{\mathrm{soft}} \in [0.4, 0.6]$ were excluded from the filtered training set; samples with $y_{\mathrm{soft}} < 0.4$ or $y_{\mathrm{soft}} > 0.6$ were retained.
Four weight configurations were evaluated (Table~\ref{tab:confidence_weight_configs}), spanning a mechanism-free filter ($w_{\mathrm{gapmind}} = 0$) and three GapMind-weighted variants; the filter and threshold were identical across configurations and applied only to training and validation samples.
The Phase 1 retention diagnostic is implemented in \texttt{scripts/figure6/figure6b\_parameter\_exploration.py}, and the Phase 2 machine-learning evaluation in \texttt{scripts/figure6/figure6b\_weight\_sweep.py}.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S11: Per-Phenotype Detail for Concordant-Training Analysis}

\paragraph{GapMind-feature ceiling failures.}
Even GapMind-feature models did not reach the random-holdout ceiling on every cross-dataset cell.
Residual gaps reflected applicability-domain effects (minority-class-sparse held-out concordant sets, where balanced accuracy is mechanically constrained; per-dataset shifts in which transporter or permease step features fire, so held-out genomes present zero-imputed step features the model never trained on) rather than failures of the GapMind rule itself.

\paragraph{Glucose feature-resolution gap.}
The largest KOFAM-vs-GapMind feature gap was glucose, where the GapMind ceiling of 0.77 substantially exceeded the KOFAM cross-dataset balanced accuracy of 0.51.
The GapMind glucose pathway invokes organism-specific transporter and PTS-system identifiers (e.g., \verb|MFS-glucose|, \verb|SSS-glucose|, \verb|ptsG-crr|) that lack direct KOFAM equivalents.

\paragraph{Histidine off-pathway stable features.}
For histidine on ATLeaf, the full-data unique stable features included $\beta$-ketoadipate-pathway components (K01032 \textit{pcaJ} 3-oxoadipate CoA-transferase $\beta$-subunit; K02623 \textit{pcaR}-type LysR regulator) and a methionine-cycle enzyme (K00547 homocysteine S-methyltransferase), all off-pathway with respect to M00045 (histidine degradation); the concordance-trained unique features were predominantly histidine-pathway paralogues or alternative steps (K01458 N-formylglutamate deformylase, K05603 formimidoylglutamate deiminase, K01745 histidine ammonia-lyase).
The $\beta$-ketoadipate pathway is widely distributed in soil and rhizosphere lineages with broad catabolic repertoires, so the full-data off-pathway features likely reflect phylogenetic co-occurrence with metabolically versatile clades rather than a histidine-specific route.

\paragraph{Calibration and per-phenotype prediction-confidence detail.}
Both the full-data and concordance-trained models were overconfident under cross-dataset shift, with expected calibration error 0.12 and 0.18, respectively (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}B).
Confidence-based abstention raised retained-subset balanced accuracy for phenotypes the models already predict well, but not for those they do not (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A): both models remained accurate for m-inositol (balanced accuracy near 0.80 at all coverage levels), the concordance-trained model exceeded the full-data model for histidine, and neither model rose above 0.5 for glucose, whose predictions had collapsed towards a single class.
On the rescue side, both FN-rescue and FP-rescue rates remained below the model's marginal class-prediction rate, indicating partial anchoring to GapMind's failure mode through shared pathway-linked features; the per-error-type asymmetry rather than the absolute rescue level is therefore the load-bearing finding.

\paragraph{Retaining false negatives versus the specificity tradeoff.}
Concordance filtering drops both GapMind false positives and false negatives, but these are not equivalent, so we compared three regimes cross-dataset under balanced class weights: \textit{unfiltered} training on every label; \textit{concordance filtering} (dropping both); and \textit{false-positive filtering} (dropping only the false positives and retaining the false negatives, organisms that grow despite an incomplete annotated pathway and so use cryptic or alternative routes GapMind does not score).
Concordance filtering gave the best cross-dataset balanced accuracy (0.051 above unfiltered).
Re-adding the false negatives raised recall by 0.030, the highest of any regime and rising in 11 of 15 phenotypes, but lowered specificity by 0.097 and balanced accuracy by 0.033, consistent with recovery of cryptic growers at the cost of a more permissive model, since cryptic routes are heterogeneous and some false negatives are likely mislabels.
Concordance filtering is therefore preferable for balanced-accuracy deployment and false-positive filtering for recall-critical screening.
A focused feature-level analysis of the retained false negatives showed that they are disproportionately metabolic generalists.
Relative to GapMind-negative non-growers, false-negative growers utilised more of the other carbon sources (pooled mean 9.5 versus 5.2 of the remaining 14; Mann-Whitney $p < 10^{-200}$) and encoded larger sugar-transporter repertoires (6.5 versus 4.3 sugar-ABC transport components; $p < 10^{-60}$), with both trends holding in almost every phenotype.
Consistent with this, the features that the false negatives introduce over concordant training were dominated by broad-specificity carbohydrate transporters and transcriptional regulators of sugar catabolism rather than by alternative catabolic enzymes, and the GapMind step most often absent in false-negative growers was a transporter rather than an enzyme (9 of 15 phenotypes).
Most of these broad transporters recur across chemically unrelated substrates and are better read as markers of metabolic versatility than as substrate-specific routes: their presence does not establish that they serve the focal substrate, so the model recovers these false negatives by recognising a generalist genomic signature rather than a substrate-specific mechanism, which is also why GapMind, requiring substrate-specific evidence, does not credit them.
The two clearest substrate-specific exceptions were both routes GapMind does not score: sucrose uptake via a broad multiple-sugar ABC importer (K02025), carried by 58 of the sucrose false negatives, of which 57 lack the canonical sucrose phosphotransferase system, and a divergent altronate dehydratase (K26399)~\cite{Watanabe2019Altronate} in galacturonate-utilising genomes that lack the canonical \textit{uxaA} gene GapMind requires, a candidate alternative isozyme rather than a validated novel route.
Partitioning the false negatives by the genomic presence of their catabolic machinery separated a recoverable fraction from a larger residual lacking such support; this residual was disproportionately associated with confident agreement between the model and GapMind against the experimental label (odds ratio 3.3), consistent with a component of experimental mislabelling of the kind quantified for near-identical cross-dataset strains in Supplementary Text~S13.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S12: Adding Data Volume Versus Concordance Filtering}

Because our set of up to 780 feature-linked strains falls below the thousand-genome scale at which phylogenetic confounding is reported to recede~\cite{Li2023-up,Yu2025BiasedSampling}, we tested directly whether adding training samples could substitute for concordance-based curation in the cross-dataset setting.
We trained CatBoost classifiers (KOFAM features) under three strategies and evaluated each on the full labels of a held-out manuscript dataset (leave-one-dataset-out; 13 shared phenotypes, four held-out datasets, five seeds): concordant samples of the other three datasets (C), all samples of the other three datasets (F), and the full other three datasets plus all BacDive genomes (FB).
The volume arm drew on a BacDive snapshot of 8{,}501 genomes with KOFAM annotations~\cite{Schober2025BacDive}, of which up to approximately 1{,}800 per phenotype entered training after intersecting labels with features and removing any genome sharing a stripped accession with the held-out test.
% TODO(PROVENANCE): pin the exact BacDive release/access date in the deposited analysis code before submission.
Because BacDive labels are strongly positive-skewed, all models used balanced class weights, applied uniformly across strategies.

Adding BacDive (FB) significantly improved cross-dataset balanced accuracy over the unfiltered model (F) (Figure~\ref{fig:bacdive_curation}A; 0.725 versus 0.677, paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$, 12 of 13 phenotypes), confirming that volume helps when the training data are uncurated.
However, FB did not significantly exceed concordance filtering of the smaller curated set (C) (0.725 versus 0.706, $p = 0.20$; Matthews correlation coefficient $p = 0.53$; area under the ROC curve $p = 0.99$); the apparent edge reflected higher precision offset by lower recall, so C scored equal or higher on $F_1$ and recall.
The benefit of added volume was not predicted by the size of the per-phenotype BacDive pool (Spearman $\rho = 0.26$, $p = 0.38$).

Across the five seeds, feature selection became progressively less reproducible as volume was added (Figure~\ref{fig:bacdive_curation}B,C; mean pairwise Jaccard of the top-10 CatBoost-importance features 0.378 for C, 0.316 for F, 0.253 for FB; C versus FB $p < 10^{-8}$), an ordering robust to restricting features to the shared KOFAM space and to an order-sensitive rank-overlap metric.
Concordance-trained models more consistently selected committed-pathway features (for example, the histidine-utilisation enzyme K01468 appeared in all 20 concordant runs but 6 of 20 under FB), whereas added volume tended to demote these in favour of more variable, dataset-driven features; mannose was an exception, where added volume both improved accuracy and yielded a more coherent transporter and catabolism feature set.
We therefore read this as evidence that, within our sample-size regime, concordance-based curation matches the cross-dataset accuracy of large-scale added data while remaining more parsimonious and yielding more reproducible, mechanism-aligned features, not as a claim that data volume is without value~\cite{Geirhos2020Shortcut}.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S13: Cross-Dataset Label Inconsistency Among Near-Identical Strains}

If part of the cross-dataset performance gap reflects label inconsistency rather than reproducible biology, then phylogenetically near-identical strains should disagree on their growth labels more often when measured by different datasets than by the same one.
Restricting comparisons to genome pairs below a stringent phylogenetic distance ($d \leq 0.01$), near-identical strains conflicted on 31.0\% (81 of 261) of cross-dataset comparisons but only 10.2\% (782 of 7{,}706) of within-dataset comparisons (logistic odds ratio 3.98), an excess that was monotone across stricter thresholds.
The datasets call positives at different rates (Biolog and Populus more liberally than ATLeaf and Marine), but the excess survived adjustment: a logistic regression of conflict on the cross-dataset indicator, controlling for the base-rate-expected conflict, gave an adjusted odds ratio of 2.85 (95\% confidence interval 2.13 to 3.81, $p = 2.0 \times 10^{-12}$), and a more conservative model conditioning on both datasets' rates remained significant (1.75, $p = 0.014$); base-rate differences account for only about a quarter of the raw gap.
Several non-exclusive causes are compatible with this pattern and the analysis cannot separate them: assay and readout differences across datasets, genuine ecological and strain-history divergence among near-identical genomes (accessory gene content, regulation, niche), and curation differences, with the base-rate control ruling out only the positive-call-rate artefact.
Regardless of cause, cross-dataset labels are less self-consistent than within-dataset labels, so concordance filtering preferentially removes the least reproducible samples, supporting the data-coherence interpretation of the main-text cross-dataset gains.
Because most near-identical strains nonetheless agree, this inconsistency limits cross-dataset transfer but does not prevent it, and the reproducible samples that concordance filtering retains are those on which transfer is expected to hold.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text S14: Enforcing Concordance in the Training Set Versus in the Predictions}

The concordance criterion can be enforced at two points, and the two are nearly equivalent.
Concordant training enforces it in the training set, retaining only samples where GapMind's pathway-completeness call agrees with the experimental phenotype; the resulting specialist is trained on roughly 70\% of the samples but predicts from genomic features alone.
Alternatively, a model trained on all samples can be restricted to the predictions that agree with GapMind: on the 71\% of genomes where the two agree it reached cross-dataset balanced accuracy 0.76, recovering much of the concordant specialist's advantage, while the remaining discordant genomes are set aside.
The two strategies differ mainly in cost: concordant training evaluates GapMind once when assembling the training set, whereas filtering predictions requires a GapMind evaluation for every genome examined.
Because the concordance-trained model then requires only genomic features, enforcing concordance in the training set is the more economical strategy.
