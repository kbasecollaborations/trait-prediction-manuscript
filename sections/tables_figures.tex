\textbf{Sample size requirements for training generalisable models.}
Learning curves showing balanced accuracy as a function of training sample size for the histidine phenotype using GapMind-concordant data.
Columns represent evaluation scenarios: (left) random splits, (middle) cross-dataset splits, and (right) out-of-clade splits.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figure8.pdf}
\caption{
\textbf{Diagnostic and applicability-domain signals for genome-based phenotype prediction.}
(\textbf{A}) Risk--coverage curve for concordant-trained models on the full held-out cross-dataset test set, pooled across phenotypes and held-out datasets.
Genomes are ranked by model confidence ($\max(p,\,1-p)$); the curve shows balanced accuracy on the retained subset as a function of coverage, the fraction of genomes the model commits to.
(\textbf{B}) Balanced accuracy split by agreement between the GapMind call and the machine learning prediction; the disagreement subset is the natural priority list for experimental characterisation of novel mechanisms.
The dotted line marks balanced accuracy on the full test set and the dashed line marks random performance (balanced accuracy~$=0.5$).
(\textbf{C}) Per-phenotype diagnostic: the shortcut gap of the full-data model (mean random-split balanced accuracy minus mean cross-dataset balanced accuracy) versus the concordance benefit (mean concordant cross-dataset balanced accuracy on the full held-out test set minus mean full-data cross-dataset balanced accuracy).
A positive trend indicates that phenotypes whose full-data models rely most on dataset- or phylogeny-specific shortcuts are also those that gain most from concordance filtering, providing an a-priori signal of when filtering is likely to pay off.
}\label{fig:selective_prediction}
\end{figure}
