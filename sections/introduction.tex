\section*{Introduction}

Microbial nutrient utilisation phenotypes are key determinants of ecological niches and underpin community assembly, nutrient cycling, and biotechnological applications~\cite{Green2008Biogeography,Malik2020Defining,Falkowski2008-bx,Lu2014-zz}.
Cultivation-based methods underlie most phenotype data but cannot characterise unculturable taxa and remain resource-intensive even for culturable organisms~\cite{Lloyd2018-ar,Barberan2017-xi}.
Phylogeny has commonly served as a proxy for phenotype prediction, however, it is insufficient for novel organisms with limited phylogenetic context or for traits that are not phylogenetically conserved \cite{Li2023-up,Goberna2016Predicting}.

Advances in genomics offer a path to overcome these limitations \cite{Quince2017Shotgun,Nayfach2021Catalog}.
Genome-based predictions provide an alternative, enabling characterisation of both cultured and uncultured taxa while potentially uncovering the genetic mechanisms underlying phenotypic variation.
Early comparative-genomics and machine-learning systems, including PICA and Traitar, learned broad panels of microbial phenotypes from gene or protein-family patterns and linked predictive features to traits~\cite{Feldbauer2015PICA,Weimann2016Traitar,Brbic2016Landscape,Karlsen2023Genotype,Lees2020Pangenome}.
More recent multi-trait models such as MICROPHERRET and BacDiveAI extend phenotype coverage or draw on high-quality curated training data, while metaTraits integrates culture-derived records with outputs from multiple genome-based prediction systems at genome-catalogue scale~\cite{Bizzotto2024MICROPHERRET,Koblitz2025Phenotype,Podlesny2026MetaTraits}.
Mechanistic approaches such as GapMind and microTrait instead infer metabolic or ecological capabilities from curated genes, pathways, and logical rules~\cite{Price2020GapMind,Price2022GapMindCarbon,Karaoz2022MicroTrait}.
For carbon utilisation specifically, Li et al.\ showed that statistical gene-content models can outperform automatically reconstructed constraint-based metabolic models, while also demonstrating strong phylogenetic confounding and sample-size dependence~\cite{Li2023-up}.
Together, these approaches establish the feasibility of genome-based phenotype prediction, but leave unresolved how measurements pooled across independent experimental collections affect cross-dataset transfer and whether learned predictors recur as mechanistically relevant features across datasets.

Machine learning (ML) models can capture the nonlinear relationships and high dimensionality common in genomic data~\cite{Greener2022-dy,Zampieri2019MLMetabolic,James2025OpenProblems}.
However, their performance and generalisability are constrained by data limitations and evaluation challenges~\cite{Whalen2022Pitfalls,Shi2025OOD}: limited and taxonomically biased training data can cause overfitting and poor applicability to diverse genomes~\cite{Whalen2022Pitfalls,Ramoneda2024-yb,Albright2023-ze}.
Phylogenetic correlations among genomic features further confound identification of mechanistically relevant predictors~\cite{Bradley2018-rs}, since taxonomically biased models primarily capture phylogenetic signal~\cite{Li2023-up}.
Increasing sample size can improve out-of-clade prediction~\cite{Li2023-up}, and training on high-quality curated phenotypes can improve broader trait prediction~\cite{Koblitz2025Phenotype}, though such gains are typically established within a single curated source rather than across independent collections.
Furthermore, if genotype--phenotype mappings are of mixed quality they may dilute the mechanistic signal available to the model~\cite{Frenay2014LabelNoise,James2025OpenProblems}.
This suggests that label--mechanism agreement in training data, rather than only feature-space artefacts~\cite{Austin2025DEBIAS}, is an important constraint alongside sample size.

We tested this hypothesis using microbial carbon utilisation as a model system.
Carbon-utilisation phenotypes sit near the boundary of what genome-derived features can capture.
The core biochemical transformations of many catabolic pathways are well characterised and conserved, yet their genomic mapping is often ambiguous.
Transporters and broad-specificity enzymes are difficult to annotate, divergent or uncharacterised genes can perform similar chemistry, and regulatory mechanisms are inconsistently conserved across phylogeny.
We integrated binary growth outcomes for 819 microbial strains across 240 carbon sources from four independent experimental collections and developed phenotype-specific classifiers for 15 shared phenotypes.
We then compared feature representations and evaluation strategies designed to test transfer across datasets and clades.

Within the held-out concordant subset, models trained on concordant samples, those whose measured growth matches their annotated pathways, act as specialists.
They generalise better across datasets, more often recover established pathway genes as top features, and rescue some samples misclassified by GapMind, a pathway-completeness predictor.
This specialisation comes at the cost of coverage: the discordant samples set aside are precisely those whose genotype--phenotype links fall outside curated pathway definitions, so the resulting models apply to pathway-consistent genomes rather than to the full diversity of genotype--phenotype relationships.
We also show that this specialisation is data-efficient, as concordance-trained performance for histidine approached its all-sample value with 50 training samples.
Genome-based phenotype prediction is therefore constrained not only by training-data size but by whether the labels agree with the mechanism being learned.
Accuracy is consequently conditional on the genomes a model was built to describe, rather than a single value reported per phenotype.
