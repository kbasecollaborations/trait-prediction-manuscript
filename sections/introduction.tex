\section*{Introduction}

Microbial nutrient utilisation phenotypes are key determinants of ecological niches and influence organism distribution, interactions, and functional roles within complex ecosystems \cite{Falkowski2008-bx,Malik2020Defining}.
A mechanistic understanding of these phenotypes is crucial for advancing microbial ecology and providing insights into community assembly, nutrient cycling, and biotechnological applications, such as probiotic design or bioremediation \cite{Green2008Biogeography,Lu2014-zz}.
Traditional laboratory-based cultivation methods have characterised most existing microbial phenotype data; however, these methods cannot characterise the metabolic capabilities of unculturable taxa \cite{Lloyd2018-ar}, introducing significant biases and restricting our understanding of microbial diversity.
Even for culturable organisms, comprehensive phenotypic characterisation across environmental conditions remains resource-intensive \cite{Barberan2017-xi}.

Rapid advances in genomics offer a promising path to overcome these limitations \cite{Quince2017Shotgun,Nayfach2021Catalog}.
The increasing availability of genomic data, including metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), presents an opportunity to predict phenotypes directly from genomic information \cite{Li2023-up}, bypassing extensive laboratory experiments.
Although phylogeny has commonly been used as a proxy for phenotype prediction, it is insufficient for characterising novel organisms with limited phylogenetic context or for traits that are not phylogenetically conserved \cite{Bradley2018-rs}.
Genome-based predictions offer a powerful alternative, enabling the characterisation of both cultured and uncultured taxa while potentially uncovering the genetic mechanisms driving phenotypic variation \cite{Weimann2016Traitar,Brbic2016Landscape,Karlsen2023Genotype,Lees2020Pangenome,Koblitz2025Phenotype}.
These predictions can leverage various genomic features, from general characteristics, such as GC content \cite{Hu2022-fr} and genome size \cite{Li2019PredictiveGenomic,Chuckran2025-ap} to specific information derived from gene annotations and inferred metabolic pathways \cite{Long2021-yz}.

Machine learning models have emerged as valuable tools for inferring phenotypes from complex genomic data, capable of handling nonlinear relationships and high dimensionality often encountered in biological systems \cite{Greener2022-dy,Zampieri2019MLMetabolic,James2025OpenProblems}.
However, the performance and generalisability of these models are constrained by data limitations and evaluation challenges \cite{Whalen2022Pitfalls}.
Limited and taxonomically biased training data can lead to overfitting and poor applicability to diverse microbial communities \cite{Ramoneda2024-yb,Albright2023-ze}.
Furthermore, phylogenetic correlations among genomic features confound the identification of mechanistically relevant predictors; Li et al.\cite{Li2023-up} showed that predictors trained on taxonomically biased data primarily capture phylogenetic signal, leading to poor cross-clade transfer, and concluded that very large datasets are required to overcome this confounding \cite{Ramoneda2024-yb,Albright2023-ze}.
This interpretation is well supported; however, another possibility is that mixed-quality genotype--phenotype mappings dilute the mechanistic signal available to the model \cite{Northcutt2021Confident}, rather than indicating an inherent need for massive sample sizes.
Given this interpretation, we sought a system in which mechanistic expectations are strong, yet known representational and biological challenges remain.

We tested this hypothesis using microbial carbon utilisation as a model system.
Carbon utilisation phenotypes are well suited for this purpose because they sit near the boundary of what genome-derived features can capture.
The core biochemical transformations underlying many carbon catabolic pathways are well characterised and well conserved; however, their genomic realisation is often ambiguous due to challenges in annotating, for example, transporters and broad-specificity enzymes, the presence of divergent or previously uncharacterised genes capable of performing similar chemistry, and regulatory mechanisms that are poorly characterised and inconsistently conserved across phylogeny.
Carbon utilisation is therefore useful here because some pathways are well characterised, whereas transport, enzyme specificity, and regulation remain unevenly represented in genome annotations.
We compiled a comprehensive dataset of 819 microbial genomes and their binary growth outcomes across 242 carbon sources from four independent experimental collections.
We developed phenotype-specific classifiers using this dataset and compared feature representations and evaluation strategies designed to test transfer across datasets and clades.

We find that models trained on concordant samples, defined as those with internally consistent genotype--phenotype mappings, show stronger cross-dataset generalisation, more often recover established pathway genes as top predictive features, and correctly classify a subset of samples misclassified by the mechanistic predictor.
For histidine utilisation, 50--100 high-quality training samples were sufficient for strong performance across evaluation settings, consistent with the hypothesis that data coherence, rather than dataset size alone, is a key constraint on phenotype prediction.
These analyses identify data curation as a major determinant of phenotype-prediction performance in this setting.
