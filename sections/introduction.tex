Microbial nutrient utilization phenotypes are key determinants of a microorganism's ecological niche and influence its distribution, interactions, and functional roles within complex ecosystems (Falkowski et al., 2008). A mechanistic understanding of these phenotypes is therefore crucial for advancing microbial ecology, providing insights into community assembly, nutrient cycling, and the development of targeted biotechnological applications such as optimized probiotic design or bioremediation of contaminated environments (Lu et al., 2014). Most of the existing microbial phenotype data have been characterized using traditional laboratory-based cultivation methods. However, these methods have inherent limitations as they cannot characterize the metabolic capabilities of a large number of unculturable microbial taxa (Lloyd et al., 2018). This reliance on culturable organisms introduces significant biases and restricts our understanding of the true diversity and functional potential of microbial communities. Moreover, even for culturable organisms, comprehensive phenotypic characterization across a wide range of environmental conditions is resource-intensive and time-consuming (Barberán et al., 2017).

Rapid advances in genomics offer a promising path towards overcoming these limitations. The increasing availability of genomic data, including metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), presents a need and opportunity to directly predict phenotypes from genomic information (Li et al., 2023), bypassing the need for extensive laboratory experiments. Most commonly, phylogeny has been used as a proxy to derive information about the phenotype of an organism. However, although phylogenetic information can be informative, particularly when phenotypes exhibit strong phylogenetic conservation, it is insufficient when characterizing novel organisms with limited phylogenetic context, when dealing with traits that are not phylogenetically conserved, or for more complex traits (Bradley et al., 2018). Genome-based predictions offer a powerful and versatile alternative, enabling the characterization of both cultured and uncultured taxa and uncovering the underlying genetic mechanisms driving phenotypic variation. These predictions can leverage various genomic features, ranging from general characteristics, such as GC content (Hu et al., 2022), genome size, and codon usage bias (Chuckran et al., 2025), to more specific information derived from gene annotations, predicted protein structures, and inferred metabolic pathways (Long et al., 2021).

Machine learning models have emerged as valuable tools for inferring phenotypes from complex genomic data, as they are capable of handling non-linear relationships and high dimensionality often encountered in biological systems (Greener et al., 2022). However, the performance and generalizability of these models are often constrained by several factors. Limited training data, particularly for less-studied phenotypes, can lead to overfitting and poor prediction accuracy for unseen data (Ramoneda et al., 2024). Data sparsity, arising from the vast number of potential gene variants and uneven representation of different taxa in genomic databases, can further complicate model training. Furthermore, biases stemming from the overrepresentation of readily culturable organisms in existing datasets can prevent models from being applicable to diverse and environmentally relevant microbial communities (Albright \& Louca, 2023). An additional challenge lies in the interpretability of machine learning models, particularly when employing mechanism-agnostic feature selection approaches. Identifying causative genomic elements is complicated by multiple factors: the choice of genomic representation (protein families, k-mers, functional annotations) can significantly influence which features are identified as important; context dependency means that even orthologous features can behave differently across different genomic backgrounds; and high correlations among features due to shared phylogenetic history can confound the identification of truly causal relationships. While prediction accuracy is important, the ultimate goal extends beyond prediction to understanding underlying mechanisms and developing actionable insights for intervention and engineering applications. Addressing these challenges requires careful data aggregation, model design, rigorous validation strategies, and innovative approaches for feature engineering.
In this study, we aimed to systematically evaluate how feature type, data source, and feature selection strategies influence machine learning model performance for predicting microbial nutrient utilization phenotypes, with the goal of establishing best practices and benchmarks for improvement.In this study, we aimed to enhance the prediction and interpretability of microbial nutrient utilization phenotypes from genomic data, addressing the challenges of incomplete gene annotations and inconsistent training data. This test case represents an ideal scenario for evaluating machine learning approaches due to its ecological and technological importance, and because carbon utilization phenotypes involve highly conserved and well-characterized mechanisms, creating conditions where generalizability should be high and interpretability can be rigorously assessed. We compiled a comprehensive dataset of 819 microbial genomes and their binary growth outcomes across 242 carbon sources from various sources (Figure 1A). To enrich genomic annotations, we integrated information from multiple resources, including metabolic features (RAST, KOFAM) and protein functional data (UniProt), alongside de novo protein clustering. We developed phenotype-specific classifiers using this extensive dataset and employed various feature pre-processing and selection strategies within a standardized evaluation framework (Figure 1B). We hypothesized that  accurate and robust phenotype predictions would result by training on large, comprehensive datasets and combining information from multiple annotation sources . Our analysis revealed that while no single annotation source consistently outperformed others in terms of average accuracy (Figure 2B), integrating feature representations from multiple annotation sources improved model performance, leading to the most accurate models for approximately 82\% (85 out of 104) of the phenotypes (Figure 5C). Furthermore, biologically informed feature selection, despite substantially reducing the feature space, maintained prediction accuracy and improved feature interpretability (Figure 5B, Figure 6). However, our results also highlighted significant challenges: dataset biases and phylogenetic distance between training and test genomes emerged as strong determinants of model performance (Figure 3, Figure 4). Consequently, cross-dataset prediction performance remained poor regardless of the feature representation or selection strategy employed (Figure 3B, Figure 4B). These findings underscore the critical importance of dataset composition and highlight the complexities of developing universally generalizable predictive models for microbial phenotypes. Our study provides a guide for future large-scale collection of phenotype data and the analysis of factors affecting generalizability and interpretation of the metabolic capabilities of organisms from their genomes.
