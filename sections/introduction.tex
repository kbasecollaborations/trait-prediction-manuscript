\section*{Introduction}

Microbial nutrient utilisation phenotypes are key determinants of ecological niche and influence organism distribution, interactions, and functional roles within complex ecosystems \cite{Falkowski2008-bx,Malik2020Defining}.
A mechanistic understanding of these phenotypes is crucial for advancing microbial ecology, providing insights into community assembly, nutrient cycling, and biotechnological applications such as probiotic design or bioremediation \cite{Lu2014-zz}.
Traditional laboratory-based cultivation methods have characterised most existing microbial phenotype data, but these methods cannot characterise the metabolic capabilities of unculturable taxa \cite{Lloyd2018-ar}, introducing significant biases and restricting our understanding of microbial diversity.
Even for culturable organisms, comprehensive phenotypic characterisation across environmental conditions remains resource-intensive \cite{Barberan2017-xi}.

Rapid advances in genomics offer a promising path to overcome these limitations \cite{Quince2017Shotgun,Nayfach2021Catalog}.
% CITATION ADDED: metagenomics advances enabling genome-resolved analysis
The increasing availability of genomic data, including metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), presents an opportunity to predict phenotypes directly from genomic information \cite{Li2023-up}, bypassing extensive laboratory experiments.
While phylogeny has commonly been used as a proxy for phenotype prediction, it is insufficient for characterising novel organisms with limited phylogenetic context or for traits that are not phylogenetically conserved \cite{Bradley2018-rs}.
Genome-based predictions offer a powerful alternative, enabling characterisation of both cultured and uncultured taxa while potentially uncovering the genetic mechanisms driving phenotypic variation \cite{Weimann2016Traitar,Brbic2016Landscape,Karlsen2023Genotype,Lees2020Pangenome,Koblitz2025Phenotype}.
% CITATION ADDED: pangenome-spanning genotype-phenotype regressions; bacterial phenotype prediction with curated data
These predictions can leverage various genomic features, from general characteristics such as GC content \cite{Hu2022-fr} and genome size \cite{Chuckran2025-ap} to specific information derived from gene annotations and inferred metabolic pathways \cite{Long2021-yz}.
% CITATION ADDED: genome size predicts bacterial growth phenotype

Machine learning models have emerged as valuable tools for inferring phenotypes from complex genomic data, capable of handling the non-linear relationships and high dimensionality often encountered in biological systems \cite{Greener2022-dy,Zampieri2019MLMetabolic,James2025OpenProblems}.
% CITATION ADDED: review of open problems in bacterial whole-genome phenotype prediction
However, the performance and generalizability of these models are constrained by data limitations and evaluation challenges \cite{Whalen2022Pitfalls}.
% CITATION ADDED: pitfalls of ML in genomics (data quality, evaluation biases)
Limited and taxonomically biased training data can lead to overfitting and poor applicability to diverse microbial communities \cite{Ramoneda2024-yb,Albright2023-ze}.
Furthermore, phylogenetic correlations among genomic features confound the identification of mechanistically relevant predictors; Li et al.\cite{Li2023-up} showed that predictors trained on taxonomically biased data primarily capture phylogenetic signal, leading to poor cross-clade transfer, and concluded that very large datasets are required to overcome this confounding \cite{Ramoneda2024-yb,Albright2023-ze}.
This interpretation is well supported, but it leaves open an alternative possibility: that generalisation failure reflects dilution of mechanistic signal by internally inconsistent genotype--phenotype mappings \cite{Northcutt2021Confident}, rather than an inherent need for massive sample sizes.
% CITATION ADDED: confident learning framework for noisy/inconsistent labels
Given this interpretation, we sought a system where mechanistic expectations are strong, yet known representational and biological challenges remain.

We tested this hypothesis using microbial carbon utilisation as a model system.
Carbon utilisation phenotypes are well suited for this purpose because they sit near the boundary of what genome-derived features can capture.
The core biochemical transformations underlying many carbon catabolic pathways are well characterised and well-conserved, yet their genomic realisation is often ambiguous due to challenges in annotating, for example, transporters and broad-specificity enzymes, the presence of divergent or previously uncharacterised genes capable of performing similar chemistry, and regulatory mechanisms that are poorly characterised and conserved across phylogeny.
This combination of well-understood chemistry and realistic representational limitations makes carbon utilisation a stringent test of when genome-based machine learning should succeed, and when failure is expected.
We compiled a comprehensive dataset of 819 microbial genomes and their binary growth outcomes across 242 carbon sources from four independent experimental collections.
We developed phenotype-specific classifiers using this dataset and employed various feature representations and evaluation strategies within a standardised framework.

We find that models trained on concordant samples---those with internally consistent genotype--phenotype mappings---achieve robust cross-dataset generalisation, consistently recovering established pathway genes as top predictive features while also correctly classifying samples misclassified by the mechanistic predictor.
Robust generalisation can be achieved with modest numbers of high-quality training samples, suggesting that data coherence, rather than dataset size alone, is the primary constraint on phenotype prediction.
Together, these findings provide a practical framework for prioritising data curation in biological machine learning.
