\section*{Introduction}

Microbial nutrient utilization phenotypes are key determinants of ecological niche and influence organism distribution, interactions, and functional roles within complex ecosystems \cite{Falkowski2008-bx,Malik2020Defining}.
A mechanistic understanding of these phenotypes is crucial for advancing microbial ecology, providing insights into community assembly, nutrient cycling, and biotechnological applications such as probiotic design or bioremediation \cite{Lu2014-zz}.
Traditional laboratory-based cultivation methods have characterized most existing microbial phenotype data, but these methods cannot characterize the metabolic capabilities of unculturable taxa \cite{Lloyd2018-ar}, introducing significant biases and restricting our understanding of microbial diversity.
Even for culturable organisms, comprehensive phenotypic characterization across environmental conditions remains resource-intensive \cite{Barberan2017-xi}.

Rapid advances in genomics offer a promising path to overcome these limitations.
The increasing availability of genomic data, including metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), presents an opportunity to predict phenotypes directly from genomic information \cite{Li2023-up}, bypassing extensive laboratory experiments.
While phylogeny has commonly been used as a proxy for phenotype prediction, it is insufficient for characterizing novel organisms with limited phylogenetic context or for traits that are not phylogenetically conserved \cite{Bradley2018-rs}.
Genome-based predictions offer a powerful alternative, enabling characterization of both cultured and uncultured taxa while potentially uncovering the genetic mechanisms driving phenotypic variation \cite{Weimann2016Traitar,Brbic2016Landscape,Karlsen2023Genotype}.
These predictions can leverage various genomic features, from general characteristics such as GC content \cite{Hu2022-fr} and genome size to specific information derived from gene annotations and inferred metabolic pathways \cite{Long2021-yz}.

Machine learning models have emerged as valuable tools for inferring phenotypes from complex genomic data, capable of handling the non-linear relationships and high dimensionality often encountered in biological systems \cite{Greener2022-dy,Zampieri2019MLMetabolic}.
However, the performance and generalizability of these models are constrained by data limitations and evaluation challenges.
Limited and taxonomically biased training data can lead to overfitting and poor applicability to diverse microbial communities \cite{Ramoneda2024-yb,Albright2023-ze}.
Furthermore, phylogenetic correlations among genomic features confound the identification of mechanistically relevant predictors; Li et al.\cite{Li2023-up} showed that predictors trained on taxonomically biased data primarily capture phylogenetic signal, leading to poor cross-clade transfer, and concluded that very large datasets are required to overcome this confounding \cite{Ramoneda2024-yb,Albright2023-ze}.
This interpretation is well supported, but it leaves open an alternative possibility: that generalization failure reflects dilution of mechanistic signal by internally inconsistent genotype--phenotype mappings, rather than an inherent need for massive sample sizes.
Given this interpretation, we sought a system where mechanistic expectations are strong, yet known representational and biological challenges remain.

We tested this hypothesis using microbial carbon utilization as a model system.
Carbon utilization phenotypes are well suited for this purpose because they sit near the boundary of what genome-derived features can capture.
The core biochemical transformations underlying many carbon catabolic pathways are well characterized and well-conserved, yet their genomic realization is often ambiguous due to challenges in annotating, for example, transporters and broad-specificity enzymes, the presence of divergent or previously uncharacterized genes capable of performing similar chemistry, and regulatory mechanisms that are poorly characterized and conserved across phylogeny.
This combination of well-understood chemistry and realistic representational limitations makes carbon utilization a stringent test of when genome-based machine learning should succeed, and when failure is expected.
We compiled a comprehensive dataset of 819 microbial genomes and their binary growth outcomes across 242 carbon sources from four independent experimental collections.
We developed phenotype-specific classifiers using this dataset and employed various feature representations and evaluation strategies within a standardized framework.

We show that models trained on concordant samples---those with internally consistent genotype--phenotype mappings---achieve robust cross-dataset generalization, consistently recovering established pathway genes as top predictive features while also correctly classifying samples misclassified by the mechanistic predictor.
Robust generalization can be achieved with modest numbers of high-quality training samples, suggesting that data coherence, rather than dataset size alone, is the primary constraint on phenotype prediction.
Together, these findings provide a practical framework for prioritizing data curation in biological machine learning.
