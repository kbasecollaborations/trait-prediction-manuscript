\section*{Materials and Methods}

\subsection*{Carbon Source Utilisation Datasets}

We obtained four binary growth datasets on defined carbon sources: ATLeaf~\cite{Schafer2023-nk}, Biolog~\cite{BiologDatasetCitation}, Marine~\cite{Gralka2023-ft}, and Populus~\cite{PopulusDatasetCitation} (assay details in Supplementary Text~S1 and Table~\ref{tab:dataset_overview}).
These contributed 819 input strain records, of which 795 had a genome assembly available for annotation.
Genome quality (CheckM2) and pangenome completeness were assessed as covariates rather than applied as filters (Supplementary Text~S2; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:pangenome_completeness}).
We aligned phenotype and genomic-feature identifiers, yielding 780 matched strains eligible for feature-based analyses.
Sample sizes varied by phenotype because not every strain was measured for every phenotype.
Carbon source names were standardised and analyses were restricted to the 15 phenotypes shared across all four datasets.
A complementary data-volume comparison for 13 phenotypes additionally drew training samples from a fixed BacDive snapshot~\cite{Schober2025BacDive} (Supplementary Text~S11).

\subsection*{Genome Annotation}

KofamScan v1.3.0 \cite{Aramaki2020KofamKOALA} assigned KEGG Orthologs (KOs) using KOFAM profile hidden Markov models from the KEGG database \cite{Kanehisa2000KEGG}.
GapMind~\cite{Price2022GapMindCarbon,Price2020GapMind} predicted carbon-utilisation pathway completeness, scored 0 (incomplete) to 2 (high confidence); it was run from the PaperBLAST suite with USEARCH~\cite{Edgar2010} and HMMer~\cite{Eddy2011} (Supplementary Text~S3).
Rapid Annotations using Subsystems Technology (RAST) \cite{Aziz2008RAST} provided functional predictions, parsed to extract subsystem ontology terms.

\subsection*{Phylogenetic Tree Construction}

We assigned taxonomy with GTDB-Tk~\cite{Chaumeil2022GTDBTk} (GTDB release v220) and placed genomes into the GTDB~\cite{Parks2018GTDB} reference tree using \texttt{classify\_wf}.
We extracted the corresponding subtree and computed distances between tips, the sum of branch lengths along the connecting path, with ETE 3 \cite{HuertaCepas2016ETE3}.
Genomes lacking species-level assignments were placed at genus or family level.

\subsection*{Baseline Predictors}

We evaluated GapMind as a mechanistic baseline using two binary thresholds on pathway-completeness calls, strict (only ``complete'' positive) and permissive (``complete'' and ``likely complete'' positive), with all other calls negative.
A phylogenetic $k$-nearest-neighbour classifier assigned phenotypes by majority vote among the $k=3$ closest training relatives.
Null baselines comprised an identity model (predicting the most common training class) and a Bernoulli model (sampling from observed training class probabilities).

\subsection*{Data Pre-processing and Feature Selection}

KOFAM, GapMind, and RAST annotations were converted into binary presence/absence matrices (GapMind pathway scores retained integer values).
Two unsupervised pre-processing filters were applied to the combined feature matrix across datasets: variance filtering (scikit-learn \cite{Pedregosa2011Sklearn} VarianceThreshold at 0.01) and correlation filtering (Spearman $\rho > 0.95$, graph-based, removing the most connected features).

\subsection*{Machine Learning Classifiers}

We trained gradient-boosted tree classifiers using CatBoost~\cite{Prokhorenkova2018CatBoost} (v1.2.8), which performed as well as or better than a range of alternative model families (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:best_models_counts}).
The CatBoost hyperparameters were tuned on performance across all phenotypes (1000 iterations, learning rate 0.03, tree depth 4; Supplementary Text~S3).
Random holdout evaluations used five 70:15:15 train/validation/test partitions generated independently for each phenotype and stratified by its binary growth label (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}A).
Cross-dataset and phylogenetic evaluations used a single 85:15 train:validation partition on the non-test data.

\subsection*{Evaluation Strategies}

\subsubsection*{Random Holdout and Cross-Dataset Testing}

Performance was evaluated under four scenarios: random holdout within the aggregated dataset; leave-one-dataset-out cross-dataset; and in-clade and out-of-clade phylogenetic splits.
Exact-duplicate feature vectors were rare and did not produce train--test overlap in leave-one-dataset-out evaluation (Supplementary Text~S3).
Phenotype--dataset combinations with fewer than 10 minority-class test samples were excluded to prevent balanced-accuracy collapse; cross-dataset missing features were imputed as zero.

\subsubsection*{Phylogenetic Partitioning}

We applied the out-of-clade partitioning algorithm of Li et al.~\cite{Li2023-up}, which selects phylogenetically separated test clades (12--18\% of samples).
For cross-dataset evaluation, an in-clade test subset was instead defined by retaining test genomes whose average-linkage cluster of the phylogenetic distance matrix contained at least two training genomes (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C; distances by split type in Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:phylogenetic_distance_splits}).

\subsection*{Concordant and Discordant Sample Analysis}

Samples were stratified by agreement between GapMind permissive-threshold predictions and experimental outcomes: concordant (GapMind matches the experiment) or discordant (GapMind contradicts it); 71.9\% of labelled samples were concordant across the 15 phenotypes and four datasets.

\subsection*{Feature Importance Analysis}

Stable predictive features were KOFAM features that recurred among the top ten by mean absolute Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) value across repeated models (Supplementary Text~S7).
Biologically redundant KOs were grouped post hoc into SHAP-supervised clusters before features were compared across datasets; clustering did not affect model fitting, predictions, or reported metrics (Figures~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}C and~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; Tables~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison} and~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).

\subsection*{Data Quality Filtering Strategies}

Two additional filtering strategies were evaluated beyond concordant sample filtering, both applied to training and validation samples only, never to the held-out test set.
Problematic-sample removal excluded universal non-growers with any GapMind growth prediction, universal growers with incomplete GapMind pathways, and the twenty most frequently GapMind-misclassified genomes.
Confidence-based weighting combined phylogenetic neighbour agreement (training labels only), GapMind pathway completeness, and experimental outcome.
Four score configurations spanning $w_{\mathrm{gap}} \in \{0, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5\}$ were evaluated, including a mechanism-free setting ($w_{\mathrm{gap}} = 0$) (full formula and sample weights in Supplementary Text~S9).
All conditions in Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B were evaluated on the same full cross-dataset held-out test set.

\subsection*{Sample Size Requirements}

Training-data requirements were assessed for all 15 shared phenotypes by fitting full-data and concordance-trained KOFAM models to stratified subsets of 25, 50, 100, 200, and all available samples (three repeats) under random-holdout and cross-dataset evaluation, scored on full, concordant, and discordant test subsets.
Subsets that retained only one class were skipped.

\subsection*{Reliability and Experiment-Prioritisation Analyses}

For full-data and concordance-trained models, prediction confidence was defined as $\max(p,\,1-p)$ and used as a ranking score; calibration was reported separately via reliability diagrams and expected calibration error (ECE).
The receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC) for confidence-based discrimination of correct from incorrect predictions was pooled across phenotypes~\cite{Pedregosa2011Sklearn}.
Risk--coverage curves retained genomes in decreasing order of confidence, scoring the retained subset by accuracy against the phenotype's majority-class rate, since balanced accuracy is undefined when the most-confident genomes share one true class.
Mean confidence and the fraction of high-confidence predictions were Spearman-correlated with per-phenotype cross-dataset balanced accuracy (Supplementary Text~S8).

\subsection*{Statistical Analysis}

Paired comparisons of balanced accuracy, precision, and recall across phenotypes used two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on phenotype-level means (\texttt{scipy.stats.\allowbreak wilcoxon}; Figures~\ref{fig:ml_performance}, \ref{fig:concordant_analysis}, and \ref{fig:improvement_strategies}).
For the experiment-prioritisation comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C,D), seeds were averaged within phenotype--held-out-dataset cells, with a paired test of the six phenotype means as a sensitivity analysis.
Per-phenotype label-free aggregates were related to cross-dataset balanced accuracy by Spearman rank correlation (\texttt{scipy.stats.\allowbreak spearmanr}; Supplementary Text~S8).
Thirteen primary tests (eleven Wilcoxon, two Spearman) were jointly adjusted by the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure at a false-discovery rate of 0.05; raw $p$-values are reported in the main text with adjusted $q$-values for borderline tests, and comparisons outside this family are unadjusted.
Summary statistics are mean $\pm$ standard deviation across folds or repeats unless otherwise noted.
