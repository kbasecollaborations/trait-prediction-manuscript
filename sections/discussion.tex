\section*{Discussion}

Machine learning models that predict microbial phenotypes from genomic features generalise poorly across datasets~\cite{Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis,Whalen2022Pitfalls}, a limitation typically attributed to limited sample size, taxonomic bias, or feature-space artefacts~\cite{Li2023-up,Yu2025BiasedSampling,Leek2010BatchEffects}.
Across four binary growth datasets and the 15 carbon sources assayed in all of them, we identify label--mechanism agreement in the training set as a determinant of cross-dataset generalisation that complements the established roles of sample size, phylogenetic structure, and feature-space choice.


\subsection*{Label--Mechanism Agreement Governs Cross-Dataset Transfer Within a Bounded Domain}

Poor cross-dataset performance was better explained by mixed-quality training samples and dataset-specific correlations that did not transfer than by clade structure, which accounted for little of the failure (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C).
Restricting training to samples whose experimental labels agreed with mechanistic predictions (concordance filtering) improved cross-dataset feature stability.
Although the classifiers have no mechanistic knowledge, concordant training led them to converge across datasets on curated pathway genes (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}).

Discordance has several coexisting causes~\cite{Galperin1998Sources,Bernstein2021Uncertainty}: false negatives may reflect annotation gaps or unknown alternative pathways, whereas false positives may reflect regulatory repression or experimental artefacts such as differences in growth-assay modality across the source datasets, that static genomic features cannot capture (Figure~\ref{fig:misclassification_diagnosis}A).
The model correctly predicted growth for some of the discordant genomes, most of which were metabolic generalists carrying broad-specificity transporters and sugar-catabolism regulators.
GapMind excludes such genes by design, since a broad transporter's presence does not establish that it serves the focal substrate.
Across the whole cross-dataset test set the concordance-trained model did not exceed GapMind, and on concordant genomes it cannot (by definition).
The gain is therefore limited to the genomes GapMind gets wrong.
Among those, the rule returns a single verdict and cannot tell them apart, whereas the model assigns higher growth probabilities to the genomes that do grow, most clearly where the annotated pathway looked incomplete (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C,D).
The model adds to the rule rather than replacing it, and the discordant genomes removed by filtering are the ones that carry this information.

Li et al.\ reported that datasets exceeding approximately 1000 genomes can enable out-of-clade prediction for some traits~\cite{Li2023-up}, whereas Yu et al.\ found that increasing sample size did not rescue generalisation under phylogenetically biased sampling~\cite{Yu2025BiasedSampling}.
Within our sample-size regime (up to 780 strains), the heterogeneity of training samples rather than their number alone drove shortcut learning, extending that second result from taxonomic to mechanistic heterogeneity.
Curated data quality is known to improve trait prediction~\cite{Koblitz2025Phenotype}, but concordance is a criterion of a different kind.
Discordant records are not necessarily erroneous, since a genome may grow through an uncharacterised route or fail to grow despite a complete pathway, so concordance removes mechanistic heterogeneity rather than error.
Increasing data volume did not improve generalisation, since adding $\sim$1800 BacDive genomes per phenotype~\cite{Schober2025BacDive} did not improve cross-dataset performance over a roughly four-fold smaller concordant training set and reduced feature reproducibility (Supplementary Text~S11).


\subsection*{Practical Deployment and Confidence-Guided Experimentation}

Concordance filtering cannot be applied without a mechanistic predictor for the phenotype in question, which exists only for well-characterised pathways.
One alternative is to weight samples by phylogenetic label agreement, which requires no pathway annotations and increased true-positive recovery, though with lower specificity and little balanced-accuracy change from unfiltered ML (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B,C)~\cite{Northcutt2021Confident,Bernhardt2022LabelCleaning}.
It is therefore recall-oriented rather than a substitute for concordance filtering.
When GapMind is available for every genome, a simpler option is to restrict a full-data model to the predictions that agree with it, which reached 0.77 cross-dataset balanced accuracy on the 72\% of held-out predictions retained.
However, unlike concordant training, this requires GapMind for every new genome rather than only for the training set.
A second constraint is how much labelled data these models need, measured on cross-dataset tests since random holdout overestimates generalisation~\cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation}.
A small concordant training set is enough to indicate whether a phenotype is learnable from genomic features, since 50 to 100 concordant samples reached at least 90\% of the all-available-sample performance for most shared phenotypes (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sample_size_phenotypes}).
Because the earliest-saturating phenotypes were also the least predictable, further measurement is worth committing mainly where that early signal exists.

Committing that measurement effort and deciding whether to trust a prediction on a genome whose concordance is unknown require a signal that does not depend on GapMind, and model confidence provides one, flagging which predictions to withhold~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject}.
Among the strategies we compared for choosing genomes to label, selecting the least-confident ones produced the largest mean transfer gain, generally concentrated in the weakest phenotypes, whereas diversity and novelty-based selection did not beat random (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}C,D).
High-throughput phenotyping methods~\cite{Nichols2011Landscape,Gralka2023-ft,Huang2023-lh,Kehe2019-vr} could scale this loop, directing new measurements towards low-confidence genomes and under-sampled clades in the weakest-transferring phenotypes rather than adding depth where performance has already saturated.
At the phenotype level, however, no label-free metric we tested correlated with cross-dataset accuracy.
Phenotype difficulty appears instead to be a property of the feature space rather than of the model, since confidence reflects agreement among the patterns a model has already seen and cannot register a mechanism that the annotations do not encode or that occurs too rarely to be learned.

\subsection*{Limitations and Future Directions}

Concordance-based training requires mechanistic tools such as GapMind, available only for well-characterised pathways, and mechanism-free weighting avoids that requirement only by trading specificity for recall while still needing experimental labels.
Genome-scale metabolic models could extend concordance filtering to a much larger set of carbon substrates, though this would require curated or optimised reconstructions, since automatically constructed constraint-based models have been reported to predict carbon utilisation little better than chance for non-model organisms~\cite{Li2023-up}.
Filtering also extends the coverage--clarity tradeoff to taxonomy, favouring pathways in the annotation space and organisms with broadly conserved enzymes while excluding those with alternate or unknown enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulation~\cite{Soucy2015HGT,Glasner2020Promiscuity,Gorke2008CCR}.
All analyses rely on genomic annotations, which carry systematic biases across taxa~\cite{Karlsen2023Genotype,Schnoes2009Annotation,Lobb2020AnnotationCoverage}.
Apparent concordance may therefore be lower in poorly studied lineages because of annotation gaps rather than different biology, biasing the approach towards well-studied genera.
Closing those gaps will require data from outside the annotation space, such as transcriptomic or regulatory profiles~\cite{Arikan2023MultiOmics}, together with targeted characterisation of organisms outside the applicability domain, for which the low-confidence ranking of Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction} provides a starting point.
Sequence-based language models offer a complementary route, since genomic and protein language models learn functional and regulatory relationships directly from sequence rather than from a curated ortholog vocabulary~\cite{Nguyen2024Evo,Hwang2024GenomicLM}, and protein-embedding classifiers have predicted bacterial phenotypes on taxa where database matching fails~\cite{FerrerFlorensa2026PathogenFinder}.

In summary, cross-dataset generalisation improved when training labels agreed with mechanistic predictions, but the resulting models are specialists that complement a curated pathway rule rather than replacing it, adding information mainly where the rule errs.
Label--mechanism agreement is therefore a diagnosable and partially mitigable driver of shortcut learning, alongside sample volume and feature-space choice.
Reliable genome-based phenotype prediction will therefore require curating training data on that criterion and reporting the domain in which a model applies.
Which phenotypes are tractable at all, however, cannot be read from a model's accuracy or its confidence, and must be established empirically.
Extending the approach beyond well-characterised pathways will require a label-quality criterion that does not depend on a curated mechanistic predictor; phylogenetic agreement alone did not provide one, making ensemble disagreement and confidence-based label filtering~\cite{Northcutt2021Confident} the natural candidates to test.
