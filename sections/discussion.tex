\section*{Discussion}

% Summary of findings
In our cross-dataset analyses, poor generalisation was not explained by the learning algorithm alone; performance improved mainly when we restricted training to samples with coherent genotype--phenotype relationships.
GapMind--experiment discordance likely reflects incomplete representation of known mechanisms, or biology not captured by genome-derived features such as regulation or environmental context.
When such samples dominate the training set, models fit correlations that hold within a dataset or clade but do not transfer, suggesting that data curation is a larger constraint than further algorithmic tuning~\cite{Mey2021Improving}.

% What constitutes high-quality data
We treated samples as high quality when mechanistic predictions and measured growth agreed, because those samples were more likely to reflect pathways already represented in the annotation space.
Concordant samples tend to involve organisms that use broadly conserved, well-characterised pathways, so filtering favours features that transfer across organisms rather than clade-specific correlates.
For any given carbon source, however, a proportion of organisms employ alternative enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulatory strategies \cite{Soucy2015HGT,Glasner2020Promiscuity}; when such organisms are included in training, the model lacks mechanistic signal and instead relies on phylogenetic or dataset-specific correlates that do not generalise.
Data quality extends beyond mechanistic concordance to encompass genome completeness and annotation consistency \cite{Bowers2017MIMAG,Parks2015CheckM}, as well as measurement reproducibility and standardised phenotype calling.
Discordance therefore includes both recoverable cases, such as annotation gaps, and cases in which the relevant mechanism is not represented in the available data.

% Interpreting discordances
Concordant-trained models reached only 0.18--0.60 balanced accuracy on discordant samples, which argues against a single explanation for discordance~\cite{Galperin1998Sources,Snitkin2008Model}.
Although these interpretations remain speculative without experimental validation, false negatives (GapMind--/Experiment+) may reflect annotation gaps or unknown alternative pathways. False positives (GapMind+/Experiment--) may instead reflect regulatory repression or experimental artefacts that static genomic features cannot capture.
Dataset-specific discordance patterns may reflect differences in experimental protocols, measurement sensitivity, or the biological composition of sampled communities; disentangling these contributions would require controlled cross-laboratory comparisons, but the variation already motivates concrete follow-up such as protocol comparisons, phenotype-calling checks, and clade-specific error analysis.

% Relationship to previous work
Previous work has demonstrated that ML models learn phylogenetic signals, concluding that massive datasets ($>$1000 genomes) are needed to overcome confounding~\cite{Li2023-up}.
In our data, phylogenetic confounding appeared strongest when training included both concordant and discordant samples, and weaker after concordance filtering, likely because concordant samples capture conserved pathways shared across lineages rather than clade-specific patterns~\cite{Collins2018GWAS,Yu2025BiasedSampling}.
Sample size and data quality therefore interact: adding mixed-quality samples was less helpful than selecting samples with coherent mechanistic signal \cite{Martiny2013-ob,Martiny2015-ie}.
Biologically informed feature selection improved interpretability without sacrificing accuracy~\cite{Li2023-up}, but this benefit depended on training-data quality: comprehensive feature sets improved within-dataset accuracy, whereas pathway-linked features paired with concordance filtering performed better across datasets.
Limited feature overlap across datasets further indicates that dataset-specific biases dominate when data quality is mixed~\cite{Leek2010BatchEffects}.

% Specialisation versus coverage
A concordant-trained model is necessarily a specialist: by retaining organisms whose phenotype the known pathway already explains, concordance filtering restricts the training distribution, and therefore the model's applicability domain, to known-mechanism organisms~\cite{Zadrozny2004SelectionBias}.
Such a model cannot predict, or discover, mechanisms absent from that distribution.
This is a real limitation, but it is not one that an unfiltered model escapes: a novel mechanism is typically confined to a few organisms within a single lineage or dataset, so a model trained on the full data does not learn it as a transferable rule but instead memorises those organisms as phylogenetic correlates, which is why apparent performance on such samples collapses under cross-dataset evaluation.
The practical choice is therefore not between a specialist and a general predictor, but between a model that is honestly bounded and one whose breadth is an artefact of shortcut learning; concordance filtering exposes this limit rather than creating it.

% Addressing circularity
A natural concern is that using GapMind-defined concordance could bias the retained samples toward the same canonical pathways encoded by the model features.
Two observations argue against this reading.
First, the concordant-trained models used KOFAM annotations as inputs while retaining the GapMind concordance criterion for sample selection alone, so the feature space was decoupled from the rule set used to define concordance.
Second, concordant-trained models retained intermediate accuracy on the natural-composition (concordant plus discordant) held-out test set (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:full_held_out_test}), classifying samples that the mechanistic predictor itself misclassifies and indicating that the learned signal extends beyond the curated rule set used to define concordance.

% Practical implications
These results suggest several practical choices for building phenotype predictors.
Changing the training set and feature representation mattered more than changing the classifier~\cite{Zha2025DataCentric,Whang2023DataQuality,Sambasivan2021DataCascades}, and ML models should be trained on high-quality samples with clear genotype--phenotype mappings when possible.
When mechanistic predictors, such as GapMind, are available, concordant samples provide the highest-quality training data; when they are unavailable, confidence-based filtering and removal of systematically problematic samples offer practical alternatives \cite{Bernhardt2022LabelCleaning}.
Feature stability across independent training sets can help distinguish mechanistic from spurious learning.
Cross-dataset and out-of-clade evaluation provide realistic performance estimates~\cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation}, whereas within-dataset cross-validation overestimates generalisation by a substantial margin.
For deployment on genomes whose concordance cannot be established in advance, the model's own prediction confidence and its agreement with the mechanistic predictor provide label-free reliability signals: abstaining on the least-confident genomes raised pooled balanced accuracy from 0.71 to 0.79 at half coverage, and predictions on which GapMind and the model disagreed were close to random (0.49; Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}).
These signals define a practical applicability domain for genome-based phenotype prediction~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Papangelou2025Conformal,Hendrickx2024Reject} and, because they require no experimental outcome, can in principle extend to traits for which no mechanistic predictor is available.
However, when test conditions are expected to resemble training conditions, such as predictions within the same experimental system or phylogenetic context, full-dataset training maximises performance and may be the preferred approach.
Sample-size requirements depend on phenotype complexity but were modest across the 15 shared carbon-utilisation phenotypes analysed here (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}), pointing to coherent labels rather than raw scale as the binding constraint.
For experimental design, standardising growth conditions, measurement protocols, and phenotype-calling criteria is essential if datasets are to be aggregated, and replicate measurements with positive and negative controls would help flag unreliable calls.
Emerging high-throughput phenotyping methods~\cite{Bochner2001PM,Nichols2011Landscape,Gralka2023-ft,Huang2023-lh,Kehe2019-vr} will accelerate data collection, but quality must be prioritised alongside quantity.

Our findings also highlight a broader limitation of genome-only prediction: models can learn only mechanisms represented in the annotations and training labels.
A curated mechanistic model enabled us to condition the learning problem on samples governed by shared, well-encoded pathways, but many phenotypes depend on regulatory, temporal, or contextual processes that genome sequence alone cannot capture.
Without alignment between question, data, and representation, larger datasets alone are unlikely to solve the problem.
% Limitations
Our framework has important constraints.
The filtering approach requires mechanistic tools such as GapMind, which exist only for well-characterised metabolic pathways; extending it to traits lacking comparable predictors (e.g., stress tolerance, virulence) will require alternative quality-assessment strategies.
We restricted the analysis to binary growth/no-growth outcomes, and quantitative traits may require different filtering strategies.
By selecting samples whose phenotypes match the known pathway, we may exclude organisms that use alternative routes; the partial predictability of discordant samples suggests this is a real limitation.
Concordance filtering may also introduce taxonomic bias toward well-studied genera, where annotation databases are most complete, so part of the cross-dataset gain could reflect broader taxonomic representation rather than purely mechanistic transferability (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomic_bias}).
Finally, all analyses rely on computational genome annotations, which carry systematic biases across taxa~\cite{Karlsen2023Genotype,Schnoes2009Annotation}; poorly studied lineages may show lower concordance because of annotation gaps rather than different biology.
Cross-dataset feature alignment by zero-imputation also conflates true gene absence with cross-pipeline annotation mismatch, potentially underestimating transferability for phenotypes whose mechanistic genes are inconsistently called.

% Conclusion
Models transferred best when training data were mechanistically coherent and the feature space matched known pathway biology.
Genome-only models nevertheless face a ceiling set by what current annotations encode: for organisms whose phenotypes depend on poorly characterised enzymes, transporters, or regulatory programs, scale alone cannot recover signal that is not represented.
Future work should test whether concordance-based filtering generalises to other trait classes (antibiotic resistance, environmental tolerance, host interactions) where comparable mechanistic predictors exist; develop label-free alternatives such as ensemble disagreement or prediction-confidence filtering for traits without such predictors~\cite{Northcutt2021Confident}; and integrate transcriptomic or regulatory data~\cite{Arikan2023MultiOmics} to capture the context-dependence that static genomic features miss.
Clades with elevated discordance rates also warrant experimental follow-up as candidate sources of novel metabolic strategies.
Progress in genotype--phenotype prediction will therefore depend less on larger datasets or more elaborate models than on infrastructure that exposes, curates, and conditions on coherent mappings within appropriately matched feature spaces.
