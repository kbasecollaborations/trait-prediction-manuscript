\section*{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Core Findings}

\textbf{Key finding:} \textit{Data quality, not algorithmic limitations, fundamentally constrains genotype-phenotype prediction.}

We restate the central finding: ML models achieve robust generalization when trained on high-quality (concordant) data, but fail systematically when trained on mixed-quality data. The problem is not that ML cannot learn metabolic mechanisms---it demonstrably can when given clean signal. Rather, discordant samples force models toward spurious correlations that happen to be predictive within specific datasets/clades but do not transfer. This reframes the ``generalizability crisis'' in biological ML from an algorithmic challenge to a data curation challenge.

\subsection{Interpretation: What Discordances Reveal About Genotype-Phenotype Mapping}

\textbf{Key finding:} \textit{Discordant samples reflect a mixture of technical artifacts and genuine biological complexity.}

The partial predictability of discordant samples (BACC 0.18--0.4) suggests heterogeneous causes---some discordances are ``recoverable'' (annotation gaps where mechanism exists but wasn't detected), others are not (regulation, alternative pathways, measurement error). Directional bias in discordances is informative:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{GapMind--/Experiment+ (false negatives):} likely annotation gaps, divergent homologs, or unknown alternative pathways---these represent opportunities for biological discovery
\item \textbf{GapMind+/Experiment-- (false positives):} likely regulatory repression, missing cofactors/transporters, or experimental artifacts---these represent the complexity that static genomic features cannot capture
\end{itemize}

Dataset-specific discordance patterns likely reflect differences in experimental protocols, growth conditions, and measurement sensitivity rather than fundamental biological differences. The concentration of discordances in specific genomes or phenotypes provides a roadmap for targeted data quality improvements.

\subsection{Relationship to Previous Work on Phylogenetic Confounding}

\textbf{Key finding:} \textit{Our findings extend and reframe previous observations about phylogenetic bias in trait prediction.}

Previous work (cite Li et al. and others) demonstrated that ML models learn phylogenetic signal, concluding that massive datasets ($>$1000 genomes) are needed to overcome confounding. Our results provide an alternative interpretation: phylogenetic confounding emerges specifically when genuine mechanistic signal is diluted by discordant samples. When trained on concordant samples only, models identify mechanistic features and generalize across clades---suggesting the problem is signal-to-noise ratio, not sample size per se. This does not contradict previous findings but provides a mechanistic explanation: mixed-quality data creates a situation where phylogenetic markers become the most consistent predictive signal. Implication: strategic data curation may be more valuable than brute-force data aggregation.

\subsection{Practical Implications: Building Generalizable Phenotype Predictors}

\textbf{Key finding:} \textit{Rigorous evaluation and quality diagnostics enable generalizable predictions even without mechanistic knowledge.}

\textbf{For computational biologists building models:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Within-dataset cross-validation provides inflated performance estimates---always evaluate with cross-dataset and/or out-of-clade holdouts
\item Feature stability across independent training sets is a diagnostic for mechanistic versus spurious learning
\item Samples consistently misclassified across multiple feature sets and algorithms may indicate data quality issues rather than model failures
\end{itemize}

\textbf{For experimentalists generating phenotype data:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Standardization of growth conditions, measurement protocols, and phenotype calling criteria is essential for aggregatable data
\item Including positive and negative controls enables quality assessment
\item Replicate measurements would enable identification of unreliable phenotype calls
\end{itemize}

\textbf{For the field broadly:}
\begin{itemize}
\item The value of a phenotype dataset depends not just on size but on the reliability of genotype-phenotype relationships it contains
\item A smaller, high-quality dataset may outperform a larger, noisier dataset for training generalizable models
\item Mechanistic concordance (where available) provides a principled approach to quality filtering, but evaluation-based diagnostics can serve similar purposes when mechanisms are unknown
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Key finding:} \textit{Our framework has important constraints that bound its applicability.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dependence on mechanistic tools:} Our filtering approach requires tools like GapMind, which exist only for well-characterized metabolic pathways. Extension to traits lacking comparable mechanistic predictors (e.g., stress tolerance, virulence, complex phenotypes) requires development of alternative quality assessment approaches.
\item \textbf{Binary phenotypes only:} We restricted analysis to growth/no-growth outcomes. Quantitative traits (growth rates, yields, substrate affinities) introduce continuous measurement noise that may require different filtering strategies.
\item \textbf{Sample size reduction:} Filtering to concordant samples reduces training data substantially (typically 40--60\% retention). For rare phenotypes with limited measurements, this may be prohibitive---though our results suggest that small high-quality datasets can outperform larger noisy ones.
\item \textbf{Circularity concern:} By filtering to samples where mechanism-based predictions match experiments, we may be selecting for samples where the known pathway is the actual mechanism, potentially missing organisms using alternative routes. The partial predictability of discordant samples (BACC 0.18--0.4) suggests this is a real limitation.
\item \textbf{Annotation dependence:} All our analyses rely on computational genome annotations, which have systematic biases across taxa. Poorly studied lineages may have lower concordance rates due to annotation gaps rather than genuinely different biology.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Directions}

\textbf{Key finding:} \textit{Characterizing discordances points toward specific research priorities and methodological advances.}

\textbf{Biological discovery from discordances:}
\begin{itemize}
\item False negative discordances (GapMind--/Experiment+) for specific phenotypes may indicate undiscovered alternative pathways---systematic investigation of these cases could expand pathway databases
\item Clades with elevated discordance rates may harbor novel metabolic strategies worth experimental characterization
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Improving data quality at the source:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Development of standardized phenotyping protocols optimized for ML training
\item Inclusion of technical replicates to enable measurement reliability assessment
\item Better integration of experimental metadata to enable condition-aware prediction
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Methodological advances:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Methods for identifying high-quality training samples without mechanistic knowledge---potentially using ensemble disagreement, prediction confidence, or cross-validation stability
\item Transfer learning approaches that leverage concordant samples to initialize models that can then learn from noisier data
\item Integration of gene expression or regulatory information to capture context-dependence that static genomic features miss
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Extension to other phenotypes:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Testing whether concordance-based filtering generalizes to other trait types (antibiotic resistance, environmental tolerance, host interactions)
\item Development of mechanistic prediction tools for additional phenotype classes to enable similar quality filtering
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

\textbf{Key finding:} \textit{The path to generalizable phenotype prediction runs through data quality, not algorithmic complexity.}

We demonstrated that ML successfully learns mechanistic genotype-phenotype relationships when provided with high-quality training data. Generalization failures reflect data quality limitations---discordant samples where genotype-phenotype relationships are obscured by annotation gaps, regulatory complexity, or measurement error. Rather than pursuing ever-larger datasets or more sophisticated algorithms, the field should prioritize: (1) rigorous evaluation using cross-dataset and cross-phylogeny holdouts, (2) quality diagnostics based on feature stability and biological relevance, and (3) strategic curation of training data to maximize signal-to-noise ratio. These principles, validated here for microbial carbon utilization, provide a framework applicable to diverse biological prediction challenges where data quality varies and mechanistic knowledge is incomplete.
