\section*{Discussion}

Predicting microbial phenotypes from genomic data underpins ecological characterisation, the study of uncultured taxa, and various biotechnological applications.
However, machine learning models trained on genomic features generalise poorly across datasets~\cite{Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis,Whalen2022Pitfalls}, a limitation typically attributed to limited sample size or feature-space artefacts~\cite{Li2023-up,Yu2025BiasedSampling,Leek2010BatchEffects}.
By integrating four binary growth datasets across 819 bacterial genomes and 242 carbon sources, we identify label--mechanism agreement in the training set as a determinant of cross-dataset generalisation that complements the established roles of sample size and feature-space choice.
Models trained on concordant samples behaved as bounded specialists rather than universal models. However, the model's prediction confidence helped guide which additional measurements most improved model performance for the lowest-performing phenotypes.
These results reframe genome-based phenotype prediction as a problem of training-set curation and applicability-domain definition~\cite{Sambasivan2021DataCascades,Zha2025DataCentric}, alongside the data-volume and algorithm-choice axes.

\subsection*{Label--Mechanism Agreement Governs Cross-Dataset Transfer}

Poor cross-dataset performance was generally observed when mixed-quality training samples drove models to fit dataset-specific correlations that do not transfer; phylogeny alone explained little of the failure, with in-clade and regular cross-dataset accuracies nearly identical (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), placing the source of generalisation loss outside clade structure.
Restricting training to samples where experimental labels agreed with mechanistic  (concordance filtering) improved cross-dataset recall and feature stability across datasets.
This shifts the diagnostic question from ``how many samples'' to ``which training subset'', indicating that data curation and applicability-domain definition can be as important as additional training samples~\cite{Sambasivan2021DataCascades,Zha2025DataCentric,Whang2023DataQuality}.

\subsection*{Concordance-Trained Models Are Bounded Specialists}

Concordance filtering implicitly defines an applicability domain, with the concordant-trained model's behaviour on discordant samples revealing what lies inside versus outside it.
The wide phenotype-level range in cross-dataset balanced accuracy is consistent with multiple coexisting causes of discordance~\cite{Galperin1998Sources,Bernstein2021Uncertainty}: false negatives (GapMind--/Experiment+) may reflect annotation gaps or unknown alternative pathways, whereas false positives (GapMind+/Experiment--) may reflect regulatory repression or experimental artefacts that static genomic features cannot capture.
This asymmetry played out in practice: the concordant-trained model rescued GapMind false negatives at roughly twice the rate of false positives (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}D).
Annotation-gap false negatives carry genomic content that the broader KOFAM feature set can recover even when GapMind's curated pathway-completeness rule misses it, while false positives driven by regulation or experimental conditions leave no genomic signature for the model to recover.
Because the rescue rate is non-zero, concordance filtering discards some discordant samples carrying transferable signal.

Previous work concluded that overcoming phylogenetic confounding requires $>$1000 genomes~\cite{Li2023-up,Yu2025BiasedSampling}; within our sample-size regime (795 retained genomes, below this threshold), our findings suggest that the heterogeneity of training samples, rather than their number alone, drives shortcut learning.
In our data, feature stability roughly doubled under concordance filtering (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B); models converged on the same predictors across datasets, consistent with concordant samples overrepresenting organisms using conserved, well-annotated pathways~\cite{Martiny2013-ob}.
The concordant-trained model is consequently a specialist that reliably recovers known mechanisms (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}) while still classifying roughly 40\% of GapMind false negatives whose mechanisms leave transferable genomic signatures.
Inside its applicability domain, however, KOFAM features encoded organism-specific enzyme and transporter annotations less reliably than GapMind's hand-curated pathway rules, leaving a residual gap most pronounced on glucose.
Its advantage over GapMind was visible under random holdout and as the partial rescue of GapMind false negatives in the discordant subset, but averaged out across the unstratified cross-dataset test (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C), supporting use of the concordant specialist where applicability-domain signals are available.
In cross-dataset evaluations, phenotype-filtered GapMind features surpassed extensive multi-annotation feature sets (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}D), yet they did not perform as well in random holdout tests. This discrepancy arises because the machine learning models depend on dataset-specific cues when they encounter a broad feature space with confounding signals.

\subsection*{Practical Deployment and Confidence-Guided Experimentation}

Deploying a concordant specialist raises two practical questions: whether a mechanism-free filter can substitute when GapMind (mechanistic information) is unavailable, and how much labelled training data the specialist requires; both must be answered on cross-dataset tests since random holdout overestimates generalisation~\cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation}.
The mechanism-free filter, combining phylogenetic neighbour agreement and experimental-label consistency, reached cross-dataset balanced accuracy between the unfiltered baseline and concordance training, with the difference from concordance not statistically significant (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies})~\cite{Northcutt2021Confident,Bernhardt2022LabelCleaning}.
Despite this balanced-accuracy equivalence, the two filters diverge where replacing GapMind matters most: concordance training improved recall over GapMind on most phenotypes (12 of 15; a borderline trend after multiple-comparison correction, $q = 0.052$; Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}C), addressing the false-negative gap left by GapMind's conservative pathway-completeness rule, whereas the mechanism-free filter did not.
This suggests that concordant-models encode mechanistic information beyond what label consistency alone captures.
Concordant training plateaued with only around 50 training samples per phenotype (Supplementary Figures~\ref{fig:sample_size_histidine} and~\ref{fig:all_phenotype_heatmap}), beyond this point more samples did not aid in improving cross-dataset performance. 
Therefore, the practical question shifts from how many additional samples to acquire to which genomes are most informative to measure.

Deploying any of these specialists on a new genome whose concordance is unknown requires a per-prediction trust signal (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A,B)~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject}.
Selecting the least-confident genomes for additional labelling improved cross-dataset transfer more than random selection, with the largest gain for glucose, the lowest performer among the three phenotypes examined (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D).
Emerging high-throughput phenotyping methods~\cite{Nichols2011Landscape,Gralka2023-ft,Huang2023-lh,Kehe2019-vr} are positioned to scale this loop by targeting applicability-domain gaps such as phylogenetically under-sampled clades, denser condition coverage, and cross-source measurement of the low-confidence genomes selective prediction flags, rather than by densifying phenotypes that already saturate.
However, no label-free metric we tested correlated with per-phenotype cross-dataset accuracy, suggesting that phenotype difficulty reflects mechanistic learnability rather than the model's own uncertainty.

\subsection*{Limitations and Future Directions}

Concordance-based training requires mechanistic tools such as GapMind, available only for well-characterised pathways; the mechanism-free filter offers a partial alternative on balanced accuracy but not on recall, and still requires experimental labels from multiple sources.
Concordance filtering also extends the coverage--clarity tradeoff to a taxonomic dimension, favouring pathways in the annotation space and organisms with broadly conserved enzymes while excluding those with alternative enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulation~\cite{Soucy2015HGT,Glasner2020Promiscuity,Gorke2008CCR}, and may bias towards well-studied genera, though the design decouples feature space from the concordance rule (Supplementary Text~S5).
All analyses rely on computational annotations, which carry systematic biases across taxa~\cite{Karlsen2023Genotype,Schnoes2009Annotation,Lobb2020AnnotationCoverage} and may produce lower apparent concordance in poorly studied lineages because of annotation gaps rather than different biology.
Additionally, capturing mechanisms beyond curated rules will require external data such as transcriptomic or regulatory profiles~\cite{Arikan2023MultiOmics}, comparative genomics across closely related strains, and targeted experimental characterisation of out-of-domain organisms; the low-confidence ranking of Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction} provides a natural starting point for selecting candidates.

In summary, microbial carbon-utilisation prediction models generalised best when training labels agreed with mechanistic predictions and the feature space matched known pathway biology, systematically surpassing the curated baseline's recall by classifying discordant samples. This is consistent with our framing of label--mechanism agreement, operationalised through GapMind concordance, as a diagnosable and partially mitigable driver of shortcut learning alongside sample volume and feature-space choice.
Reliable phenotype prediction will therefore require curating training data with strong label--mechanism agreement and using applicability-domain signals to flag when models extend beyond curated rules.
Because label consistency captures only part of what concordance provides, extending the mechanism-free filter towards alternatives such as ensemble disagreement or prediction-confidence-based filtering~\cite{Northcutt2021Confident} could close the recall gap, while transcriptomic or regulatory data~\cite{Arikan2023MultiOmics} will be needed for context-dependence.
