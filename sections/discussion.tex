\section*{Discussion}

Machine learning models that predict microbial phenotypes from genomic features generalise poorly across datasets~\cite{Pasolli2016MetaAnalysis,Whalen2022Pitfalls}, a limitation typically attributed to limited sample size or feature-space artefacts~\cite{Li2023-up,Yu2025BiasedSampling,Leek2010BatchEffects}.
Across four binary growth datasets (819 bacterial strain records, 240 carbon sources), we identify label--mechanism agreement in the training set as a determinant of cross-dataset generalisation that complements the established roles of sample size and feature-space choice.
Models trained on concordant samples behaved as bounded specialists, and their prediction confidence helped prioritise which additional measurements most improved the lowest-performing phenotype.
These results reframe genome-based phenotype prediction as a problem of training-set curation and applicability-domain definition~\cite{Sambasivan2021DataCascades,Zha2025DataCentric}, alongside the data-volume and algorithm-choice axes.

\subsection*{Label--Mechanism Agreement Governs Cross-Dataset Transfer}

Poor cross-dataset performance was associated with mixed-quality training samples and non-transferable dataset-specific correlations; phylogeny alone explained little of the failure (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_performance}C), suggesting that the source of generalisation loss lies beyond clade structure.
Restricting training to samples where experimental labels agreed with mechanistic predictions (concordance filtering) improved cross-dataset recall and feature stability.
This shifts the diagnostic question from ``how many samples'' to ``which training subset'', indicating that data curation and applicability-domain definition can be as important as additional training samples~\cite{Sambasivan2021DataCascades,Zha2025DataCentric,Whang2023DataQuality}.

\subsection*{Concordance-Trained Models Are Bounded Specialists}

Concordance filtering implicitly defines an applicability domain, and the concordance-trained model's behaviour on discordant samples reveals what lies inside versus outside it.
The wide phenotype-level range in cross-dataset balanced accuracy is consistent with multiple coexisting causes of discordance~\cite{Galperin1998Sources,Bernstein2021Uncertainty}: false negatives (GapMind--/Experiment+) may reflect annotation gaps or unknown alternative pathways, whereas false positives (GapMind+/Experiment--) may reflect regulatory repression or experimental artefacts that static genomic features cannot capture.
Such experimental artefacts may include differences in growth-assay modality across the source datasets which need not reflect any genomic signal.
This asymmetry is genomically grounded: annotation-gap false negatives carry genomic content the broader KOFAM feature set can recover even when GapMind's pathway-completeness rule misses it, whereas false positives driven by regulation or experimental conditions leave a weaker one concordant training does not sharpen.
Because recovered false negatives are disproportionately metabolic generalists, the model recovers chiefly a generalist genomic signature correlated with growth rather than a substrate-specific mechanism: a broad transporter's presence does not establish that it serves the focal substrate, which is why GapMind excludes such genes.
This recovery rests on the same lifestyle-correlated signal that drives shortcut learning elsewhere, here aiding recall without transferable mechanism.
Because the rescue rate is non-zero, concordance filtering discards some discordant samples carrying transferable signal.

Li et al.\ suggested that datasets exceeding approximately 1000 genomes can enable out-of-clade prediction for some traits~\cite{Li2023-up}, whereas Yu et al.\ found that increasing sample size did not rescue generalisation under phylogenetically biased sampling~\cite{Yu2025BiasedSampling}; within our sample-size regime (up to 780 strains), our findings suggest that the heterogeneity of training samples, rather than their number alone, drives shortcut learning.
Adding over 8000 BacDive genomes~\cite{Schober2025BacDive} improved the unfiltered model but did not significantly exceed concordance filtering and reduced feature reproducibility (Supplementary Text~S11; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:bacdive_curation}).
Curated phenotype quality matters similarly for multi-trait models~\cite{Koblitz2025Phenotype}; our result adds that a phenotype-specific biological criterion improves cross-dataset feature transfer with the feature space and model architecture held fixed.
Under concordance filtering, models converged on the same predictors across datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}B; Table~\ref{tab:main_feature_comparison}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}): when training labels agree with the mechanistic prediction, a mechanism-blind learner converges on the same pathway genes a curator would select.
Inside the concordance-trained model's applicability domain, however, general KOFAM features did not reconstruct GapMind's hand-curated pathway rules from their own evidence, leaving a residual gap most pronounced on glucose.
The specialist's advantage over GapMind was nonetheless visible under random holdout and as the partial rescue of false negatives in the discordant subset, but averaged out across the unstratified cross-dataset test (Figure~\ref{fig:concordant_analysis}C,D), supporting its use where applicability-domain signals are available.

\subsection*{Practical Deployment and Confidence-Guided Experimentation}

Deploying the specialist raises two questions: whether a mechanism-free weighting strategy can substitute when GapMind is unavailable, and how much labelled training data the concordance-trained model requires; both must be answered on cross-dataset tests, since random holdout overestimates generalisation~\cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation}.
Mechanism-free weighting requires no pathway annotations and increased true-positive recovery over both baselines with little balanced-accuracy change from unfiltered ML, but lower specificity (Figure~\ref{fig:improvement_strategies}B,C)~\cite{Northcutt2021Confident,Bernhardt2022LabelCleaning}.
It is therefore recall-oriented rather than a balanced-accuracy substitute for concordance training.
This suggests that concordance-trained models encode mechanistic information beyond what label consistency alone captures.
Alternatively, restricting a full-data model to predictions agreeing with GapMind recovers much of the specialist's cross-dataset accuracy but requires GapMind for every genome (Supplementary Text~S13).
Turning to the second question, 50 concordant training samples for histidine reached at least 90\% of the all-available-sample performance, although requirements for other phenotypes remain unresolved (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sample_size_histidine}).
The practical question therefore includes both how many samples to acquire and which genomes are most informative to measure.

Deploying any of these specialists on a new genome whose concordance is unknown requires a per-prediction trust signal (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}A,B)~\cite{Weaver2008AppDomain,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification,Hendrickx2024Reject}; selecting the least-confident genomes for labelling then produced the largest observed mean transfer gain, concentrated in the weakest of the three phenotypes shown (Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}D).
Emerging high-throughput phenotyping methods~\cite{Nichols2011Landscape,Gralka2023-ft,Huang2023-lh,Kehe2019-vr} could scale this loop by targeting applicability-domain gaps, such as under-sampled clades and the low-confidence genomes selective prediction flags, rather than densifying already-saturated phenotypes.
However, at the phenotype level no label-free metric we tested correlated with per-phenotype cross-dataset accuracy, suggesting that phenotype difficulty reflects mechanistic learnability rather than the model's own uncertainty.

\subsection*{Limitations and Future Directions}

Concordance-based training requires mechanistic tools such as GapMind, available only for well-characterised pathways; mechanism-free weighting avoids that requirement but exchanges specificity for recall and still requires experimental labels from multiple sources.
Concordance filtering also extends the coverage--clarity tradeoff to a taxonomic dimension, favouring pathways in the annotation space and organisms with broadly conserved enzymes while excluding those with alternative enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulation~\cite{Soucy2015HGT,Glasner2020Promiscuity,Gorke2008CCR}, and may bias towards well-studied genera, though the design decouples feature space from the concordance rule (Supplementary Text~S5).
All analyses rely on computational annotations, which carry systematic biases across taxa~\cite{Karlsen2023Genotype,Schnoes2009Annotation,Lobb2020AnnotationCoverage} and may lower apparent concordance in poorly studied lineages through annotation gaps rather than different biology.
Capturing mechanisms beyond curated rules will require external data such as transcriptomic or regulatory profiles~\cite{Arikan2023MultiOmics}, comparative genomics, and targeted characterisation of out-of-domain organisms, for which the low-confidence ranking of Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction} provides a natural starting point.
Genome-scale metabolic models could in principle extend concordance filtering beyond GapMind's curated pathways to a much larger set of carbon substrates.
Such an extension would require curated or optimised reconstructions, however, since automatically constructed constraint-based models have been reported to predict carbon utilisation little better than chance for non-model organisms~\cite{Li2023-up}.

In summary, microbial carbon-utilisation prediction models generalised best when training labels agreed with mechanistic predictions and the feature space matched known pathway biology; concordance-trained models also partly rescued GapMind false negatives.
This is consistent with our framing of label--mechanism agreement, operationalised through GapMind concordance, as a diagnosable and partially mitigable driver of shortcut learning alongside sample volume and feature-space choice.
Reliable phenotype prediction will therefore require curating training data with strong label--mechanism agreement and using applicability-domain signals to flag when models extend beyond curated rules.
Because phylogenetic label consistency did not reproduce what concordance provides, extending mechanism-free weighting towards alternatives such as ensemble disagreement or prediction-confidence-based filtering~\cite{Northcutt2021Confident} could close the recall gap, while transcriptomic or regulatory data~\cite{Arikan2023MultiOmics} will be needed for context-dependence.
