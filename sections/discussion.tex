\section*{Discussion}

% Summary of findings
In our cross-dataset analyses, poor generalisation was not explained by the learning algorithm alone. Performance improved mainly when we restricted training to samples with coherent genotype--phenotype relationships.
In this dataset, GapMind--experiment discordance likely reflects at least two problems: incorrect or incomplete representation of known mechanisms, and cases in which the relevant biology is not captured by genome-derived features, such as regulation or environmental context.
When discordant samples dominate the training set, models can fit correlations that work within a dataset or clade but do not transfer.
For these carbon utilisation phenotypes, our results suggest that data curation is a larger constraint on transfer than further algorithmic tuning~\cite{Mey2021Improving}.

% What constitutes high-quality data
In this study, we treated samples as high quality when mechanistic predictions and measured growth agreed, because those samples were more likely to reflect pathways already represented in the annotation space.
Concordant samples tend to represent organisms that use broadly conserved, well-characterised metabolic pathways. Filtering to these samples therefore favours features that transfer across organisms rather than clade-specific correlates.
For any given carbon source, however, a proportion of organisms employ alternative enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulatory strategies \cite{Soucy2015HGT}; when such organisms are included in training, the model lacks mechanistic signal and instead relies on phylogenetic or dataset-specific correlates that do not generalise.
Data quality extends beyond mechanistic concordance to encompass genome completeness and annotation consistency \cite{Bowers2017MIMAG,Parks2015CheckM}, as well as measurement reproducibility and standardised phenotype calling.
Discordance therefore includes both recoverable cases, such as annotation gaps, and cases in which the relevant mechanism is not represented in the available data.

% Interpreting discordances
Concordant-trained models reached only 0.18--0.60 balanced accuracy on discordant samples, which argues against a single explanation for discordance~\cite{Galperin1998Sources,Snitkin2008Model}.
Although these interpretations remain speculative without experimental validation, false negatives (GapMind--/Experiment+) may reflect annotation gaps or unknown alternative pathways. False positives (GapMind+/Experiment--) may instead reflect regulatory repression or experimental artefacts that static genomic features cannot capture.
Dataset-specific discordance patterns may reflect differences in experimental protocols, measurement sensitivity, or the biological composition of the sampled communities. Disentangling these contributions would require controlled cross-laboratory comparisons, but the variation already points to concrete follow-up work, including protocol comparisons, phenotype-calling checks, and clade-specific error analysis.

% Relationship to previous work
Previous work has demonstrated that ML models learn phylogenetic signals, concluding that massive datasets ($>$1000 genomes) are needed to overcome confounding~\cite{Li2023-up}.
In our data, phylogenetic confounding was strongest when training included both concordant and discordant samples, and it was much weaker after concordance filtering.
This likely reflects the fact that concordant samples capture conserved pathways shared across phylogenetic lineages, allowing the models to learn features that transfer rather than clade-specific patterns~\cite{Smith2022Phylogenomic,Knyshov2024Predicting}.
Sample size and data quality therefore interact: adding samples helps only when those samples carry coherent mechanistic signal.
This aligns with observations that dataset composition and phylogenetic distance strongly affect model performance \cite{Martiny2013-ob,Martiny2015-ie}, but in our study, adding more mixed-quality samples was less helpful than selecting samples with consistent mechanistic signal.
The divergence between within-dataset accuracy and cross-dataset generalisation under combined feature representations is consistent with previous observations that biologically informed feature selection improves interpretability without sacrificing accuracy \cite{Li2023-up}. In our data, comprehensive feature sets worked best when test conditions matched training, whereas pathway-specific features worked better for cross-dataset transfer.
Limited feature overlap across datasets indicates that dataset-specific biases dominate when data quality is mixed~\cite{Chen2015Selecting}.
The results therefore depended on both what information we gave the model and which samples we trained it on. GapMind-based features helped most when the training set was restricted to concordant samples.

% Practical implications
These results suggest several practical choices for building phenotype predictors.
In this study, changing the training set and feature representation mattered more than changing the classifier \cite{Zha2025DataCentric,Whang2023DataQuality,Sambasivan2021DataCascades}.
ML models should be trained on high-quality samples with clear genotype--phenotype mappings when possible.
When mechanistic predictors, such as GapMind, are available, concordant samples provide the highest-quality training data; when they are unavailable, confidence-based filtering and removal of systematically problematic samples offer practical alternatives\cite{Zhang2020Combining}.
Feature stability across independent training sets can help distinguish mechanistic from spurious learning.
Cross-dataset and out-of-clade evaluation provides realistic performance estimates \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation}, as within-dataset cross-validation overestimates generalisation by 0.15--0.25 balanced accuracy.
However, when test conditions are expected to resemble training conditions, such as predictions within the same experimental system or phylogenetic context, full-dataset training maximises performance and may be the preferred approach.
Sample size requirements appear manageable: learning curves for histidine utilisation suggest that 50--100 high-quality samples per phenotype may suffice, although this requires validation for other phenotypes.
For experimental design, standardising growth conditions, measurement protocols, and phenotype-calling criteria is important if datasets are to be aggregated. Positive and negative controls, together with replicate measurements, would also help identify unreliable phenotype calls.
Emerging high-throughput phenotyping methods \cite{Bochner2001PM,Nichols2011Landscape,Gralka2023-ft,Huang2023-lh,Kehe2019-vr} will accelerate data collection; however, quality must be prioritised alongside quantity.

Our findings also highlight a broader limitation of genome-only prediction: models can learn only mechanisms that are represented in the annotations and in the training labels.
In this study, a curated mechanistic model enabled us to condition the learning problem by identifying samples governed by shared, well-encoded pathways.
However, many biologically important phenotypes depend on regulatory, temporal, or contextual processes not captured by genome sequence alone; extending our approach to such phenotypes will require the integration of additional data modalities---transcriptomics, proteomics, or functional genomics \cite{Arikan2023MultiOmics}---together with mechanistic assertions that support data stratification.
Without that alignment between question, data, and representation, larger datasets alone are unlikely to solve the problem.
% Limitations
Our framework has important constraints.
Our filtering approach requires mechanistic tools such as GapMind, which exist only for well-characterized metabolic pathways, and extending it to traits lacking comparable mechanistic predictors (e.g., stress tolerance and virulence) requires the development of alternative quality assessment approaches.
We restricted the analysis to binary growth/no-growth outcomes, and quantitative traits may require different filtering strategies.
Filtering to concordant samples reduces training data substantially ($\sim$30\% removed), which may be prohibitive for rare phenotypes, although our results suggest that smaller high-quality datasets can outperform larger noisy ones for generalisable model training.
By filtering to samples where mechanism-based predictions match experiments, we may be selecting for samples where the known pathway is the actual mechanism, potentially missing organisms using alternative routes; the partial predictability of discordant samples suggests this is a real limitation.
Moreover, concordance filtering may introduce a taxonomic bias toward well-studied genera, where annotation databases are most complete; therefore, the observed cross-dataset generalisation could partly reflect the broader representation of such taxa across datasets rather than purely mechanistic transferability.
Finally, all analyses rely on computational genome annotations, which have systematic biases across taxa \cite{Karlsen2023Genotype,Schnoes2009Annotation}, and poorly studied lineages may have lower concordance rates owing to annotation gaps rather than genuinely different biology.

% Future directions
Discordances point toward specific research priorities.
False negative discordances may indicate undiscovered alternative pathways, and systematic study of these cases could expand pathway databases. Clades with elevated discordance rates may also harbour novel metabolic strategies worth experimental follow-up.
Methods for identifying high-quality training samples without mechanistic knowledge---for example from ensemble disagreement, prediction confidence, or cross-validation stability \cite{Northcutt2021Confident}---would extend this approach to less-characterized phenotypes.
Integration of gene expression or regulatory information could capture context-dependence that static genomic features miss.
Testing whether concordance-based filtering generalises to other trait types, such as antibiotic resistance, environmental tolerance, or host interactions, would establish broader applicability.

% Conclusion
Overall, the models transferred best when training data were mechanistically coherent and the feature space matched known pathway biology. However, the scope of what they can capture is still bounded by the available genomic features.
Even well-trained models face an inherent ceiling for organisms that rely on poorly characterised metabolic strategies, the genes of which are underrepresented in annotation databases---a limitation that scale alone cannot overcome.
Progress in biological machine learning will therefore depend on infrastructure and experimental strategies that expose, evaluate, and condition on coherent genotype--phenotype relationships within appropriate representational spaces.
