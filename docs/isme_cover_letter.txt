Please consider our manuscript, "Data coherence over data volume drives generalisable genome-based prediction of microbial carbon utilisation," for publication as an Original Article in The ISME Journal.

The carbon sources a microbe can use shape its ecological niche, and because most taxa cannot be cultivated, these phenotypes are increasingly inferred from genomes. Machine-learning models that predict them accurately within one dataset often fail on another. This failure is usually attributed to small or taxonomically biased training sets, and larger, more diverse collections are proposed as the remedy. We tested a different explanation. Growth on a given substrate may proceed through a conserved catabolic pathway or an uncharacterised alternative route, and a genome may fail to grow despite carrying a complete pathway. Pooling independent collections therefore mixes mechanistically distinct genotype-phenotype relationships, and a model trained on that mixture may learn dataset-specific correlations rather than transferable mechanism. Gains from curated training data have typically been demonstrated within a single curated source; we tested this explanation across independent experimental collections.

We harmonised binary growth outcomes for 819 strains and 240 carbon sources from four independent collections and focused on the 15 carbon sources assayed in all four. Balanced accuracy fell from 0.86 within datasets to 0.62 across them, and restricting evaluation to relatives in the same clade recovered only 0.03 of that loss, so phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure. We then trained only on concordant samples, whose measured growth agrees with their annotated pathways, to obtain a mechanistically coherent training set. Concordance filtering doubled the number of phenotypes for which independent collections shared a stable predictive feature, from 6 to 12 of 15, whereas size- and class-matched random subsets remained at the unfiltered level. The shared features were often established pathway genes. For histidine, they included two urocanate-pathway enzymes and the histidine-utilisation repressor, consistent with the known co-regulation of the hut genes.

Data volume did not substitute for this coherence. Adding up to approximately 1800 BacDive genomes per phenotype to the full training set improved cross-dataset accuracy over full-data training but did not significantly outperform a concordant set roughly four-fold smaller, and it made feature selection less reproducible. Adding the same genomes to the concordant set gave a modest further gain, suggesting that coherence and volume are complementary rather than alternatives. Prior work found that datasets exceeding approximately 1000 genomes can enable out-of-clade prediction for some traits. Our collections fall below that size, and within this regime the mechanistic heterogeneity of the training samples, rather than their number alone, appears to drive the shortcut learning that limits transfer.

We report the limits of this approach alongside its gains. Concordance-trained models are bounded specialists: they favour genomes with conserved, well-annotated pathways and cover organisms with alternative enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulation less well. Across the full cross-dataset test set they did not outperform GapMind, the curated pathway-based predictor that defines concordance. Where GapMind missed growth, however, their growth probabilities separated growers from non-growers better than those of full-data models (AUC 0.64 versus 0.53). Prediction confidence can make these bounds explicit. For sucrose, abstaining on the least-confident half of genomes raised retained-subset accuracy from 0.75 to 0.88, although abstention added little for an already accurate model and could not rescue a model without transferable signal. In simulated follow-up experiments, labelling the least-confident genomes gave the largest mean gain in cross-dataset accuracy, mostly for the weakest-transferring phenotypes, although the advantage was not consistent across phenotypes. All four collections profile aerobic heterotrophic isolates, so applicability to anaerobes and metagenome-assembled genomes remains untested.

These results recast genome-based phenotype prediction as a problem of training-set curation and applicability-domain definition, alongside data volume and algorithm choice. The work builds on studies published in The ISME Journal on predicting microbial traits from phylogeny (Goberna and Verdú, 2016), on genomic traits that predict bacterial growth in culture and in soil (Li et al., 2019), on trait-based microbial strategies (Malik et al., 2020), and on inter-phylum horizontal gene transfer that has shaped bacterial metabolism (Caro-Quintero and Konstantinidis, 2015). Our study follows from this work and asks when a genome-based prediction can be trusted beyond the collection used to train it. The answer matters to microbial ecologists who assign metabolic function to uncultured taxa, to developers of genome-to-phenotype methods, and to groups planning high-throughput phenotyping, which can target low-confidence genomes and under-sampled clades rather than add measurements where performance has already saturated.
