Please consider our manuscript, "Data coherence over data volume drives generalisable genome-based prediction of microbial carbon utilisation," for publication as an Original Article in The ISME Journal.

Microbial nutrient utilisation phenotypes are key determinants of ecological niches and underpin community assembly, nutrient cycling, and biotechnological applications. Because cultivation cannot characterise most taxa, these phenotypes are increasingly inferred from genomes, yet machine-learning models achieving high within-dataset accuracy generalise poorly across datasets. That failure is commonly attributed to limited or taxonomically biased training data, motivating calls for larger and more diverse collections. The interpretation is well supported but incomplete. Growth on a given carbon source may proceed through a conserved catabolic pathway, through an uncharacterised alternative route, or not at all despite a complete pathway held silent by regulation, so an aggregated training set pools mechanistically distinct genotype-phenotype relationships. A model fitted to that pool learns dataset-specific correlations in place of transferable mechanism.

We integrated binary growth outcomes for 819 microbial strains across 242 carbon sources from four independent collections and analysed the 15 phenotypes shared by all four. Cross-dataset balanced accuracy declined even between close relatives, so phylogenetic distance alone does not account for the failure. Restricting training to concordant samples, those whose measured growth agrees with their annotated pathway, raised the mean cross-dataset balanced accuracy on the full held-out test from 0.64 to 0.68 and, more informatively, doubled the number of phenotypes for which independent collections recovered the same stable predictive features, from six of fifteen to twelve; size- and class-matched random subsets reproduced only the unfiltered level. Those identified shared features were predominantly established pathway genes: for example for histidine, the two core urocanate-pathway enzymes along with the hut repressor were shared, consistent with their long-recognised operonic co-regulation.

Data volume did not substitute for this coherence. Supplementing training with more than 8000 BacDive genomes gave no detectable gain over roughly 380 concordant samples (p = 0.20), while rendering feature selection markedly less reproducible. The pattern indicates what constrains generalisation: a classifier weights most heavily the mechanisms best represented in its training data, so additional strains contribute rare or idiosyncratic routes faster than they contribute support for any one of them. Concordance filtering acts in the opposite direction, concentrating support on recurrent mechanisms, which is why the same pathway genes reappear across collections. Prior work concluded that overcoming phylogenetic confounding requires more than 1000 genomes; our collections remain below that threshold, and our results suggest that the mechanistic heterogeneity of the training samples, rather than their number alone, drives the shortcut learning that limits transfer.

The concordance-trained models achieves better generalisation at the cost of mechanistic breadth, favouring organisms with conserved, well-annotated pathways over those with alternative enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulation. These models accordingly were comparable to a curated pathway-based predictor overall, acting as bounded specialists, but were able to recover growth missed by incomplete annotations (false negatives) about twice as often as they overturned annotated pathways yielding no growth (false positives); 44% versus 19%. These false negatives were predominantly metabolic generalists distinguished by broad-specificity transporters rather than substrate-specific enzymes.

These results reframe genome-based phenotype prediction as a problem of training-set curation and applicability-domain definition, alongside the established axes of data volume and algorithm choice, shifting the diagnostic question from how many samples to which training subset. Two consequences bear on the Journal's readership. Prediction confidence ranks reliable calls above unreliable ones, so abstaining on the least confident defines a practical applicability domain, the subset of genomes within which a model can be trusted: for histidine, withholding the least-confident half raised balanced accuracy from 0.76 to 0.84. Genome-derived trait calls, increasingly used to assign metabolic function to uncultured taxa from metagenome-assembled genomes, can therefore be reported within a stated domain rather than as uniformly reliable. The same signal also directs experimental effort: labelling the least-confident genomes improved cross-dataset transfer more than random or diversity-based selection, so emerging high-throughput phenotyping is better spent on applicability-domain gaps, under-sampled clades and low-confidence genomes. We present the negative and bounded results alongside the positive ones so that readers can judge where genome-based prediction is, and is not, currently reliable.
