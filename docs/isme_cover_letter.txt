Please consider our manuscript, "Data coherence over data volume drives generalisable genome-based prediction of microbial carbon utilisation", for publication as an Original Article in The ISME Journal.

The carbon sources a microorganism can use shape its ecological niche and its contribution to community dynamics. Because many taxa remain uncultivated, such traits are increasingly inferred from genomes. Recent work in The ISME Journal has used genome-derived traits to map bacterial diversity across tens of thousands of genomes (Hoffert et al., 2026), and as genome databases expand, the accuracy of such inferences needs to be validated (Ramoneda et al., 2024). Validation matters because traits do not always transfer between contexts: life-history strategies are difficult to generalise across soils and experimental conditions (Stone et al., 2023), and temperature may confound genomic signatures of growth optimisation (Weissman et al., 2026). Our manuscript asks whether genome-based predictions of carbon utilisation transfer across independent experimental collections. Models that predict growth on a substrate accurately within one dataset often fail on another, a failure commonly attributed to small or taxonomically biased training sets and usually addressed by assembling larger, more diverse collections. We test a different explanation for this failure.

Growth on a substrate may proceed through a conserved pathway or an uncharacterised alternative route, and an organism may fail to grow despite encoding a complete pathway. Pooling collections therefore mixes mechanistically distinct genotype-phenotype relationships, and models trained on this mixture may learn dataset-specific correlations rather than transferable mechanisms. To test this explanation, we harmonised growth outcomes for 819 strains and 240 carbon sources from four independent collections. For the 15 carbon sources assayed in all four, median balanced accuracy fell from 0.86 under random holdout to 0.62 across datasets. Restricting evaluation to relatives in the same clade changed balanced accuracy by only about 0.03, so phylogenetic distance alone does not explain the failure. We therefore trained models only on concordant samples, whose measured growth agrees with their annotated pathways. Concordance filtering doubled the number of phenotypes with reproducible genomic signals across datasets, from 6 to 12 of 15, whereas size- and class-matched random subsets remained at or below the unfiltered level. The shared signals often corresponded to established pathway genes; for histidine, they included two urocanate-pathway enzymes and the histidine-utilisation repressor.

Coherent training data were also more efficient than added volume. Models trained on the concordant set approached the cross-dataset performance of models augmented with up to approximately 1800 BacDive genomes per phenotype, using roughly four-fold fewer training samples. Adding those genomes to the concordant set gave a modest further gain, suggesting that coherence and volume are complementary.

The manuscript turns these findings into practical guidance. Where a curated pathway predictor such as GapMind exists, training samples can be curated for agreement between measured growth and its predictions, although such predictors cover only well-characterised pathways. Models trained this way are specialists for genomes with conserved, well-annotated pathways, so each model should be reported with its applicability domain. Prediction confidence, which requires no pathway predictor, helps define that domain: where a model transfers, abstaining on its least-confident predictions improves accuracy. Whether a phenotype transfers at all must still be tested empirically: cross-dataset learning curves from 50 to 100 concordant samples typically indicated whether a phenotype was learnable from genomic features, and further measurement is worth committing mainly where that early signal exists. In simulated follow-up experiments, labelling the least-confident genomes gave the largest mean gain, mostly for the weakest-transferring phenotypes.

These results recast generalisation as a problem of agreement between training labels and known mechanism, alongside data volume and feature-space choice, and they respond to calls to validate genome-based trait inferences (Ramoneda et al., 2024). The work therefore speaks to the journal's broad readership: microbial ecologists who assign metabolic functions to uncultured taxa, developers of genome-to-phenotype methods, and groups planning high-throughput phenotyping.
