Please consider our manuscript, "Data coherence over data volume drives generalisable genome-based prediction of microbial carbon utilisation," for publication as an Original Article in The ISME Journal.

Microbial nutrient utilisation phenotypes are key determinants of ecological niches and underpin community assembly, nutrient cycling, and biotechnological applications. Because cultivation cannot characterise most taxa, these phenotypes are increasingly inferred from genomes, yet machine-learning models achieving high within-dataset accuracy generalise poorly across datasets. That failure is commonly attributed to limited or taxonomically biased training data, motivating calls for larger and more diverse collections. The interpretation is well supported but incomplete. Growth on a given carbon source may proceed through a conserved catabolic pathway, through an uncharacterised alternative route, or not at all despite a complete pathway held silent by regulation, so an aggregated training set pools mechanistically distinct genotype-phenotype relationships. A model fitted to that pool learns dataset-specific correlations in place of transferable mechanism.

We integrated binary growth outcomes for 819 microbial strains across 240 carbon sources from four independent collections and analysed the 15 phenotypes shared by all four. Cross-dataset balanced accuracy declined even between close relatives, so phylogenetic distance alone does not account for the failure. Restricting training to concordant samples, those whose measured growth agrees with their annotated pathway, raised the mean cross-dataset balanced accuracy on the full held-out test from 0.64 to 0.68 and, more informatively, doubled the number of phenotypes for which independent collections recovered the same stable predictive features, from six of fifteen to twelve; size- and class-matched random subsets reproduced only the unfiltered level. Those identified shared features were predominantly established pathway genes: for example for histidine, the two core urocanate-pathway enzymes along with the hut repressor were shared, consistent with their long-recognised operonic co-regulation.

Data volume did not substitute for this coherence. Adding up to approximately 1800 BacDive genomes per phenotype to full-data training improved balanced accuracy over full-data training alone (0.725 versus 0.677, p < 0.001) but did not significantly outperform the much smaller concordant set (0.725 versus 0.706, p = 0.20), and feature selection became less reproducible as volume increased. The pattern indicates what constrains generalisation: a classifier weights most heavily the mechanisms best represented in its training data, so additional strains contribute rare or idiosyncratic routes faster than they contribute support for any one of them. Concordance filtering acts in the opposite direction, concentrating support on recurrent mechanisms, which is why the same pathway genes reappear across collections. Prior work concluded that overcoming phylogenetic confounding requires more than 1000 genomes; our collections remain below that threshold, and our results suggest that the mechanistic heterogeneity of the training samples, rather than their number alone, drives the shortcut learning that limits transfer.

The concordance-trained models achieve better generalisation at the cost of mechanistic breadth, favouring organisms with conserved, well-annotated pathways over those with alternative enzymes, divergent pathway variants, or complex regulation. Accordingly, they were comparable overall to a curated pathway-based predictor, acting as bounded specialists, but their growth probabilities added ranking information where the pathway rule missed growth (AUC 0.64 versus 0.53 for full-data training). The higher-ranked genomes were predominantly metabolic generalists carrying broad-specificity transporters and sugar-catabolism regulators.

These results reframe genome-based phenotype prediction as a problem of training-set curation and applicability-domain definition, alongside the established axes of data volume and algorithm choice, shifting the diagnostic question from how many samples to which training subset. Two consequences bear on the Journal's readership. Prediction confidence ranks reliable calls above unreliable ones, so abstaining on the least confident defines a practical applicability domain, the subset of genomes within which a model can be trusted: for sucrose, withholding the least-confident half raised retained-subset accuracy from 0.75 to 0.88, whereas abstention added little for an already strong model and could not rescue a model without transferable signal. Genome-derived trait calls, increasingly used to assign metabolic function to uncultured taxa from metagenome-assembled genomes, can therefore be reported within a stated domain rather than as uniformly reliable. The same signal also directs experimental effort: labelling the least-confident genomes produced the largest mean gain in cross-dataset transfer (0.09 versus 0.07 for random selection), although the advantage was not consistent across phenotype means. Emerging high-throughput phenotyping can therefore target applicability-domain gaps, under-sampled clades, and low-confidence genomes. We present the negative and bounded results alongside the positive ones so that readers can judge where genome-based prediction is, and is not, currently reliable.
